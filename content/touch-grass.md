Grass (*Poaceae*) is a monocotyledonous flowering plant of near-global distribution. It is frequently recommended. This guide is intended for the reader who has received such a recommendation and wishes to comply thoroughly rather than approximately.

The author has compiled this guide from secondary sources over eleven months.[^eleven]

---

## Field marks

The grasses are distinguished from the sedges and the rushes by a rule that is, mercifully, both ancient and rhymed:

> Sedges have edges,
> rushes are round,
> grasses are hollow
> right up from the ground.

Commit this to memory before departure. It is the only portion of this guide the author can personally guarantee, having tested it against four photographs.

**Diagnostic characters:**

- Stems (*culms*) hollow and cylindrical, interrupted at intervals by solid **nodes**
- Leaves two-ranked, alternating along the culm
- Leaf **sheath** split, wrapping the culm
- A **ligule** at the sheath–blade junction: membranous, hairy, or absent

The ligule is the single most reliable character in the family and it is roughly the size of a fingernail clipping. This is the point at which most beginners return indoors.

## Key to common turf-forming grasses

Work through the couplets in order. At each, choose the statement that matches your specimen and proceed to the numbered couplet indicated.

**1a.** Ligule a distinct membrane, easily seen → **2**
**1b.** Ligule a fringe of hairs, or absent → **4**

**2a.** Leaf tip abruptly hooded, boat-shaped; blade with two faint parallel lines flanking the midrib → ***Poa*** (meadow-grasses). The boat-tip is decisive. Press the tip between thumb and forefinger; it will fold rather than crease.
**2b.** Leaf tip tapering to a point → **3**

**3a.** Blade glossy on the underside, folded in bud; leaf-base often reddish → ***Lolium*** (rye-grasses). The gloss is genuinely striking and is the one moment in this activity that rewards the eye.
**3b.** Blade dull on both surfaces, rolled in bud → ***Agrostis*** (bents)

**4a.** Blade tightly inrolled, wiry, near-circular in cross-section; forms dense low tufts → ***Festuca*** (fescues)
**4b.** Blade flat, broad, coarse; plant robust → **5**

**5a.** Nodes conspicuously swollen and dark → ***Dactylis*** (cock's-foot)
**5b.** Nodes not conspicuous; you have been outside for forty minutes; it is beginning to rain → **cease**

## Habitat

Grass occurs on verges, lawns, playing fields, central reservations, the strip between the pavement and the road, and — in a form the literature describes as *unimproved* — in places requiring a train.

For the purposes of the original recommendation, the strip between the pavement and the road is sufficient. It is *Poa annua* and it is available at all hours.

## Seasonal notes

| Season | Condition | Advisability |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Growing, soft, ligules clear | Optimal |
| Summer | Dry, seeding, full of other people | Acceptable |
| Autumn | Wet, cold, structurally intact | Marginal |
| Winter | Frozen or absent | The recommendation may be deferred |

## Hazards

- **Moisture.** Grass is wet at nearly all times, including when it does not appear to be. This is the fact most consistently omitted by those issuing the recommendation.
- **Occupancy.** Dog faeces, glass, ants, and other people are all found in grass, sometimes in combination.
- **Duration.** No source consulted specifies how long contact must be maintained. The author has been unable to resolve this and treats it as an open question.

## Equipment

None. This is the difficulty.

There is no hand lens to purchase first, no key to laminate, no boot to break in — or rather, all of these exist and can be bought, and the author has bought several, and none of them are required, and the grass has been there the entire time.

---

The recommendation, restated in the terms of this guide: **locate a specimen of *Poa annua*, extend the hand, and make contact with the blade.** Identification is optional. The ligule does not need to be examined. It is fifteen steps from the door.
