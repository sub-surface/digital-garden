# ⁛ Zero

<div className="dropcap">

Visual metaphors for knowledge, I find[^1], are everywhere.  

</div>

[^1]: and after this article, you will too.

> I *see* what you mean, that *looks* right to me, I *get* it, I *grasp*[^hands] the concept, I *perceive* the truth, I *recognise* the object, I *envision* the future, I *imagine* the possibilities, I *picture* the scene, I *visualise* the solution, I *observe* the phenomenon, I *notice* the details, I *spot* the error, I *detect* the pattern, I *discern* the meaning, I *comprehend* the argument, I *apprehend* the situation. 

[^hands]: Not all of these are seeing. *Grasp*, *get*, *comprehend*, *apprehend* (from *prehendere*, to seize) are the hand's words, not the eye's. Hold on to that. We'll come back to the hands.

or, in latin:

> *aspicere*, *visum*, *spectare*, *videre*, *intueri*, *conspicere*, *perspicere*, *observare*, *considerare*, *cognoscere*, *intellegere*, *percipere*, *comprehendere*, *apprehendere*.

Visual metaphors are so ubiquitous, that they are often overlooked, yet they are so powerful that they shape our thoughts about the world down to the molecular level, infiltrating each one of us with privation over sensation, calling forth more sight to explicate the world, more images to make sense of it.  

As a photographer often working in the documentary mode, I reluctantly endorse the visual; to place the image at the seat of understanding, as supreme metaphor, I think, is an understandable wager! For who wouldn't be drawn to the idea that images could convey the presence or truth of some particular object ([[Roland Barthes|Barthes']] *ça-a-été* [that has been], from [[Books/Camera Lucida|Camera Lucida]])?  

The problem comes, as many do, in the failure to recognise the *radical autonomy* of things. I find comfort in colleagues of mine working on what's known as the "Speculative school of Documentary"[^2], a fascinating and nuanced turn where the *image-as-testimony* morphs and contorts (kicking and screaming) into the *image-as-mythology*. Fortunately for us lovers of myths, this turn presents a wonderful opportunity to inherit everything that came before, its catastrophes and eschatologies included: the breakdown of narrative and representation as such are all at stake in this case - for we cannot take the death of the image lightly, though *it has already died before*... 

[^2]: You can find their call to action [here](https://schoolofspeculativedocumentary.org) and from Max Pinckers' PhD thesis, "[Speculative Documentary Photography](https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/8706389)" Published 2021. I wrote about one of the movement's touchstones, Hito Steyerl, in [[The-Uncertainty-Principle|The Uncertainty Principle]].

So before anything else, here is the question this essay keeps asking, and keeps answering differently each time it comes round. It's the question that led me into philosophy in the first place, a long walk down the arcades looking in every window for someone who had asked it before me:

<blockquote className="pullquote">What is an image?</blockquote>

The Greeks had already folded an answer into their grammar. *Oida*, "I know", is the perfect tense of the verb "to see": to know is *to have seen*.[^oida] Which is a fine answer, if you already know what seeing is.

[^oida]: The same root (*\*weid-*) gives Greek *eidos* and *idea*, Latin *videre*, Sanskrit *veda*, and English *wit*, *wise* and *witness*. *Theōria* is spectating (a *theōros* was the official sent to watch the festival), and *evidence* is what is seen out of (*e-videre*). The law's word for proof is a visual metaphor.

# ⁖ One

## The History of The Image

### i. Sun

The light of the sun impinges on us, it arrives "fully formed", radiant and perfect. Though, as those who enjoy eclipses know, it is much beyond our physical capacity to apprehend. Our biological faculties, exhausted by the impedance of those photons, buckle and fizz in near-unison when we try. Despite this, the desire to have the sun's unblemished image remains with us beyond all our failed attempts, why?  

![D&G](Media/Deleuze_1.jpg)  

> *As goodness stands in the intelligible realm to intelligence and the things we know, so the sun stands in the visible realm to sight and the things we see.*  
—Plato, *The Republic*

[[Wiki/Philosophers/Plato|Plato]] goes further than the analogy. Of all the organs of sense, Socrates says, the eye is "the most like the sun" (*hēlioeidestaton*, sun-formed). The eye is a little sun, which is why it can see the big one. And yet the big one is the thing it cannot look at. That is the first lesson every eclipse-watcher learns, and the first time this essay will notice that the eye can be *wounded* by what it wants to see.

[Georges Bataille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Bataille), who looked harder than most, split the sun in two:

> The sun, from the human point of view (in other words, as it is confused with the notion of noon) is the most elevated conception. It is also the most abstract object, since it is impossible to look at it fixedly at that time of day. [...] If on the other hand one obstinately focuses on it, a certain madness is implied, and the notion changes meaning because it is no longer production that appears in light, but refuse or combustion.  
—Georges Bataille, *Rotten Sun* (1930)

There is the sun you don't look at, Plato's sun, all "mathematical serenity and spiritual elevation" as Bataille has it, and there is the sun you do look at, which burns. Philosophy has mostly preferred the first. It keeps its eyes on the sun by never quite looking at it.

### ii. Puddle

Socrates admits as much. In the [*Phaedo*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1658), a few hours before his death, he tells his friends how he gave up studying nature directly:

> I thought that as I had failed in the contemplation of true existence, I ought to be careful that I did not lose the eye of my soul; as people may injure their bodily eye by observing and gazing on the sun during an eclipse, unless they take the precaution of only looking at the image reflected in the water, or in some similar medium. So in my own case, I was afraid that my soul might be blinded altogether if I looked at things with my eyes or tried to apprehend them by the help of the senses. And I thought that I had better have recourse to the world of mind and seek there the truth of existence. I dare say that the simile is not perfect—for I am very far from admitting that he who contemplates existences through the medium of thought, sees them only 'through a glass darkly'...  
—Plato, *Phaedo* 99d–e (trans. Jowett)

Look at what has happened here. The founding turn of philosophy, away from the senses and towards the *logoi*, the world of accounts and arguments, is made *by way of an image*: the sun in a puddle. Socrates turns to thought the way an eclipse-watcher turns to the water, to save his eye. He even admits the simile isn't perfect.[^jowett] The retreat from images begins with an image, and a protective one.

[^jowett]: Plato's Greek speaks only of examining things "in images" (*en eikosi*). "Through a glass darkly" is Jowett's, borrowed from St Paul (1 Corinthians 13:12), so a Victorian translator has slipped the Christian mirror into Plato's puddle. Scholars still argue about whether Socrates' "second sailing" leaves images behind or only changes the medium (see Rafael Ferber, "Second sailing towards immortality and God", *Mnemosyne*, 2020), which is this essay's question in miniature.

Plato doesn't keep to it, either. In the *Republic* the freed prisoner, climbing out of the cave, will "last of all be able to see the sun, and not mere reflections of him in the water, but he will see him in his own proper place". So Plato disagrees with himself about the puddle: one dialogue settles for the reflection forever, the other promises that one day we'll stare straight at the source.

![Jan Saenredam after Cornelis van Haarlem, Plato's Cave, 1604](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/Jan_Pietersz_Saenredam_after_Cornelis_Cornelisz_van_Haarlem%2C_Plato%27s_Cave%2C_1604%2C_NGA_62542.jpg/1280px-Jan_Pietersz_Saenredam_after_Cornelis_Cornelisz_van_Haarlem%2C_Plato%27s_Cave%2C_1604%2C_NGA_62542.jpg)
> *Jan Saenredam after Cornelis van Haarlem,* Plato's Cave *(1604). National Gallery of Art, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jan_Pietersz_Saenredam_after_Cornelis_Cornelisz_van_Haarlem,_Plato%27s_Cave,_1604,_NGA_62542.jpg) (CC0). The fire behind, the shadows ahead, the puppeteers' wall between.*

It took [[Wiki/Philosophers/Baruch Spinoza|Spinoza]] to say that the reflection was never the problem:

> For example, when we look at the sun, we imagine that it is about 200 feet away from us. We are mistaken in this, so long as we are ignorant of the true distance. But once its distance is known, the error is certainly taken away but not the imagination [...]. Thus when the rays of the sun fall upon the surface of water and are reflected into our eyes, we imagine it exactly as if it were in water, even though we know its true location. Similarly with all the other imaginations by which the mind is misled [...] they are not contrary to the true and do not disappear in its presence.  
—Spinoza, [[Wiki/Texts/Ethics (Spinoza)|*Ethics*]] IVp1s (trans. Silverthorne & Kisner)

The sun stays in the water even after you know better. Knowing the truth doesn't drain the puddle. The image isn't an error waiting to be corrected; it's a real thing, a real effect of real light on a real body, and it goes on being one.

The photograph at the top of this essay is a puddle in a South Wales valley, made in homage to [Escher's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._C._Escher) woodcut of the same name. The leaves floating on the surface are soft; the branches reflected beneath them, upside down, are sharp. The focus went *through* the surface onto the reflection, which is to say the camera seems to accord with Spinoza: what's in the water is as real as what's on it. Making it was, like most images, a decision and an encounter, a selection and a dictation. The light did half the work.

<blockquote className="pullquote">An image is what the sun does to water.</blockquote>

### iii. Point

![Gemma Frisius observing the solar eclipse of 24 January 1544 through a camera obscura](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/57/1545_gemma_frisius_-_camera_obscura_-_121704_1_1.png)
> *Gemma Frisius watches the eclipse of 24 January 1544 at Louvain through a pinhole, from* De Radio Astronomica et Geometrica *(1545), the first published picture of a camera obscura (public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1545_gemma_frisius_-_camera_obscura_-_121704_1_1.png)). The only safe image of the eclipsed sun is an inverted one.*

Around the time of Plato, the later [Mohists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohism), followers of the Chinese philosopher Mozi, described the mechanism behind the formation of images by pinhole:  

<blockquote className="pullquote"> The image is inverted; at the crossing there is a point, and the image extends in length. The explanation lies in the point.  </blockquote>

At the risk of taking this too literally, let's consider this description applied to the production of images *de facto*. We have an inverted image, an extension, a point, and an explanation. Note that "inverted" here pre-supposes a primary image produced by nature, which the pinhole acts on to produce the inversion. I'd like you, dear reader, to hold this mechanistic image in your mind as we go further - the process is mirroring the canonical theory of knowledge in an honest way: our representation here is not wholly faithful, and the Mohists hold no pretence that it is - the explanation lies in the point.[^pixel]

[^pixel]: Two and a half thousand years later, Hito Steyerl's *How Not To Be Seen* (2013) offers the other half of the lesson: "To become invisible one has to become smaller or equal to one pixel." The image is made through a point, and escaped below one.

For a photographer the point is not an abstraction. It's the aperture, and it's the focus: where you put the point decides what the image explains, as the puddle above shows. The Mohists already knew that an image is made somewhere, by something passing through something else, and that the making leaves its mark (the inversion) on what is made.

### iv. Ox

The point became a hole in a wall, and the wall became an eye. [Alhazen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham) built his dark rooms in eleventh-century Cairo; Kepler, in 1604, called the image on the back of the eye a *pictura*, a painting; and in 1637 [[Wiki/Philosophers/René Descartes|Descartes]] went to the butcher. In the fifth discourse of his [*Optics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Dioptrique) he describes taking the eye of a freshly dead ox, scraping the back of it thin, and setting it into a hole in a window shutter. On the scraped membrane, there it is: the world outside, small, sharp and upside down.[^ox] The first photograph in philosophy, developed in a dead animal's eye.

![Descartes' figure of images formed on the back of the eye, observed by a man below](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fc/Dioptrique_images_sur_fond_oeil.jpg)
> *Descartes' figure from* La Dioptrique *(1637; here from Cousin's 1824 edition, public domain, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dioptrique_images_sur_fond_oeil.jpg)). The world arrives inverted on the back of the eye, and underneath, a bearded man peers up at it. Someone has to look at the picture in the head.*

[^ox]: René Descartes, *Optics* (*La Dioptrique*), Discourse V (1637). Bataille, characteristically, found the sacrifice in it before the science: in *Rotten Sun* "the bull himself is also an image of the sun, but only with his throat slit." Mithras slays a bull and Descartes cuts out an ox's eye, and in both cases the image of the sun costs an animal.

So when [[Gilles Deleuze|Deleuze]] reaches for an image of how dialectical thinking distorts difference, he reaches for Descartes' ox. Here is the passage, which [[Attention & Difference|I have used before]], at full length this time, because the lines before the famous simile matter as much as the simile:

> Stereoscopic images form no more than an even and flat opposition, but they depend upon something quite different: an arrangement of coexistent, tiered, mobile planes, a 'disparateness' within an original depth. Everywhere, the depth of difference is primary. [...] Space and time display oppositions (and limitations) only on the surface, but they presuppose in their real depth far more voluminous, affirmed and distributed differences which cannot be reduced to the banality of the negative. It is as though we were in Lewis Carroll's mirror where everything is contrary and inverted on the surface, but 'different' in depth. [...] There is a false profundity in conflict, but underneath conflict, the space of the play of differences. The negative is the image of difference, but a flattened and inverted image, like the candle in the eye of the ox - the eye of the dialectician dreaming of a futile combat?  
—Gilles Deleuze, [[Difference & Repetition|*Difference and Repetition*]], p. 51

Attentive readers will note the issue. Deleuze runs three optical machines in four sentences: the [stereoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereoscopy), [the looking-glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Through_the_Looking-Glass), the ox's eye. In each, a flat opposition on the surface (left against right, mirror against world, upright against inverted) is produced by a difference in depth that the surface can't show. The stereoscope makes depth out of the *disparity* between two flat pictures, not out of their likeness.

![Two panels: a stereoscope making depth from the disparity of two flat pictures, and a candle inverted on the back of an ox's eye](Media/Writing/image-inversion-as-trace.svg)

That is the issue with every inversion so far. The Mohists' upside-down image, the shadows on the cave wall, the scraped retina of Descartes' ox, Hegel's negative: each reads a flat opposition where something deeper is going on. Both the dualist and the dialectician treat the inversion as a *loss*, a copy that has gone wrong, the original turned on its head. Neither sees the relation actually occurring in the formation of the image, which is that something happened at the point. The inversion isn't a failure of the image; it's the trace of its making.

<blockquote className="pullquote">An image is a difference, seen flat.</blockquote>

St Paul had a word for the whole predicament. ["For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face."](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+13%3A12&version=KJV) His Greek says we see *di' esoptrou*, "through a mirror", which is impossible: you can see *in* a mirror, never *through* one. The glass was always both, a window and a mirror, and the translations have never settled which.[^glass]

[^glass]: Wycliffe (c. 1382) has "by a mirror in darkness"; Darby turns it into "a dim window"; one modern version has us "looking in a looking-glass", which puts Carroll in the Bible; another says the image is "reflected to us in a mysterious word", so the mirror becomes language. Paul's mirror is a *speculum*, and *speculation*, etymologically, is knowing in a mirror, in riddles (*in aenigmate*).

# ⁖ Two

## What Is an Image?

In April 2019 the [Event Horizon Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_Horizon_Telescope) collaboration [published a picture of the black hole at the centre of the galaxy M87](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/): a smudged orange ring around a dark centre. No one has ever seen it and no one ever will; the object emits no light. The picture was computed from data gathered by radio telescopes spread across the planet, and what it shows is light bent around something that can't be seen. It is, by any ordinary measure, an image. It looks like nothing that could be looked at. Its likeness is to nothing, and its causal story (photons, dishes, atomic clocks, algorithms) is complete.

![The first image of a black hole, M87*, from the Event Horizon Telescope, 2019](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Black_hole_-_Messier_87_crop_max_res.jpg/1280px-Black_hole_-_Messier_87_crop_max_res.jpg)
> *M87\*, Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration (2019), [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/) via ESO and [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Black_hole_-_Messier_87_crop_max_res.jpg). Light bent around something that will never be seen.*

So, *what is an image?*

[W. J. T. Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._J._T._Mitchell), whose essay of that title I read years before I knew what I was looking for in it, begins by admitting the problem:

> We speak of pictures, statues, optical illusions, maps, diagrams, dreams, hallucinations, spectacles, projections, poems, patterns, memories, and even ideas as images, and the sheer diversity of this list would seem to make any systematic, unified understanding impossible. [...] It might be better to begin by thinking of images as a far-flung family which has migrated in time and space and undergone profound mutations in the process.  
—W. J. T. Mitchell, "What Is an Image?" (*Iconology*, 1986)

Mitchell's answer is to draw a family tree: graphic images, optical images, perceptual, mental and verbal, branching from a common trunk of likeness. He's right that images are a family. But it's telling that the best-known attempt to say what an image is had to be *drawn*, and drawn as a tree, with likeness for a trunk.[^tree] Mitchell also notices where the old model leads: "If we begin talking as if the mind is a tabula rasa or a camera obscura, it won't be long before the blank page and the camera begin to have minds of their own." I think that's less a reductio than a clue.

[^tree]: Deleuze and Guattari would call this an arborescent answer. "The rhizome in opposition to the tree" is how Deleuze, looking back, described what his chapter on the image of thought led to.

My own answer has taken a while, and I want to give it plainly before testing it. **Images are what they are, when they are.** They are not copies first and things second. An image is an event: a cut in a flow of light that leaves a trace of a real occurrence. Whether that trace *resembles* anything is a further question, something an image may or may not go on to do. The M87 ring resembles nothing and is no less an image for it. Likeness is one of the things images do, not what they are.

![Diagram: a flow of light passes through a point and is kept as an inverted trace, which can be taken up as likeness or as relation](Media/Writing/image-the-cut.svg)

Deleuze and Guattari offer the minimal definition I need, and they anticipate the obvious objection to it:

> In what respect are desiring-machines really machines, in anything more than a metaphorical sense? A machine may be defined as a system of interruptions or breaks (*coupures*). These breaks should in no way be considered as a separation from reality [...]. Every machine, in the first place, is related to a continual material flow (*hylè*) that it cuts into.  
—Deleuze & Guattari, *Anti-Oedipus*

A camera is a machine in exactly this sense: it interrupts a flow of light and keeps the interruption. So is an eye. So, for that matter, is a puddle. The break is not "a separation from reality", the copy's distance from its original; it's a cut *in* reality, and the image is what the cut keeps.

None of this is new, which is the strange part. Spinoza had a non-mimetic theory of images in 1677, and almost nobody took it up:

> [...] the term we will use for affections of the human body whose ideas represent external bodies as present to us is images of things, *even though they do not reproduce the shapes of the things*. [...] I want you to realize that imaginations of the mind, considered in themselves, contain no error [...]. For if the mind, as it imagines non-existent things as being present to itself, also knew at the same time that those things do not in truth exist, it would certainly value this power of imagining as a virtue not as a fault of its nature.  
—Spinoza, [[Wiki/Texts/Ethics (Spinoza)|*Ethics*]] IIp17s (trans. Silverthorne & Kisner; emphasis mine)

Read that again with a photograph in mind. Images are affections of a body, the marks of its having been touched, and they need not reproduce the shapes of anything. Spinoza's example, just before this, is Paul, who goes on regarding Peter as present to him even after Peter has died, because the image belongs to the body that received it. That is Barthes in front of the Winter Garden photograph, three centuries early.[^barthes] And the power of imagining, once its causes are known, is "a virtue not a fault".

[^barthes]: I've written about Barthes, the punctum and his mother's photograph in [[Attention & Difference]]. Barthes has a lovely Latin gloss of his own: a photograph would be *imago lucis opera expressa*, an image "expressed (like the juice of a lemon) by the action of light". *Expression*, not representation, which is Spinoza's word too.

The analytic tradition arrived at the causal half of this from another direction and then split over it. Roger Scruton argued that the photograph's relation to its subject is "causal and not intentional", and concluded that photographs therefore aren't really representations and have no interest of their own.[^scruton] Kendall Walton took the same causal relation and concluded the opposite: "Photographs are transparent. We see the world through them," literally, the way we see through a telescope. Both agree the photograph is a causal trace. Both assume that thought lives somewhere else, in intention (Scruton) or in seeing (Walton). The third option, the one I want, is that the causal relation is where the image does its thinking. The trace isn't beneath meaning; it's how meaning gets in.

[^scruton]: Roger Scruton, "Photography and Representation", *Critical Inquiry* 7 (1981); Kendall Walton, "Transparent Pictures", *Critical Inquiry* 11 (1984). Walton insists his claim isn't a figure of speech: "I must warn against watering down this suggestion". So does Deleuze about machines. Two traditions refuse to let their optical claims be read as metaphors, which will matter when we get to the objection that all this is "only a figure of speech".

![A diagram of Spinoza's attribute of extension drawn over a long exposure of a river at a bridge pier](Media/Spinoza_1.jpg)
> *Attribute of extension, drawn over the river Taff at a bridge pier. 

This is also why I've ended up making images out of diagrams. A diagram is an image too, but one that maps relations rather than appearances, and it makes no claim to the photograph's indexicality. Laying the two together (a philosopher's diagram over a photograph of the thing it might be describing) separates the two jobs that "representation" has always run together: likeness and relation, the mirror and the map.[^diagrams]

![Diagram: mirror (likeness), map (relation) and the composite that lays one over the other](Media/Writing/image-mirror-and-map.svg)

[^diagrams]: More of these, and more on diagrams as their own kind of thinking, in [[On Diagrams]]. The recent book I'd point to is David Burrows, John Cussans, Dean Kenning and Mary Yacoob, *Drawing Analogies: Diagrams in Art, Theory and Practice* (2025), which builds on Peirce's definition of the diagram as "an analogy of relations".

What, then, has philosophy been doing with images all this time? Not getting them wrong, exactly. Sellars called it [the Myth of the Given](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_the_given): something taken to justify on its own, without being asked how. The image has been philosophy's given. Not false, just never questioned, used so fluently as a figure for knowing that nobody stopped to ask what it was. Deleuze has a better word for what that produces than error. He calls it stupidity, and he says the use of philosophy is to harm it. What forces thought, he says, is not a recognition but an encounter, something that "can only be sensed": "It is not the given but that by which the given is given."

<blockquote className="pullquote">Images are what they are, when they are.</blockquote>

# ⁖ Three

## The Thought of Image

> I am afraid that there are more people than I can imagine who can go no further than appreciating a picture that is a rectangle with an object in the middle of it, which they can identify. They don't care what is around the object as long as nothing interferes with the object itself, right in the centre. [...] They want something obvious. [...] I am at war with the obvious.
—[William Eggleston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Eggleston), in conversation with Mark Holborn, 1988

A photographer's complaint, and a precise one: a picture valued for an object in the middle *which they can identify*. That is recognition, and it is the target of what follows.

The clearest description of what has gone wrong here belongs, unsurprisingly, to Deleuze. In [[Difference & Repetition]], he argues that philosophy has, almost without noticing, modelled thinking on an act of recognition: a subject (light) encounters an object (camera),[^inversion] matches it against a concept already possessed (becoming-inverted), and calls this matching "knowledge." Deleuze names this the dogmatic image of thought (Deleuze, 1994, ch. 3) — and the word image is not incidental. Recognition presupposes exactly the visual scene the Mohists describe: a clear object, positively identified, held steady before an inner eye. The metaphor of vision attempts to illustrate an independently arrived-at theory of knowledge, while supplying the explanation of this view with its own album of images to draw from.

[^inversion]: Yes, light is the subject here, and the camera the object. That's deliberate. [Bergson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_and_Memory): "we imagine perception to be a kind of photographic view of things, taken from a fixed point by that special apparatus which is called an organ of perception [...] But is it not obvious that the photograph, if photograph there be, is already taken, already developed in the very heart of things and at all the points of space?" (*Matter and Memory*, ch. 1). Light is what acts; the camera, or the eye, is what it happens to.

> A new image of thought — or rather, a liberation of thought from those images which imprison it: this is what I had already sought to discover in Proust. Here, however, in Difference and Repetition, this search is autonomous and it becomes the condition for the discovery of these two concepts. [...] the rhizome in opposition to the tree, a rhizome-thought instead of an arborescent thought.  
*—Gilles Deleuze, Preface to Difference & Repetition*

The two concepts are difference and repetition, and the images that imprison thought are the ones Deleuze lists just before this: that thought has a good nature and naturally wants the true, that recognition is its model, that error is its only enemy, and that truth comes as answers to questions already posed. "Everybody knows" what it means to think, and that's precisely the trouble.

Spinoza offers a resource for thinking past this without simply inverting it into another representationalism. In the Ethics, *mind* and *body* are *not* two substances that must somehow resemble or correspond to one another; they are [[Wiki/Concepts/Substance Monism|*one substance*]] expressed under two attributes, such that "the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things" (Spinoza, Ethics, IIp7, trans. Curley, 1996). 

![Spinoza's ontology, substance, attributes and modes, drawn over a suspension bridge pylon](Media/Spinoza_3.jpg)

Take note what this does not say: it *does not say that ideas depict, mirror, or resemble things*. It says they are *parallel articulations* of a *single* expressive order. This is the ontological move we need to free the camera obscura from the grasp of identity and negation. Representation, on this model, was never a relation of likeness between an inner picture and an outer object. Treating it as two, assuming the mind-image and the world-image are distinct substances that must somehow come to resemble one another, rather than different attributes of the one, is the assumption I keep finding underneath the arguments in the rest of this essay. Neither the mind nor body alone satisfies the representational descriptions or rules set out by those arguments, and that assumption is what leaves them at odds with the world.

Even Spinoza, mind you, couldn't do without the figure. At the summit of the *Ethics*, where he argues that we "sense and experience that we are eternal", he reaches for it: "the eyes of the mind with which it sees and observes things are the proofs themselves" (Vp23s). But look at what surrounds it. He has just said there can be "no *traces*" of our eternity in the body, and that we *sense* (*sentimus*) it. The eyes of the mind sit between a trace and a feeling, and the eyes are proofs: demonstrations, procedures. Even here the eye works like a machine for producing conclusions, not a window.

Deleuze continues,

> It is not difference which presupposes opposition but opposition which presupposes difference, and far from resolving difference by tracing it back to a foundation, opposition betrays and distorts it. Our claim is not only that difference in itself is not 'already' contradiction, but that it cannot be reduced or traced back to contradiction, since the latter is not more but less profound than difference. On what condition is difference traced or projected on to a flat space? Precisely when it has been forced into a previously established identity, when it has been placed on the slope of the identical which makes it reflect or desire identity, and necessarily takes it where identity wants it to go - namely, into the negative.  
*—Gilles Deleuze, Difference & Repetition, p.51*


It is worth noting, if only to forestall the objection that this is a purely continental complaint, that [[Wiki/Philosophers/Richard Rorty|Richard Rorty]] makes structurally the same argument from within the analytic/pragmatic tradition. Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature (1979) traces the idea of mind as a "glassy essence,"[^glassy] an inner arena that mirrors an outer world with greater or lesser accuracy, and argues that epistemology as a discipline is largely the history of polishing this mirror rather than questioning whether the mind is the kind of thing that could have a surface at all. Two very different philosophical cultures arrive, independently, at the same diagnosis: the trouble is not any particular theory of knowledge, but the visual scene those theories keep restaging.

[^glassy]: Rorty borrows the phrase from C. S. Peirce's "Man's Glassy Essence" (1892), and Peirce from *Measure for Measure*: "his glassy essence, like an angry ape". The mirror-mind was a Shakespearean insult before it was an epistemology.

Amusingly, Rorty's immunity to this mistake is speculative at best:  
> I argue that when extended in a certain way they let us see truth as, in James's phrase, "what it is better for us to believe," rather than as "the accurate representation of reality." Or, to put the point less provocatively, they show us that the notion of "accurate representation" is simply an automatic and empty compliment which we pay to those beliefs which are successful in helping us do what we want to do.  
—Richard Rorty, *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979)

I hope you can **see** the irony in his turns of phrase here, that representation would be **shown** to be an empty compliment in the face of practice.

Rorty, to be fair to him, has an answer ready (his pragmatism trades truth for [[Wiki/Concepts/Warranted Assertibility|warranted assertion]]). "Let us see" is a dead metaphor; it means what it literally means and commits him to nothing. And he never denied that the world pushes back on us. As Robert Brandom puts it, Rorty's critique of representation is "founded not on denying or ignoring the causal context in which our talk takes place [...] but precisely on a hard-headed insistence and focus upon the significance of that context". The world constrains us causally, all the time. What Rorty denies is that it has any *authority* over what we say: "the world does not speak. Only we do."

I can grant him that. The world doesn't speak. But it does *image*, and images aren't assertions; they don't claim authority over anything. A realism that asks the world to speak deserves Rorty's suspicion. A realism that only asks us to notice what the world keeps doing to our eyes, our films and our sensors doesn't need the world to have the last word, just *a* word that isn't ours.[^governor] Whether "I see what you mean" really commits us to nothing is a question about whether dead metaphors stay dead, and I'll come back to it.

[^governor]: Brandom notes that pragmatism had its own image of the mind all along: "The distinctive pragmatist shift in imagery for the mind is not from mirror to lamp, but from telescope and microscope to flywheel governor" (*Pragmatism and Idealism*, 2022). A machine, then, but one that regulates rather than one that cuts and keeps.

Part of what keeps me at this question is exactly that it won't stay answered. The visual figure turns up most reliably in the writing of its fiercest critics: Rorty letting us "see" and "showing" us, Deleuze looking for "a new *image* of thought", Spinoza's "eyes of the mind", [[Wiki/Philosophers/Jacques Derrida|Derrida]]'s metaphysics as white mythology written under the sun. If the metaphor were dead, its adversaries wouldn't keep reaching for it.

## Not a Metaphor

I promised to come back to the dead metaphor, so here is the objection at full strength. Donald Davidson put it most cleanly: metaphors "mean what the words, in their most literal interpretation, mean, and nothing more".[^davidson] On this view "I see what you mean" is a fossil. The eye in it died long ago; what's left is an idiom, and idioms commit nobody to anything. Rorty would add that the whole essay so far has been a kind of etymological séance, summoning the eye out of words that have long since stopped seeing. You can say "sunrise" without being a geocentrist.

[^davidson]: Donald Davidson, "What Metaphors Mean", *Critical Inquiry* 5 (1978). Davidson's point is that a metaphor's force lies in what it makes us notice, not in some second, hidden meaning.

It's a good objection, and I think it's wrong, for three reasons that come from three very different places.

The first is historical. [Hans Blumenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Blumenberg) spent a career showing that some metaphors can't be cashed out into concepts at all: they *orient* thinking rather than decorate it, and when they change, the thinking changes with them. His study of light as a metaphor for truth (1957) follows it from Plato's light, which makes things visible, through the blinding light of revelation, to the Enlightenment's lamp, something *we* carry into the dark corners. Each is a different theory of knowledge wearing the same figure. A dead metaphor doesn't keep changing its mind.

The second is Derrida's. In "White Mythology" he argues that philosophy can't step outside its metaphors to survey them, because the very concept of a concept is drawn from the field it would survey. Metaphysics, for him, is metaphor rubbed smooth, like a coin whose face has worn away while it goes on circulating at full value. The worn coin is still money. And the metaphor philosophy keeps returning to, Derrida notes, is the sun's: the heliotrope, the flower that turns to the light, is his name for metaphor itself.

The third is cognitive. George Lakoff and Mark Johnson showed in *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) that [conceptual metaphors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_metaphor) organise inference, not just vocabulary. If UNDERSTANDING IS SEEING, then a lot follows without anyone deciding it should: clarity is truth, obscurity is error, an argument has a "point of view", and the knower stands outside the scene, looking in. Dead metaphors do logical work. That's precisely what makes them hard to see.

![Foucault's quadrilateral of language from The Order of Things, drawn over a dusk roofline](Media/Foucault_Language_1.jpg)
> *[[Michel Foucault|Foucault]]'s quadrilateral of language from* The Order of Things*, drawn over a dusk roofline. Attribution, articulation, designation, derivation. In the classical age the tropes, metaphor among them, sit at one corner of the table of language, not outside it.*

There's a fourth reason, and it was already in the second section. When Deleuze and Guattari say the image is a machine, they ask, of their own concept, "in anything more than a metaphorical sense?" and answer yes. When Walton says we see through photographs, he warns us against "watering down this suggestion". Two traditions that agree on almost nothing both refuse to let their optical claims be read as figures of speech. They know what Davidson's objection would cost them.

The linguistic turn tried to settle all this by moving house: from the mirror to the text (Derrida) or to the conversation (Rorty). But a change of medium isn't an escape. Language, too, is a mode of representation, and it dissolves into difference and repetition along with the image (which is roughly the sentence I put at the top of this essay in my first year of university, before I understood it). The disagreement worth having isn't between pictures and words, it's between two ideas of difference: Derrida's *différance*, which *defers*, always postponing the meaning it promises, and Deleuze's difference, which *produces*, making something each time it repeats.

### The hands

Try saying any of this without the eye. It can be done, but it costs something:

I can tell you that I grasp the argument, that I follow it, that I hear what you're getting at, that it strikes me, that it rings true, that it sits badly with me, that I have a feel for it, a hold on it, a sense of where it goes. Knowing becomes a matter of contact and rhythm, of what touches and what sounds, of what weighs and what moves. Arguments grow heavy or slight; positions turn firm or shaky; a thinker becomes someone you can lean on.[^lipogram] The vocabulary is all there. It just has a different body.

[^lipogram]: That paragraph contains no seeing. It's harder than it reads, a little like writing without the letter *e*, which Georges Perec did for a whole novel (*La Disparition*, 1969).

And it was there from the start. Go back to the list that opened this essay: *grasp*, *get*, *comprehend*, *apprehend*. These are the hand's words, from *prehendere*, to seize. German philosophy's word for the concept itself, *Begriff*, comes from *greifen*, to grip; our *concept* comes from *concipere*, to take in, to conceive. Alfred North Whitehead built an entire metaphysics on *prehension*, the act by which one thing takes hold of another, and he diagnosed exactly what had gone wrong with the eye:

> As to the direct knowledge of the actual world as a datum for the immediacy of feeling, we first refer to Descartes in Meditation I, "These hands and this body are mine"; also to Hume in his many assertions of the type, we see with our eyes. Such statements witness to direct knowledge of the antecedent functioning of the body in sense-perception. [...] It is this withness that makes the body the starting point for our knowledge of the circumambient world. [...] Hume and Descartes in their theory of direct perceptive knowledge dropped out this withness of the body; and thus confined perception to presentational immediacy.  
—Alfred North Whitehead, *Process and Reality* (1929)

We see *with* our eyes. The eye is an organ of contact before it is a window, and modern philosophy, in Whitehead's telling, simply forgot the *with*. Molyneux's old puzzle (would a man born blind, suddenly given sight, recognise by eye the shapes he knew by touch?) was only ever a puzzle because sight and touch had been split apart in the first place.[^molyneux] And the split isn't only historical. Some people, perhaps a few in every hundred, report no voluntary mental imagery at all, a condition only named in 2015 as [aphantasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphantasia), and they reason, remember and understand perfectly well. If knowing were seeing, they would be epistemically blind. They aren't.

[^molyneux]: More on the puzzle in [[Wiki/Concepts/Molyneuxs Problem|Molyneux's Problem]]; and for a philosophy of the body that refuses the split, Merleau-Ponty's [[Wiki/Texts/Phenomenology of Perception (Merleau-Ponty)|Phenomenology of Perception]], with its hand that touches and is touched at once.

### The wound

The founding scene of Christian belief is a touch, not a look. Thomas, who missed the first appearance of the risen Christ, sets his terms:

> Except I shall see in his hands the print of the nails, and put my finger into the print of the nails, and thrust my hand into his side, I will not believe. [...] Then saith he to Thomas, Reach hither thy finger, and behold my hands; and reach hither thy hand, and thrust it into my side: and be not faithless, but believing. [...] Jesus saith unto him, Thomas, because thou hast seen me, thou hast believed: blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed.  
—[John 20:25–29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+20%3A24-29&version=KJV) (KJV)

![Caravaggio, The Incredulity of Saint Thomas, c. 1601–2](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/The_Incredulity_of_Saint_Thomas-Caravaggio_%281601-2%29.jpg/1280px-The_Incredulity_of_Saint_Thomas-Caravaggio_%281601-2%29.jpg)
> *Caravaggio,* [The Incredulity of Saint Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Incredulity_of_Saint_Thomas_(Caravaggio)) *(c. 1601–2), public domain, via Wikimedia Commons. The finger inside the wound.*

Look at what Thomas asks for. Not a vision but "the print of the nails" (the Greek is *typos*, a stamp, an impression, the root of our *type*), and the right to put his finger in it. The wound is a trace in exactly the sense this essay has been building: a mark left by contact, verified by contact. The finger that goes in is the *index* finger, which is also the name Peirce gave to the sign that works by physical connection, smoke to fire, footprint to foot, photograph to light. And there's a lovely paradox in the scene. The most empirical demand in the gospels, *show me and let me touch*, ends in the most complete confession of faith ("My Lord and my God"). The man who wanted proof gets it and believes; and the blessing goes to those who never got to touch at all.

Nick Land, who has never been gentle about anything, makes the same point about every act of vision: we still think like "ancient Hellenes, interpreting vision as an outward movement of perception, rather than as a subtilized retinal wounding, inflicted by exogenous energies".[^land] Light doesn't go out from the eye to fetch the world; the world comes in and marks the eye. Which brings us back to the eclipse at the start of this essay, and the first wound in it. The eye was always being touched.

[^land]: Nick Land, *The Thirst for Annihilation: Georges Bataille and Virulent Nihilism* (1992), p. 21. The "ancient Hellenes" are the extramission theorists (Empedocles, Plato's *Timaeus*, Euclid), for whom the eye sent out a ray. Alhazen reversed the arrow; the camera obscura is the machine of the reversal.

So, to the second question this essay keeps asking, alongside the first:

<blockquote className="pullquote">What is a wound? An image you can put your finger in.</blockquote>

If the image isn't a metaphor, then the question becomes how philosophy managed to treat it as one for so long, how a cut in a flow of light became a copy, and the copy became an institution: the dark room of the mind, the camera of ideology, the photograph as evidence. That is a story about representation.
