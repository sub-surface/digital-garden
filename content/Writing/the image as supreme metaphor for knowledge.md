# ⁛ Zero

Visual metaphors for knowledge, I find (and you will too), are everywhere.  

> I *see* what you mean, that *looks* right to me, I *get* it, I *grasp* the concept, I *perceive* the truth, I *recognise* the object, I *envision* the future, I *imagine* the possibilities, I *picture* the scene, I *visualise* the solution, I *observe* the phenomenon, I *notice* the details, I *spot* the error, I *detect* the pattern, I *discern* the meaning, I *comprehend* the argument, I *apprehend* the situation. 

or, in latin

> *aspectum* , *visum*, *spectare*, *videre*, *intueri*, *conspicere*, *perspicere*, *observare*, *considerare*, *cognoscere*, *intellegere*, *percipere*, *comprehendere*, *apprehendere*.

They are so ubiquitous, that they are often overlooked, yet they are so powerful that they shape the way we think about the world down to the molecular level, infiltrating thought with its privation over sensation, calling for our sight to explicate the world, to make sense of it.  

As a photographer, I reluctantly approach this topic with a heavy bias *in favour* of the visual: to place the image at the seat of understanding, as supreme metaphor, I think, is an understandable mistake! For who wouldn't be drawn to the idea that images could convey the presence of an object (Barthes' *ça-a-été*)?  

My aim of this essay is to challenge my own bias, by looking at how (un-)knowing use of visual metaphor in philosophy has shaped our understanding of knowledge itself. 

# ⁖ One

## The history of the visual metaphor for knowledge

Aristotle's naive assertion, then Plato's sun/cave (Republic 507b–517a) giving it its full metaphysical architecture (the Good as sun, knowledge as illumination), then Descartes's "natural light" and the mind's eye (Meditations, Regulae) as the move that internalises vision — makes seeing a model for certainty itself, not just perception.



# ⁖ Two

## The image of thought

The clearest statement of what has gone wrong here belongs, unsurprisingly, to Deleuze. In [[Difference & Repetition]], he argues that philosophy has, almost without noticing, modelled thinking on an act of recognition: a subject encounters an object, matches it against a concept already possessed, and calls this matching "knowledge." Deleuze names this the dogmatic image of thought (Deleuze, 1994, ch. 3) — and the word image is not incidental. Recognition presupposes exactly the visual scene Plato and Descartes describe: a clear object, correctly identified, held steady before an inner eye. The metaphor of vision is not illustrating an independently arrived-at theory of knowledge; it is supplying the theory's structure and then hiding behind it.

Spinoza offers the resource for thinking past this without simply inverting it into another representationalism. In the Ethics, mind and body are not two substances that must somehow resemble or correspond to one another; they are one substance expressed under two attributes, such that "the order and connexion of ideas is the same as the order and connexion of things" (Spinoza, Ethics, IIp7, trans. Curley, 1996). Notice what this proposition does not say: it does not say that ideas depict, mirror, or resemble things. It says they are parallel articulations of a single expressive order. This is the ontological move your Deleuze/Spinoza framing needs: representation, on this model, was never a relation of likeness between an inner picture and an outer object. Treating it as one — assuming the mind contains images of things the way a photograph contains an image of a face — is the specific error this essay is built to diagnose.

It is worth noting, if only to forestall the objection that this is a purely continental complaint, that Richard Rorty makes structurally the same argument from within the analytic tradition. Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature (1979) traces the idea of mind as a "glassy essence," an inner arena that mirrors an outer world with greater or lesser accuracy, and argues that epistemology as a discipline is largely the history of polishing this mirror rather than questioning whether the mind is the kind of thing that could have a surface at all. Two very different philosophical cultures arrive, independently, at the same diagnosis: the trouble is not any particular theory of knowledge, but the visual scene those theories keep restaging.
