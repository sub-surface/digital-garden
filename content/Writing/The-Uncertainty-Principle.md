> *This essay was originally written during my second year of the BA Documentary Photography programme at the University of South Wales (2025), and first published in two parts on [Substack](https://newplace2frown.substack.com/p/cont-the-uncertainty-principle) in February 2025. # Part One

## Introduction

Hito Steyerl, born 1966, is a filmmaker, artist, philosopher, and more.

A unique voice in the art world, with important ideas to be considered for any practicing documentary photographer, Steyerl's hybrid practice-theoretical approach places doubt at the centre of her conception of modern **documentary practice**.

This essay aims to investigate Steyerl's *principle of documentary **uncertainty***, through her theoretical writings and filmography as it reshapes traditional notions of truth and authenticity in documentary, and how this might inform my own documentary practice going forward.

## Documentary (un)certainty

Since the earliest printed photography, such as Talbot's *The Pencil of Nature*, photographs have been presented and used as documentary evidence and have generally been held (even if it was by magic) to represent the real (Flusser, 2011, p. 6). The Griersonian, "Creative treatment of actuality"— a definition of documentary which highlighted the tension between creativity and truth in documentary film (Grierson, 1933), remains a central issue in discourse around photography and documents today (Buckland, 2014). However, the mass production of cameras and democratisation of photography, as recognised by [[Walter Benjamin]] (Benjamin, 2008), seemed to allow for a more subjective mode of representation. By the late 20th century, this culminated with exhibitions like *New Documents* at MoMA 1967 where the documentary mode moved "toward more personal ends" (Szarkowski, 1967).

One key assumption in the photography discussed by thinkers like Benjamin or Grierson, be it photography in pursuit of the creative or the actual, is that the images should have a clarity, sharpness or "straight-ness" (Kramer, 1973) as Walker Evans might say, to render the subject in a way similar to the human eye. Henri Cartier-Bresson, branded the "eye of the century", famously used a 50mm lens, which matches the focal length of the human eye; the adoption of colour by photographers like William Eggleston in the US and Paul Graham in the UK reflected an attempt by documentary photographers to more "accurately" depict their subjects— where "accurate" seems to mean, *'as you would see it with the un-aided eye'*. This essay doesn't seek to critique these assumptions about how to approach documentary work, but to add a new conceptual toolbox for documentary practitioners to use where appropriate. This toolbox arises as a principle of uncertainty, epitomised in both the theory and practice of Hito Steyerl. I hope to identify and describe some of the tools which are made possible by applying this uncertainty, such as using low-resolution (poor) images, meta-commentary (breaking the fourth wall), implicating the subject, and dethroning the author as authority—hoping to apply a multiplicity of these ideas in conjunction.

### Essays: Theoretical Basis

Steyerl's numerous theoretical insights across her published essays make a complete summary of her work in its totality difficult. (Pek, 2022) looks at Steyerl's "\[...\] films, videos, and installations from 1998 to 2015" and describes Steyerl's work as "committed to exploring the representation and production of reality". This can be seen most clearly in the essay *Documentary Uncertainty*, where Steyerl critiques,

> *"\[...\] documents are usually condensations of power. They reek of authority, certification, expertise and concentrate epistemological hierarchies. Dealing with documents is thus a tricky thing; especially if one aims to deconstruct power, one has to keep in mind, that existing documents are—as Walter Benjamin once wrote—mainly made and authorised by victors and rulers." (Steyerl, 2011)*

This quote lays out her theoretical approach clearly: Steyerl wants "to deconstruct power" by "dealing with documents" which prop up and justify "victors and rulers" who traditionally "make and authorise," said documents. While *deconstruction*, a post-modern philosophical method pioneered by Jacques Derrida, appears to be the key influence in this passage, I would argue that the central focus on power relations places [[Michel Foucault]] as a more appropriate thinker to whom Steyerl may owe a debt. The aforementioned dissertation (Pek, 2022) describes Steyerl's own PhD Dissertation, *Die Farbe der Wahrheit* (The Colour of Truth), as utilising ideas from "Michel Foucault, Walter Benjamin, and Theodor Adorno to propose an understanding of the documentary with regard to Foucault's triad of discourse, knowledge, and power" (Pek, 2022). Unfortunately, Steyerl's dissertation has not yet been publicly translated from the original German. However, we can keep in mind Foucault, Benjamin, and Adorno's work as key references when looking at the manifestation of Steyerl's theoretical insights in her filmmaking, and when considering my own documentary practice.

#### The Poor Image (2009)

In this essay, Steyerl identifies "a copy in motion. Its quality is bad, its resolution substandard. As it accelerates, it deteriorates" (Steyerl, 2009). This conceptualisation of the poor image may serve as a model for potential types of images that go beyond the limits which traditional documentality imposes on photographic practice. As mentioned, the pursuit of accuracy relative to "normal" human vision places limits on the types of representations made in a project. Cartier-Bresson, balancing the line between creativity and actuality throughout his work, was quoted saying "Sharpness is a bourgeoise concept"—and while Steyerl departs rapidly from the thought and practice of Bresson, their mutual philosophies appear to align against the notion of sharpness as paramount in photographic practice.

Beyond low-resolution images, Steyerl highlights *Circulation* as a key aspect in the production of poor images. *Circulation* is the life of an image after it has been produced, usually after it has been disseminated on the internet, where poor images live a kind of "afterlife" being digitally reproduced and shared, mutating along the way. Steyerl highlights the "resurrection as" poor images being the result of,

> "the neoliberal radicalization of the concept of culture as commodity, to the commercialization of cinema, its dispersion into multiplexes, and the marginalization of independent filmmaking. \[...\] resistant or non-conformist visual matter disappeared from the surface into an underground of alternative archives and collections, kept alive only by a network of committed organizations and individuals, who would circulate bootlegged VHS copies amongst themselves." (Steyerl, 2009)

This pre-internet process of circulation produced poor images as a reflex against the political and economic throes of the 1990s, where it was left to this "network of committed organisations and individuals" to both share and preserve the images. Naturally, the visual degradation which followed was an unintended feature of this archival practice, but this very degradation itself begins to serve as documentary evidence of these political and economic factors. As Steyerl notes,

> "With the possibility to stream video online, this condition started to dramatically change. An increasing number of rare materials reappeared on publicly accessible platforms, some of them carefully curated (Ubuweb) and some just a pile of stuff (YouTube)." (Steyerl, 2009)

In this essay, I'd like to focus on photographic production, but the same ideas could be applied to other documentary practices such as filmmaking or curation. The poor image is Steyerl's best-known theoretical contribution—since its first appearance in e-flux, the poor image has become a prevalent idea in media studies (Schonig, 2022) and serves as a powerful question to traditional ideas about documentary. In *Authenticity and the poor image in the age of deep learning* (Wasielewski, 2023), the author uses the poor image as a theoretical frame for looking at notions of authenticity in contemporary photography in relation to deep learning, where "copies or reproductions are now forming the basis for a new model of authenticity, which exists latently in the formal properties of a digital image" (Wasielewski, 2023). This highlights how Steyerl's contribution, looking at the "formal properties" of the digital image, serves as a powerful theoretical tool for critiquing traditional documentary images, and opens new modes of representation when it comes to the promise of emerging technologies and the future of documentary amidst a shifting media landscape.

#### Documentarism as Politics of Truth (2003)

In *Documentarism as Politics of Truth*, Steyerl asks and answers, "Does truth determine politics or politics truth? It is a question of how the production of truth has always been influenced and standardized by power relations". Steyerl's example of this is the Colin Powell presentation on nuclear weapons in Iraq at the UN Security Council in 2002/3, where the use of heavily annotated documents made,

> "Every indexical sign reference, which is traditionally regarded as a characteristic of documentary authenticity, \[…\] quite paltry in the pictures and charts and \[were\] mainly supported by "secret" sources."

Thereby suggesting that the manipulation of documents negatively impacted their authenticity. This shows how context is highly impactful on what we might call the "truth" of documents in general, and that even when documents are produced authentically, we should still be critical of the claims these documents are used in service of furthering, since curatorial and editorial practices might totally change their meaning. In the final point of the essay, Steyerl points out that "We have to leave it open – and hope that this confusing gap will open up the path to other visibilities." Highlighting Steyerl's commitment to new ways of seeing the truth and leaving the door open for more discursivity and novel experimentation on this "path".

Here, the uncertainty principle manifests similarly to [[Roland Barthes]]' 1967 proclamation of the "death of the author"—where audience interpretation supersedes authorial intent when analysing a text. Dethroning the author in documentary means critically looking at how politics influences the production of truth during the production of documents. Steyerl draws upon Foucault directly, invoking his term "politics of truth" to highlight the "set of rules that determine the production of truth, distinguishing true statements from false ones, and fixing procedures of the production of truth." This invocation suggests recognising the danger of appointing the author as an authority on the authenticity of documents, in favour of protecting the aforementioned "complex and codified procedures for determining truth" from manipulation by bad actors and misaligned power structures.

# Part Two: Case Study — *How Not To Be Seen: A Fucking Didactic Educational .MOV File* (2013)

**IF IT IS TRUE** that the truth is constructed by those around us, often nefariously through the manipulation of symbols and images to convey false narratives, then Steyerl offers us a way out of being victimised by this ocular apparatus with the promise of her film's title: *How Not To Be Seen*.[^hntbs] The title was taken originally from a 1970 Monty Python sketch of the same name, (How Not to Be Seen, 1970) filmed in the style of a classic BBC informative video, showing the audience how one might evade the gaze of the camera—placed looking at nature, with named figures obscured until instructed to reveal themselves or be detonated, often behind various greenery.

[^hntbs]: Link to view [How Not To Be Seen](https://www.artforum.com/video/hito-steyerl-how-not-to-be-seen-a-fucking-didactic-educational-mov-file-2013-165845/) (2013).

![How Not To Be Seen — Monty Python's Flying Circus, 1970](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C-M2hs3sXGo)
> *How Not To Be Seen — Monty Python's Flying Circus, 1970 (YouTube)*

This sketch, even though short and light-heartedly ironic, quietly evokes themes of state surveillance, the gaze, and the power of those with the camera over those being seen (the sketch concludes with the narrator laughing maniacally as he explodes the various individuals "hiding in" certain shots).

The subtitle, already breaking formal documentary sensibilities through the use of expletive, informs us that the film is "fucking didactic"—providing a moral teaching, and in the format of a ".MOV file" which places the film in the digital medium. Through the title alone, we understand that the film is trying to inform us about being seen in the digital age and offers us an ironic tutorial in escaping this gaze.

Interestingly, the Greek root of the term for *didactic*— "*didaskein*" is very similar to the Latin root for *document*— "*docere*" in that they both mean "*to teach*". This informs my analysis of this film, as I am thinking about how Steyerl's ideas might relate to my documentary practice going forward, and the relationship between images and reality. With that in mind, I'll consider the techniques and ideas Steyerl employs in her film. Structurally, the film is divided into "five lessons", each revolving around not being seen as the title suggests:

> LESSON I: HOW TO MAKE SOMETHING INVISIBLE FOR A CAMERA
>
> LESSON II: HOW TO BE INVISIBLE IN PLAIN SIGHT
>
> LESSON III: HOW TO BECOME INVISIBLE BY BECOMING A PICTURE
>
> LESSON IV: HOW TO BE INVISIBLE BY DISAPPEARING
>
> LESSON V: HOW TO BECOME INVISIBLE BY MERGING INTO A WORLD MADE OF PICTURES
>
> (How Not To Be Seen: A Fucking Didactic Educational .MOV File, 2013)

Each lesson seems to increase in its impossibility, from standard visual effects in *Lesson I* to becoming one with a universal multiplicity of images in its conclusion. Visually within the film, this takes place starting with Steyerl's use of green screens and green paint to key out parts of the image, making them (and herself as in *Figure 3*) invisible. This simple process of visual effects reflects early notions of image manipulation using simple tools such as Adobe Photoshop or After Effects to simply alter documents. Contrasted by later in the film (*Figure 1*), where dancing figures robed in green are projected onto a studio greenscreen, showing the total disappearance of the photographic representation, except for some of the edges of the screen, where the artificiality of the production leaks through, into the real…

![Figure 1: Still at 10m:24s, How Not To Be Seen (2013)](Media/Steyerl_HNTBS_1.jpg)
> **Figure 1:** *Still at 10m:24s (How Not To Be Seen: A Fucking Didactic Educational .MOV File, 2013)*

A key difference between Steyerl's film and the Monty Python sketch is that Steyerl appears to be coming from a much more explicitly critical perspective. Informed deeply by the political landscape mentioned before, the film is situated in a post 9/11 world, released the same year as the Edward Snowden leaks, which detailed the far-reaching results of the 2001 Patriot Act—giving US intelligence agencies the ability to spy on US citizens in the name of,

> "Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism" (United States, 2001).

This context surrounding the film enriches the content by allowing the visual material and themes to enter a dialogue with the political reality of the time, with the satire of the past (Monty Python), and with the shifting apparatus of the internet itself. At the same time, the transgressive power of satire in the Monty Python film should not be lost, since it still evokes ideas of the gaze through the narrator's use of (explosive) power on those who were hiding, capturing the fundamental Foucauldian critique of power. If documents are "usually condensations of power" (Steyerl, 2011), might this hybrid practice be a kind of *post-documentary*?

Steyerl's oblique approach to documentary emerges from her strong critiques of it, highlighted by (Morinis, 2014) who describes the film as "a sly parody of an instructional video". The ironic tone used throughout the film could potentially point towards the absurdity of remaining unseen in an age of hyper-vigilance through technology. This irony, combined with the use of mixed virtual and photographic representations throughout, produce a surreal conflict of reality when watching the film; it demonstrates Steyerl's underlying critique of certainty by forcing the audience to ask, "what's real about these virtual representations". It also invites the audience to ask, "why would I not want to be seen?" Since 20th century ideas of individualism took hold in western audiences, the very notion of being un-seen might be anathema to American ideas of celebrity, or to socially progressive ideas of mutual recognition. As a result, Steyerl's film is wrapped up in many contemporary debates simultaneously. Low-resolution imagery in *How Not To Be Seen* invites a meta-commentary about the "truthiness", veracity, indexicality etc. of images online— "As it accelerates, it deteriorates" (Steyerl, 2009).

![Figure 2: Still at 8m:15s, How Not To Be Seen (2013)](Media/Steyerl_HNTBS_2.jpg)
> **Figure 2:** *Still at 8m:15s (How Not To Be Seen: A Fucking Didactic Educational .MOV File, 2013)*

As the film progresses, photographic representation takes a back seat and Steyerl re-appropriates digital imagery from a luxury accommodation video showing projections of to-be-built locations. Within these spaces, as seen in *Figure 2*, Steyerl places "pixels" which dance and mill about these non-existent spaces. The disembodied robotic narration explains,

> *"To become invisible one has to become smaller or equal to one pixel"*
> (How Not To Be Seen: A Fucking Didactic Educational .MOV File, 2013).

What should we take from this post-representational take on the documentary? Well, we should think about the critiques of power mentioned earlier; if *How Not To Be Seen* can be interpreted as a documentary, would that not make Steyerl a very agent of these issues where documentaries "*reek of authority, certification, expertise and concentrate epistemological hierarchies*"? I argue, that without some of these interventions Steyerl employs, this would be true. To offer an evasion from the gaze usually implies that you are not subjecting others to your own, and Steyerl takes great care to ensure that she is the only person explicitly represented on screen and, as a result, implicates herself as both the subject and author of this documentary work.

![Figure 3: Still at 4m:59s, How Not To Be Seen (2013)](Media/Steyerl_HNTBS_3.jpg)
> **Figure 3:** *Still at 4m:59s (How Not To Be Seen: A Fucking Didactic Educational .MOV File, 2013)*

In implicating herself, Steyerl's approach dethrones the modernist ideal of the authoritative and objective documentarian, in favour of a more post-modern and multiplicitous perspective where the author offers one mere perspective out of many others. In *How Not To Be Seen* this multiplicity partly manifests through the script, where in Lesson Five it is said, "there are fifty-four ways to merge into a world made of images". This tongue-in-cheek use of an arbitrary number serves to suggest to the audience that any amount of advice given in the film can only go so far, and that these strategies for escaping the gaze of late capitalism are to be discovered, not disseminated to the masses like a 1960s instructional video.

## Conclusion

In this essay, I hope to have shown how Hito Steyerl sits at the epicentre of a revolution in the way documentaries can be made and interpreted. With her insights, I can look towards making my own documentary projects and injecting them with a rich sense of doubt, inviting reflection on the very nature of documentary practice itself. A rare treasure, Steyerl's work stands as a route out of complacency and traditionalism for documentarians. By recognising the organising powers behind documents, dethroning individuals as arbiters of truth, implicating those who seek to turn their gaze on the powerless, and keeping a strong theoretical basis to inform these decisions, I think that Steyerl's films will stand as a crucial body of work for any image-maker working in contemporary contexts such as digital media, politics, and philosophy.
