<div className="dropcap">

When you look at a star, you are looking at light that left it a long time ago. Some of the stars you can see may no longer be there. This is usually offered as a melancholy fact, a reminder that the present is less present than it seems. I want to take it as an ordinary one. Everything we see has already happened. Sunlight left the sun about eight minutes before it reaches you; the light from this screen reaches your eye a few billionths of a second after it leaves. Seeing is always a little late. An image is what arrives.

</div>

Roland Barthes knew this was the heart of photography:

> The photograph is literally an emanation of the referent. From a real body, which was there, proceed radiations which ultimately touch me, who am here; the duration of the transmission is insignificant; the photograph of the missing being, as Sontag says, will touch me like the delayed rays of a star. A sort of umbilical cord links the body of the photographed thing to my gaze: light, though impalpable, is here a carnal medium, a skin I share with anyone who has been photographed.  
—Roland Barthes, [[Books/Camera Lucida|*Camera Lucida*]] (1980, trans. Richard Howard)

"The duration of the transmission is insignificant." Eight minutes or eighty years, the structure is the same: an effect that has come loose from its cause and goes on existing without it. This essay is about that structure, and about two very different ways in which light can come to have no source.

## Hysteresis

The Italian philosopher Maurizio Ferraris has a name for effects that outlive their causes. He borrows it from physics, where it describes a material that keeps the history of what was done to it (a magnet that stays magnetised after the field is gone):

> [...] from a metaphysical [...] point of view it designates the circumstance in which an event keeps track of the events that preceded it, so that the effects outlive the causes. The scratch on my mobile phone that reminds me of when I dropped it, the compound interests of capital, learning a language and the neurosis that pushes us to set up relationships with others by repeating patterns experienced when we were children are just a few examples of hysteresis.  
—Maurizio Ferraris, "Metaphysics of the Web", [*Rivista di estetica*](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/7070)

And it isn't only a fact about minds and money:

> Something happens – a summer storm, a forest fire, a meteorite falling on the Moon – and matter remembers it in the form of moisture, ash or craters [...] Indeed, matter is the archive of past events: craters recall ancient explosions, mountains preserve shells dating back to the time when they made up the bottom of now-vanished seas.

Ferraris makes a bold claim for this. Asked the oldest question in metaphysics, why there is something rather than nothing, he answers: "Because there is hysteresis, namely the past repeated by matter and remembered by memory." And he notices something about the trace that the parent essay has been circling: "The effect was passive and bound to the here and now of its production, but the trace is active (it exists independently of the cause)."

That's a good description of a photograph. The light that struck the film was passive, a momentary contact. The negative is active. It goes on doing things, being printed, sent, looked at, grieved over, long after the moment and often after the people in it.

One disagreement to flag at the outset. Ferraris thinks the twentieth century got the future of media wrong: it expected writing to give way to "warmer media, such as orality and images", and instead recording, documents and writing won. I think he's right about the boom in recording and wrong to set images against it. A photograph is the purest case of what he calls hysteresis: matter remembering, with no one doing the writing. (To argue properly in the body of the essay.)

## Outline of the rest

### The first virtual world

- From my 2024 draft ("Virtual Worlds, Invisible Images", unpublished): Plato's cave as "arguably the first virtual world in philosophy". Revisit: the cave is usually read as a world of copies, but the shadows are traces too. The fire is real; the shadows are caused. The prisoners' error isn't that they see images but that they take them for the whole.
- Link back to the puddle station in the parent essay (Phaedo 99d–e: Socrates looks at the sun in water to save his eyes).

### Light from dead sources

- Nietzsche, *Gay Science* §125 (the epigraph): the madman is "too early"; the death of God is light that hasn't arrived yet. The image runs in both directions: delayed rays from a dead source, and an event whose light is still travelling towards us.
- Barthes, above: the umbilical cord of light, "a skin I share with anyone who has been photographed".
- The deaths of photography. Dewdney, via Batchen, on "a number of distinct points of death plunged into the body of photography", and his own "zombie photography". The point to make: each announced death is itself a delayed ray. Photography keeps dying and keeps arriving.
- Brassier and Lyotard on solar death (already in [[Writing/The-Watchtower-and-the-Mirror|The Watchtower and the Mirror]]): every earthly image is already the trace of a star that will die. Keep it to a paragraph here; point across.

### Light with no source

- Steyerl, "Mean Images" (*New Left Review*, 2023): generative images "are not dependent on the actual impact of photons on a sensor, or on emulsion. They converge around the average, the median; hallucinated mediocrity. They represent the norm by signalling the mean. They replace likenesses with likelinesses."
- The distinction the essay turns on (workbench claim C37): a delayed ray has a source that has gone; a mean image never had one. Both are sourceless light, but in opposite senses. One is hysteresis: an effect that outlived its cause. The other is closer to an effect that never had a cause in particular, an average of many traces that is itself a trace of none.
- Galton's composite portraits as the ancestor (Steyerl's own comparison). AI renderings of famous news photographs as the test case: what is lost when the event is replaced by its likeliness.
- Hold back from the easy verdict. A mean image is still a real thing with real causes (data, labour, compute). The question isn't whether it's "fake" but what it keeps, and what it lets go.

### A third light

- The speculative documentary, "a construction that resists probability in order to create a new world" (Pinckers). Against the mean image, which is probability itself, a made image that admits it's made and stays true to something.
- Steyerl's blurred war footage: "these pictures do not really represent anything [...] But amazingly, we can still speak of truth with regard to them." (See [[Writing/The-Uncertainty-Principle|The Uncertainty Principle]].)

### The oldest light

- Ending: the cosmic microwave background, the oldest light there is, released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang when the universe first became transparent. Astronomers call the place it comes from "the surface of last scattering". It's a photograph of nothing in particular, the universe before there were things, and its tiny irregularities are where everything later came from: hysteresis at the largest scale there is.
- Close on the parent essay's refrain, recontextualised, not repeated.
