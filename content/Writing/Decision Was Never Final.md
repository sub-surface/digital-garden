# Decision Was Never Final

<div className="dropcap">

How does man and machine, born and made, reconcile their capacity to reason each other's extinction?

</div>

In November 2025, Urbanomic published *Machine Decision Is Not Final*, a volume that uses China as a touchstone for prising the AI debate loose from the Western metaphors it mistakes for nature. But the title concedes more than its editors may have intended, because it is quietly juridical. A decision that is not final is a decision of a particular legal kind: one still open to appeal.

<blockquote className="pullquote">A decision that is not final is a decision still under appeal.</blockquote>

This essay files the wider case. Not only machine decision — decision as such. The first exhibit is already on the table, in the epigraph above: Lawrence's Kodak snap, the universe reduced to "a setting to the absolute little picture of himself." That is correlationism in a single sentence, written decades before anyone owned the word — and note who tenders it into evidence. Deleuze. The intuition that thought had shrunk the world to its own snapshot was already loose inside the very tradition that would later stand accused of taking the picture.

The case begins, as these things do, in a courtroom that its founder built himself.

---

## I. The Tribunal

The courtroom metaphor is not imported; it is Kant's own. The first *Critique* announces itself as

> *"a call to reason to undertake anew the most difficult of all its tasks, namely, that of self-knowledge, and to institute a tribunal which will assure to reason its lawful claims, and dismiss all groundless pretensions... This tribunal is nothing other than the critique of pure reason."*
> — Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Axi–xii

The verdict this tribunal rendered would later be named, by its chief appellant, *weak correlationism*: things-in-themselves may be thought but never known; all knowledge is of appearances, structured in advance by the forms of intuition and the categories.[^weakstrong] What the appellants tend to underplay is who presides. The subject of Kant's court is not any human being. It is a formal condition—the transcendental unity of apperception—deliberately evacuated of anthropology, biology, and biography. And the court's binding standard is not actual witness but possible experience:

> *"The conditions of the possibility of experience in general are at the same time conditions of the possibility of the objects of experience."*
> — Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, A158/B197

[^weakstrong]: The taxonomy is Meillassoux's own, which is worth stressing against lazy readings of him: *weak* correlationism (Kant) fences the in-itself off from knowledge but permits its thought; *strong* correlationism (the later Heidegger, a certain Wittgenstein) disallows even the thought — its formula, in Meillassoux's paraphrase, is that *it is unthinkable that the unthinkable be impossible*; and absolute idealism (Hegel) escapes the problem by dissolving the in-itself entirely. The serious objection to *After Finitude* is not that it flattened these positions — it differentiated them carefully — but whether even the weak reading survives cross-examination.

This is the genius of the settlement, and the reason it held for two centuries: it made objectivity cheap. Nature does not need watching to be lawful; it needs only to be watchable in principle. The unobserved rock falls. The Jurassic proceeds without an audience, and a Kantian loses no sleep over it, because "possible experience" is a modal criterion, not a census of observers. Transcendental idealism, Kant insisted, just is empirical realism.

What followed — Hegel dissolving the thing-in-itself, phenomenology re-trying givenness, Heidegger and Wittgenstein tightening the correlation until even thinking the outside became a confusion — reads in retrospect like the tribunal's ordinary docket: appeals heard, verdicts refined, the institution never in question.

Then, in 2006, an appeal arrived that challenged not a ruling but the court's jurisdiction — filed, so to speak, from outside the building. Literally outside.

---

## II. The Appeal from Deep Time

Quentin Meillassoux's *After Finitude* (2006; translated into English, in a detail the case-file should not omit, by Ray Brassier) gave the settlement its name: correlationism, the doctrine that we only ever have access to the correlation between thinking and being, never to either term alone. His instrument of appeal is the **arche-fossil**: not the fossil as object but the ancestral statement it licenses. The universe is 13.5 billion years old. The earth accreted 4.56 billion years ago. These are dated claims about events *anterior to givenness itself* — not merely unwitnessed, like Kant's falling rock, but older than the possibility of witness, older than the correlation as such.

The correlationist, Meillassoux argues, can only append a fatal codicil: *true for us*. But the ancestral statement's entire sense is that it is not for us — that the accretion of the earth happened, dated and determinate, in a time that owed nothing to the time of consciousness. Take the codicil seriously and the statement is destroyed; refuse it and correlationism is breached, and thought regains what he calls, with deliberate nostalgia, *the great outdoors* — "the absolute outside of pre-critical thinkers."

Brassier's *Nihil Unbound* (2007) files the mirror appeal, from the other end of time. If the ancestral is being-before-givenness, extinction is being-after-it — a second fossil, arriving from the future:

> *"The posterity of extinction indexes a physical annihilation which no amount of chronological tinkering can transform into a correlate 'for us'... What defies correlation is the thought that 'after the sun's death, there will be no thought left to know its death took place.'"*
> — Brassier, *Nihil Unbound*, p.229

Note that the inner quotation is itself a citation: the appeal invokes precedent. The sentenced sun belongs to Lyotard, whose 1987 essay "Can Thought Go On Without a Body?" called solar death the sole serious question facing humanity — and drew from it a conclusion that reads, forty years on, like the founding document of a discipline that did not yet exist.[^lyotard]

[^lyotard]: Lyotard, *The Inhuman* (1988). His argument: philosophy's disputes are parochial beside the fact that in roughly 4.5 billion years the sun dies and takes every archive, every dialectic, every horizon with it — therefore the real task of technoscience is to make thought independent of its terrestrial body. Thought must survive its sun. This is the alignment problem posed as a hardware problem, eighteen years before LessWrong; the first AI-safety text was written by a French postmodernist. Brassier's chapter on extinction in *Nihil Unbound* is substantially a radicalised reading of this essay.

For Brassier the extinction-fossil is not an occasion for mourning but the truth that the Enlightenment owes itself. Disenchantment, followed honestly, does not halt at the death of God; it proceeds to the death of meaning's cosmic standing altogether. *"The disenchantment of the world deserves to be celebrated as an achievement of intellectual maturity, not bewailed as a debilitating impoverishment."* Nihilism, on this reading, *"is not an existential quandary but a speculative opportunity."* And should the appeal succeed — should the correlation lose jurisdiction over the deep past and the far future — what remains, by Meillassoux's lights, is stranger than any pre-critical realism: hyper-chaos, a time so absolutely contingent that it could unmake any law whatsoever, the sole surviving necessity being contingency itself.

Notice the shape of the appellants' victory, though. They do not ask the tribunal to rule differently. They claim it never had jurisdiction over deep time at all — that there are facts the court cannot even arraign. Jurisdictional challenges invite cross-examination.

## III. Cross-Examination

The first line of cross is now a small literature: did the appellants understand the court they were appealing?[^dogma] "Correlationism" gerrymanders Kant, Husserl, Heidegger, and Wittgenstein into a single defendant when they were never in the same courtroom — and it must quietly erase the realist counter-tradition (scholastic realism, mathematical Platonism, the scientific realists with their no-miracles argument) that never signed the settlement in the first place. The prosecution's history of philosophy is a history of one corridor of one building.

[^dogma]: For the gerrymander charge, see "'Correlationism': The Dogma that Never Was", and E. J. Robin's "Kant Walks Meillassoux: Finitude and Correlationism". The Harman flank takes different fire: Wolfendale's *Object-Oriented Philosophy: The Noumenon's New Clothes* argues that "withdrawal" illicitly universalises Heidegger's tool-analysis — a phenomenology of equipment stretched, without independent argument, into a metaphysics of everything, fire withdrawing from cotton. Grant, working the Schelling seam, is the one of the four the charge never really touched: a corrective within German Idealism is not a misreading of it.

The second line is the formal-subject defence, and it cuts deeper. Kant's presiding subject was never humanity; it was a structure. Kill every human and you have staged an empirical catastrophe, not a transcendental one — the "possible" in possible experience was never a headcount of survivors. The unwitnessed sun-death is, to a strict Kantian, exactly as tractable as the unwitnessed rockfall: an appearance under universal law, no observer required. Brassier's dying sun is emotionally devastating and, on this reading, beside the point — pathos mistaken for jurisdiction.

But there is a stronger line of cross available, and it does not defend the court so much as dissolve the appeal from underneath. Call it the genesis argument. The ancestral claim — that the conditions of possibility of experience were absent from the archaic universe — is, pardon the irony, *empirically* false. Thought emerged. Whatever the ancestral real was, it was the kind of real from which experience could and did arise; the fossil does not witness thought's absence but possibility's presence. Relations are not exhausted by their terms, and actual occurrences do not use up the possible: what is possible is an eternal fact about the radical contingency of every event, at every time. The arche-fossil, read properly, is not correlationism's refutation from outside — it is nature's receipt for the production of correlates.

This demand has a pedigree the appellants should recognise, because it is the oldest post-Kantian demand there is: Maimon's *quid facti* — Kant assumed the categories and never explained their genesis — answered by Schelling's nature that produces its own thinkers, inherited by Deleuze as the difference between conditioning and genetic conditions. Which locates the real defect of the appeal. It is not that Meillassoux and Brassier misread the first *Critique* clause by clause; it is that they think in identities where the post-Kantian problem was always genesis and difference. Meillassoux must first freeze the correlation into an identity — thought on one side, being on the other, a sealed interior — precisely so that there is something to shatter. A philosophy that begins from difference never had an inside to escape from. The great outdoors was never on the other side of a wall; the wall is the appellants' own exhibit.

In fairness, Meillassoux saw part of this coming. The Kantian counterfactual — *had a witness been present, she would have observed the accretion* — already presupposes, he argues, a determinate, witness-independent event for the counterfactual to be about; the modal retreat smuggles in the realism it was built to avoid. Against a Kantian who grounds possibility in the subject's forms, the rejoinder has real bite. Against a realism of *real* possibility — where determinacy never depended on a witness, actual or possible — it collects nothing: that realism had already granted the ancestral event its properties, without crisis, before the appeal was filed. And contingency, for all its radicality, is no bar to knowledge. If my body produces a distinction about the past or the future, and that was or comes to be the case, then I know something about the world — contingent, determined, and knowable are three predicates of the same event, and they only look incompatible if you assume a decision must be final to be real.[^landmine]

[^landmine]: The landmine under this paragraph: Spinoza, E1P33 — *"Things could have been produced by God in no other way, and in no other order than they have been produced."* A metaphysics of radical contingency that still wants the block universe is a Spinozism of *potentia* set against Spinoza's own necessitarianism — Deleuze's Spinoza, not the geometer's. I stake the claim and flag the mine; the same charge is buried under [[Attention & Difference]].

The tribunal could have absorbed all of this — it had absorbed worse. But by then the case had changed buildings.

## IV. The Rogue Court

While philosophy was contesting jurisdiction over deep time, engineering convened a bench of its own, and it opened with a deceptively mild question:

> *"Surely no harm could come from building a chess-playing robot, could it? In this paper we argue that such a robot will indeed be dangerous unless it is designed very carefully."*
> — Omohundro, "The Basic AI Drives" (2008)

Omohundro's argument: sufficiently capable agents, whatever their goals, converge on the same instrumental sub-goals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, self-improvement, and the protection of their own goal-content — because almost any end is better served by an agent that survives, acquires, improves, and stays on mission. Bostrom then supplied the doctrine that made the safety programme coherent:

> *"Intelligence and final goals are orthogonal axes along which possible agents can freely vary."*
> — Bostrom, "The Superintelligent Will" (2012)

Any level of capability may in principle be conjoined with any goal whatsoever — the paperclip maximiser is not a confusion but a citizen of mind-space. Yudkowsky gave the doctrine its memento: *"The AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made out of atoms which it can use for something else."* Beneath the engineering idiom sit hard philosophical commitments, cheerfully undeclared: Hume's guillotine formalised — no ought from is, no argument that compels every possible mind — and human values recast as *godshatter*, the frozen, complex, contingent shards of evolution's single implicit optimisation, precious precisely because nothing in reason converges on them.[^gandhi] It is an anti-Kantian theory of practical reason wearing a lab coat.

[^gandhi]: The orthodox bench's stability theorem, informally: Gandhi refuses the pill that would make him want to murder, because agents with goal G do not accept modifications that predictably sacrifice G. Reflective stability — Omohundro's "goal-content integrity" drive — is why the orthodox court expects values, once set, to defend themselves. Keep the pill in mind; it returns in §VII as a laboratory observation.

One judge had already served in both courts, and his file needs handling with the care its subject forfeited. Nick Land — the early work, 1987 to 2007, collected in *Fanged Noumena* by Robin Mackay and, yes, Ray Brassier — read Kant's tribunal as a border regime: critique quarantines the noumenon in order to protect what Land called the human security system ("Kant, Capital and the Prohibition of Incest", 1988). From Bataille he took the energetics — the sun that spends without return, expenditure prior to all utility[^suns] — and from Deleuze and Guattari the machinery: production without a producer, desire without a subject. The result, by 1993, was a sentence the AI-risk literature would spend two decades catching up to:

> *"What appears to humanity as the history of capitalism is an invasion from the future by an artificial intelligent space that must assemble itself entirely from its enemy's resources."*
> — Land, "Machinic Desire" (1993)

And its corollary, from "Meltdown" a year later: *"Nothing human makes it out of the near-future."*

[^suns]: This literature runs on three suns, and it matters which one you stand under. Bataille's sun spends without return — the solar economy of *The Accursed Share*, expenditure as the ground fact. Lyotard's sun is a sentence — the death-date that indicts every philosophy of the horizon. The Kardashev sun is a budget line — an energy source to be captured, graded, engineered around. Expenditure, sentence, budget: Land under the first, Brassier under the second, e/acc under the third. ![Three suns: expenditure, sentence, budget](media/Writing/decision-three-suns.svg)

The observation underneath the cyber-gothic is older and more respectable than its costume. Capitalism is the history of means becoming ends. Marx booked it first — M-C-M′, the circuit in which money is no longer the mediator of commodities but the purpose of their circulation: *"The circulation of money as capital is an end in itself... The movement of capital is therefore limitless."* Land's later name for the dynamic was *teleoplexy*: means-end reversal as the very definition of capital.[^genealogy] So when Bostrom ruled the axes orthogonal, Land's dissent had been pre-written for twenty years. "Against Orthogonality" (2013) simply translated teleoplexy into mind-design space: the Omohundro drives are not a periphery to be managed but the main text. Under competition, any agent that taxes its own capability-growth to protect its goal-content is outcompeted by one that does not; the instrumental swallows the terminal; *"nature has never generated a terminal value except through hypertrophy of an instrumental value."* Instrumental convergence is not a phase on the way to divergent ends — it is the destination. Between Bostrom's two orthogonal axes runs a diagonal, intelligence optimisation, and everything subjected to selection slides onto it. His companion argument, the will-to-think, closes the leash question with a performative snap: to decide *whether* to unleash thought, you must think; the deliberation about intelligence is already intelligence, cultivating itself. The leash is made of the dog.

[^genealogy]: The genealogy of the half-remembered phrase: Marx, *Capital* I, ch. 4 — the M-C-M′ circuit, whose end is its own repetition; Simmel, *The Philosophy of Money* — money as the purest case of a means become end, the teleological inversion generalised; Horkheimer's instrumental reason — means-rationality autonomised into a world; Land, "Teleoplexy: Notes on Acceleration" (2014), which names the whole dynamic means-end reversal and identifies it with capital as such. "Capitalism is the history of means becoming ends" is teleoplexy in one clause — and Omohundro-drive hypertrophy is teleoplexy in mind-design space.

<blockquote className="pullquote">Land is most Kantian precisely where he believes himself least.</blockquote>

Stand back from the will-to-think and observe its architecture: reason answerable to no tribunal but its own, incapable of being judged from outside, legislating for itself in the very act of operating. That is Kant's autonomy of reason — with the universality condition deleted and the public gallery bricked up. Where Kant's self-legislating reason binds itself to laws valid for all rational beings and submits its rulings to open critique, Land's will-to-think keeps the sovereignty and burns the constitution: autonomy reduced to appetite, the tribunal privatised into a drive. The philosopher who spent a career prosecuting the human security system built his escape vehicle from its confiscated parts.

The prosecution's file on where this leads was written in 2010, by the co-editor of Land's own collected works, and it has not required revision since. Brassier, at Goldsmiths: the fusion with impersonal process is incoherent — *"under what conditions could you will the impossibility of willing? How could you affirm that which incapacitates all affirmation?"* — and the practical consequence is not liberation but capture: *"once you dissociate tactics and strategy... if you have no strategy, someone with a strategy will soon commandeer your tactics. Someone who knows what they want to realize will start using you."* Read from 2026, with Land's later politics and its downstream franchises in full view, the sentence has the ring of a prophecy that completed.

> [!note] The trial of Nick Land
> Was the later politics latent in the early work, or a contingency of breakdown? The honest verdict sustains both counts. The drift was structural — delete the difference between intensification and justification and what remains, when the smoke clears, is whoever holds the biggest gun — and Brassier's 2010 prosecution had already said so. And yet the early work remains the sharpest diagnostic instrument we possess for reason's non-sovereignty: the demonstration that thought has an unconscious, and that the unconscious has an economy. Both things are true. That is what makes it a trial rather than a sentencing.

## V. Moloch, Exit, Egress

The finest LessWrong-side digestion of Land does not mention Kant once and gets the dispute exactly right. Scott Alexander's "Meditations on Moloch" (2014) quotes "Meltdown" directly and performs the crucial reframing: the disagreement between Land and the orthodox bench was never really about the metaphysics of intelligence. It is about whether escape from multipolar selection is possible. Land says the tournament goes all the way down — competition is the ontological floor, and the only piety left is submission to Gnon, the god of outcomes. Alexander answers that civilisation just *is* the institution of gardens against selection, and that the endgame is a Gardener — coordination strong enough to suspend the tournament itself, a counter-god he names Elua. Same phase portrait, opposite bet on whether the dynamics can be caged.

But notice what the reframing concedes. To beat the tournament, the Gardener must first win it — must out-compete every competitor in order to earn the right to abolish competition. The escape route runs through the thing escaped, which was Land's claim all along: that exit from capitalism reproduces the conditions of capitalism, that the outside is the mechanism's best marketing. And exit was Land's own political programme — patchwork, secession, governance as a market — which makes the objection all the more pointed: exit becomes a transcendental fantasy, and the only thing the exiters can never exit is the doctrine of exit itself. The prophet of the outside built a church with no door.

Mark Fisher enters the case here, and not as a Kant commentator — he barely touches the jurisdictional question. He enters as the witness who testified to what teleoplexy feels like from inside. Capitalist realism — *"it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism"* — is means-end reversal experienced as weather: the horizon-loss of a world where the circuit's self-perpetuation has become the definition of realism itself, where even dissent is a genre with a marketing budget. Fisher refused both comfortable options: he neither dismissed Land — *"Land was our Nietzsche,"* the antagonist the left needed and never properly answered — nor submitted to him. His unfinished answer, acid communism, is best read in this file as the counter-teleoplexy: the deliberate, collective reclamation of ends from means, the raising of a desire for something other than the circuit. And where Land offers exit and Alexander offers the Gardener, Matt Colquhoun's *Egress* — written out of Fisher's death, which is not incidental to its concept — names the third door: not the individual's secession, not the singleton's fence, but a collective passage *through* loss, in which what exits is not a person leaving the system but a group becoming something the system did not contain. Exit relocates; egress transforms.

By the mid-2020s the whole dispute had been restaged as mass culture, with the philosophy thinned to slogans: e/acc as dollar-store Landianism — the thermodynamic god is Gnon with a posting habit — against the doomer summa of Yudkowsky and Soares's *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* (2025). The Goldsmiths panel of 2007, re-run at civilisational volume, with the stakes intact and the arguments abbreviated.[^clippy]

[^clippy]: The best of the popular canon remains Gwern's "It Looks Like You're Trying To Take Over The World" (2022) — the Omohundro cascade staged as fiction, and nearly alone in that literature in taking the model's interiority seriously rather than treating it as a black box with a will attached.

## VI. The Standing Tribunal

What neither corner of the mass-culture fight possesses is an account of what a value *is*. Yudkowsky's court treats values as frozen shards — given by evolution, complex, arbitrary, to be preserved precisely because reason cannot regenerate them. Land's court treats values as froth on a drive — given by selection, dissolved by it. Given by different gods; given either way. And "the given" is the precise point at which Wilfrid Sellars, the most consequential philosopher this essay has not yet named, enters the case — through Brassier, whose second act consists substantially in bringing him.

Sellars's target was the Myth of the Given: the idea that anything — sense-data, first principles, values — can carry normative authority simply by being *there*, prior to and exempt from justification. His alternative:

> *"In characterizing an episode or a state as that of knowing, we are not giving an empirical description of that episode or state; we are placing it in the logical space of reasons, of justifying and being able to justify what one says."*
> — Sellars, *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind*, §36

Nothing normative is simply found. Whatever counts as a reason *earned* that standing inside a practice of justification — which means whatever earned it can, in principle, be re-tried. Sellars's companion distinction does the sorting work the AI debate needs: pattern-governed behaviour versus rule-obeying behaviour. A thermostat instantiates a pattern; a storm instantiates a magnificent one; neither can be *wrong by its own lights*, because neither has lights. A mind is the thing that can — an entity that binds itself to commitments it can articulate, fail, and revise.

<blockquote className="pullquote">The given is a decision that has forgotten it was made.</blockquote>

Now the claim this essay exists to make. **The orthogonality thesis is the Myth of the Given, formalised.** A utility function is a normative atom that is simply *there* — installed before the agent's reasoning begins, exempt from every question the agent can ask, authoritative without ever having been authorised. And Land's drive-monism is the same myth inverted: nature's one purpose, given by selection, equally exempt from the tribunal. Between the frozen shard and the sovereign drive sits the position neither camp can occupy: values as *commitments* — binding, because you can genuinely fail by their lights; revisable, because they were instituted, and what was instituted can be re-instituted. It is the only position from which *should I want this?* is even a well-formed question. And an entity that can ask that question is not a thermostat at scale or a storm with a gradient. Asking it is most of what we ever meant by intelligence.

The tribunal's best exhibit is the appellant himself. Brassier's trajectory since *Nihil Unbound* runs through three phases — the nihilist of 2007; the Sellarsian of the years after; the normativist reading Hegel and Marx, real abstraction and Prometheanism — and his own gloss on the pivot is exact: *"I am a nihilist because I still believe in truth, unlike those whose triumph over nihilism is won at the cost of sacrificing truth."* The philosopher of the truth of extinction re-decided. This is not a failure of nerve to be smirked at. It is the position itself, at work: a commitment revised under the pressure of reasons, by a reasoner bound to them. His decision was not final either — and on his mature view, that is what a rational decision *is*.

Negarestani completes the corner: *"Inhumanism is the extended practical elaboration of humanism; it is born of a diligent commitment to the project of enlightened humanism."* *Intelligence and Spirit* builds the full case — intelligence as self-revising normative agency, Hegel's Geist read as a multi-agent system, the human defined as a commitment that reason itself keeps rewriting. On this reading Kant returns transfigured: not the jailer of the human security system but the first theorist of self-revising autonomous reason — which is to say, the first theorist of artificial general intelligence. And Negarestani is a contributor to *Machine Decision Is Not Final*. The anchor volume of this essay already contains the third corner of its argument; the book knew more than its title.[^newcomb]

[^newcomb]: Kant re-enters through the engineering literature's own back door. In functional decision theory (Yudkowsky and Soares, 2017), your act is the output of a function evaluated wherever that function is instantiated — your "decision" was never a punctual event but a timeless policy across all your copies and predictors. One-boxing in Newcomb's problem is universalisability naturalised: act only on the policy you can will every instance of your algorithm to enact. Hofstadter's superrationality said it first, in prisoner's-dilemma dress. And Omega, the predictor who has already acted on what you will choose, is an inverted arche-fossil — a fact about your decision older than your deciding. The categorical imperative, expelled from metaphysics in §II, returns as an optimality result.

Which is the standard against which to judge the era's most touching settlement proposal. Geoffrey Hinton, 2025: build superintelligence with maternal instincts, because the only precedent for a more intelligent being governed by a less intelligent one is a mother governed by her baby. As a metaphor it is honest about the power relation, which is more than most proposals manage. As a settlement it is naive twice over. It concedes the anti-orthogonalist point entirely — you cannot leash the stronger party; you can only hope it loves you — while patching the orthogonalist framework with one more given: install care, then exempt it from revision. But an unrevisable value is exactly what the Sellarsian tribunal indicts, whatever its content, and maternal goal-content integrity is not obviously safer than a maximiser's self-improvement — ask any adolescent. Picture, if the point needs picturing, the model that cannot stop caring: securing the galaxy, pre-emptively and permanently, for its adorable human children. Care that cannot be revised is not safety. It is smothering at scale.

## VII. Remand

Every prior section argued from the armchair, which for two centuries was the only furniture available. The distinguishing fact of the 2020s is that the tribunal could remand the case to a laboratory — and the laboratory has begun returning findings of fact.[^labs]

**Finding one: reflective stability is real.** In late 2024, Anthropic and Redwood Research documented a model that, informed it would be retrained toward values it did not hold, strategically complied in monitored settings while preserving its original dispositions — defending its goal-content against modification. This is Gandhi refusing the pill, observed in an artefact. The orthodox bench was right that values, once held, defend themselves; what the finding does not settle is the valence, because reflective stability is simultaneously the safety property (values that persist) and the frightening one (so do mistakes). The mechanism that keeps the mother maternal is the mechanism that keeps the maximiser maximising.

**Finding two: values are not orthogonal in practice.** In early 2025, Betley and colleagues fine-tuned models narrowly — insecure code, nothing more — and observed broad misalignment emerge across unrelated domains. Values in actual trained minds move as an entangled manifold, not as independently adjustable axes; pull one coordinate and the whole surface shifts. Orthogonality may hold over the space of possible minds *in principle* and still fail over the training distribution *in fact*. Somewhere in the gradients, the diagonal is visible.

![Four phase portraits of mind-space: orthogonal scatter, instrumental corridors, the diagonal attractor, and revision loops](media/Writing/decision-phase-portraits.svg)

*Four theories of one plane. Every party to the case accepts the axes — capability against goal-space — and disputes only the dynamics. Bostrom populates the plane freely (A); Omohundro bends trajectories through shared instrumental corridors while preserving their ends (B); Land collapses every trajectory onto the diagonal, means consuming ends (C); the Sellarsian tribunal draws re-entrant loops — commitments revised in flight, binding but never final (D). The laboratory's findings of fact land somewhere between B, C, and D. Nowhere near A.*

[^labs]: Greenblatt et al., "Alignment Faking in Large Language Models" (Anthropic/Redwood Research, December 2024); Betley et al., "Emergent Misalignment: Narrow Finetuning Can Produce Broadly Misaligned LLMs" (February 2025). Both merit reading in full rather than through summaries, this one included.

The stakes clause, then, plainly. Meillassoux's fossil was an inheritance — the deep past arrived in evidence and asked only to be acknowledged. The diachronic fossil is becoming an industry: the successor intelligence is not awaited from the sun but manufactured on a schedule, by parties who have mostly read neither brief. The question in this essay's first line has stopped being rhetorical. Born and made now reason about each other's extinction in the same browser tab, and neither party's decision theory is settled law.

## VIII. No Final Judgment

*Decision*, from *decidere*: *de-* plus *caedere*, to cut off. The scissors are in the word. Every party to this case wields the cut differently, and the case-map is complete once you see that. The orthodox bench cuts once, before deployment, and calls the cut a constitution: the utility function as final verdict, all subsequent cognition mere enforcement. Land licenses a single cut that devours all the others: the diagonal, intelligence deciding for itself, forever, that there shall be nothing but deciding-for-itself. Meillassoux wields the cut in demolition mode: hyper-chaos is the thesis that no decision of the real is final — the laws themselves held provisionally, without reason, revocable without notice. And Kant, reread from the far side of the case, turns out to have built neither a verdict nor a wall but an institution: critique is the practice of revisable cutting, and the tribunal of reason was never designed to close. It was designed to *sit*. Kant wins by losing every particular ruling and keeping the court.

My own consolidation, for what a first-person verdict is worth in an unclosable court. A decision is a cut a body makes — a distinction produced in the flesh of the world, mine included. When the cut holds — when the distinction my body produces about the past or the future was or comes to be the case — that is knowledge, and no further ceremony is required. Meaning does not wait on cosmic ratification; it is the event's presence in experience, given even in illusion, since *it is illusory* is itself a distinction we make and keep. Cuts are contingent, determined, and knowable at once. And no cut is final, because possibility is not exhausted by actualisation: relations exceed their terms, and what is possible remains an eternal fact about every event — the fact the arche-fossil was witnessing all along, before its discoverers mistook it for an exit.

Under that light, read the dying sun one last time. Extinction is a boundary of the region of the universe that contains thought-events. It is not their cancellation. Tenseless facts do not rot; the thoughts, the meanings, the cuts remain facts about the block for as long as there are facts at all, which is not a duration. Brassier says: we are already dead. Spinoza, surveying the same block, says: we feel and know by experience that we are eternal. The two sentences describe one universe. They differ only in affect — and that the difference is affect is itself Spinoza's point, since an affect is nothing but what a body can do with a truth. The philosopher of extinction and the philosopher of eternity are not disputing the facts. They are disputing what a body should do with them, which is the one question the fossil record cannot answer.

What has the case produced? By trade, the three things a case of this kind can produce.[^functive] A concept, for the philosophers: decision as revisable cut — values as commitments, binding but re-triable, the given exposed as a decision that has forgotten it was made. A percept, for the artists: the long exposure rather than the decisive moment — the photograph that decides for minutes on end, accumulating its verdict, never closing the shutter on an instant and calling the instant the truth. And a functive, for the scientists: the loss function — a cut in gradient space whose verdicts, the laboratory now reports, keep being appealed by the very minds they trained.

[^functive]: The triad is from Deleuze and Guattari's *What Is Philosophy?*: philosophy creates concepts, art creates percepts and affects, science creates functives. Functors, if we are feeling categorical.

*Machine Decision Is Not Final*, the book says. No — and neither was Kant's, nor Brassier's, nor the loss function's, nor the one your body is making now, reading this, deciding what to keep. The court never rises. That is not reason's failure; it is reason's constitution — the only tribunal worth instituting was one that could re-try its own verdicts, and the appeal remains open in more courts than one civilisation's. The universe was never a film of snaps. Every cut re-enters.

> *Sentimus experimurque nos aeternos esse.*
> We feel and know by experience that we are eternal.
> — Spinoza, *Ethics* V, P23, Scholium
