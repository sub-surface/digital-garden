<div className="dropcap">

The word *speculation* has two halves, and we have mostly kept the wrong one. One half is the *specula*, the watchtower: a high place from which you look out over country you can't reach, guessing at what moves there. The other is the *speculum*, the mirror: a surface in which you see something, yourself included, at one remove. Both come from *specere*, to look. The watchtower gave us the speculator, the investor and the philosopher who reasons beyond experience. The mirror gave us Paul's glass, *per speculum in aenigmate*, through which we see darkly now and will one day see face to face.

</div>

This essay is about what happens to knowledge when you take both halves seriously at once. It's also about a group of philosophers who, about fifteen years ago, decided to start speculating again, and about what a photographer might learn from them, and against them.

## The watchtower

The School of Speculative Documentary, which Max Pinckers describes as "a fictional school formed by a group of researchers and documentary practitioners", including himself, takes its name from the watchtower half. Pinckers' own gloss is careful:

> Originating from the late sixteenth century Latin *speculat-* 'observed from a vantage point', from the verb *speculari*, from *specula* 'watchtower', from *specere* 'to look', the term 'speculate' means to form a theory or conjecture about a subject without any firm evidence, yet this theory is based on elements derived from reality and is not purely a figment of the imagination. [...] Speculative narration is a construction that resists probability in order to create a new world.  
—Max Pinckers, *Speculative Documentary Photography* (2021)

And the School's manifesto describes the attitude it wants: "a practice based on conjecture rather than knowledge", which "openly embraces perpetual uncertainty, contamination, contestation, befoggedness and messiness in an engagement with, and creation of, multiple and mutable realities". Pinckers adds a lovely military footnote. *Speculative fire* is the tactic of shooting blind at places the enemy might be, to make them reveal themselves: "Like speculative fire, we can only attempt to poke at our own blind spots in order to reveal them."

I like all of this. But notice where it stands. The watchtower is a place of distance. You look out, you conjecture, you fire into the dark and see what fires back. The thing looked at stays over there, and the looker stays up here, safe behind the parapet. Even the blind spots are *ours*; the documentary reveals the photographer's limits, which is a real virtue, but it keeps the knowing pointed one way.

The mirror half never comes up. I think it's the better half, and I'll come back to it.

## The speculative turn

In 2011 Levi Bryant, Nick Srnicek and Graham Harman edited a collection called *The Speculative Turn*, and their introduction is a useful map of what had happened in continental philosophy over the previous few years. Its title is a deliberate provocation:

> [...] we propose 'The Speculative Turn', as a deliberate counterpoint to the now tiresome 'Linguistic Turn'.  
—Bryant, Srnicek & Harman, "Towards a Speculative Philosophy" (2011)

The linguistic turn, roughly, had taught that we never meet the world except through language, discourse, power, text. The speculative turn wanted the world back. The editors are clear that this isn't a return to naive metaphysics:

> [...] 'speculation' [...] recuperates the pre-critical sense of 'speculation' as a concern with the Absolute, while also taking into account the undeniable progress that is due to the labour of critique.

and they name what unites a very mixed group of thinkers: "all of them [...] have begun speculating once more about the nature of reality independently of thought and of humanity more generally."

The whole of [[Writing/Image-Metaphors|What Is an Image?]] lives inside that sentence. If the image is a trace, something that happens in the world whether or not anyone is there to read it, then speculating about images is already speculating about reality independent of thought. A puddle reflects the sun with nobody watching. The question is how to do that speculating without simply climbing the watchtower again.

## Kant in good faith

The speculative realists have a villain, and his name is Kant. It's worth being fair to him first, because the fairness changes the argument.

Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* is, among other things, a long limit set on "speculative cognition", reason's attempt to know things beyond any possible experience. But he never says that what lies beyond experience is unreal. He says the opposite:

> [...] even if we cannot cognize these same objects as things in themselves, we at least must be able to think them as things in themselves. For otherwise there would follow the absurd proposition that there is an appearance without anything that appears.  
—Immanuel Kant, [[Wiki/Texts/Critique of Pure Reason (Kant)|*Critique of Pure Reason*]], Bxxvi (trans. Guyer & Wood)

An appearance without anything that appears would be an image of nothing, and Kant finds the idea absurd. What he denies is that we can *know* the thing that appears, as it is in itself. That's a limit on knowing, not on being. The speculative turn's own editors concede the point:

> While for Kant there remains the possibility of thinking the noumenal (if not knowing it), Hegel absolutizes the correlate [...]

It's a generous admission, and it relocates the problem. The thing the speculative realists are fighting isn't quite Kant. It's what happened when Kant's limit on knowing was quietly converted into a limit on being, so that whatever can't be known stops counting as real. Quentin Meillassoux gave the converted position its name, *correlationism*, "the idea according to which we only ever have access to the correlation between thinking and being, and never to either term considered apart from the other."

Kant also has something to say about images that gets lost in the fight. His illusions are real. Transcendental illusion "does not cease even though it is uncovered", he says, "just as little as the astronomer can prevent the rising moon from appearing larger to him, even when he is not deceived by this illusion" (A297). The moon on the horizon stays large after you know it isn't. That isn't a failure of knowledge. It's the knowledge that an appearance is a real event with its own causes, which you can understand without being able to switch it off. Spinoza says the same of the sun, which we imagine to be two hundred feet away and go on imagining so after we know better. Neither of them thinks the illusion is a veil over the real. It is part of the real, behaving as it does.

## The arche-fossil

Meillassoux's argument against correlationism starts from a particular kind of evidence, which he calls the *arche-fossil*:

> [...] materials indicating the existence of an ancestral reality or event; one that is anterior to terrestrial life. [...] an isotope whose rate of radioactive decay we know, or the luminous emission of a star that informs us as to the date of its formation.  
—Quentin Meillassoux, *After Finitude* (2006, trans. Ray Brassier)

Science makes claims about events before there was anyone to experience them: the accretion of the earth, the formation of a star. The correlationist has to add a quiet rider to every such claim, that it's true *for us*. Meillassoux thinks the rider destroys the claim's meaning, because the whole point of the statement is that the event happened with nobody there. He defines the arche-fossil as "givenness of a being anterior to givenness".

Look at his second example. Starlight that tells you when the star formed is an image in the strongest sense this essay's parent has been trying to defend: a trace that something leaves in the world, which can be read long after, and which was not made *for* anyone. The arche-fossil is a photograph that took itself. Every photograph is a small one: light from a moment that has gone, arriving at a moment that had no part in making it.

This is where I think the photograph is a better test case for correlationism than the fossil. The fossil is ancient and its source is far away; the photograph is ordinary, and its source was often right in front of you. Barthes calls it "the absolute Particular, the sovereign Contingency, matte and somehow stupid, the *This*". The stupidity is the point. The photograph doesn't know what it shows. It shows it anyway.

## The sun

Ray Brassier, who translated *After Finitude*, takes the argument somewhere darker. In *Nihil Unbound* he follows Lyotard's late essay "Can Thought go on Without a Body?", which begins from the fact that the sun will one day die and take every earthly thought with it. Lyotard's line is that "the death of the sun is a death of mind, because it is the death of death as the life of the mind". Brassier refuses to treat this as a distant event:

> [...] the solar catastrophe needs to be grasped as something that has already happened; as the aboriginal trauma driving the history of terrestrial life as an elaborately circuitous detour from stellar death. [...] This mark imprinted upon thought by its relation to the sun is the trace of stellar death, which precedes and succeeds, initiates and terminates, the life and death with which philosophers reckon.  
—Ray Brassier, *Nihil Unbound: Enlightenment and Extinction* (2007), ch. 7

Here the old solar metaphor of knowledge, Plato's sun that makes things visible, is inverted twice. The sun isn't what lets us see; it's what will end all seeing. And thought doesn't picture that end; it *bears its mark*. Brassier calls the relation between them "adequation without correspondence": knowledge as a mark made equal to what made it, rather than a picture that matches its object.

That's very close to what I've been calling an image, and I want to be honest that our routes diverge only at the end. Brassier's conclusion is that the subject of philosophy "must also recognize that he or she is already dead, and that philosophy is neither a medium of affirmation nor a source of justification, but rather the organon of extinction."

I can't follow him there, and not only because this garden is in the affirmation business. The trace is the strongest thing we have. A star's light arriving after the star has gone doesn't prove that nothing matters; it proves that what happened stays happened. A photograph of someone who has died doesn't announce their death so much as refuse to let their having lived be undone. Brassier is right that the trace is indifferent to us. It doesn't follow that it's a death sentence. The light has already reached us, whatever becomes of its source. (I've argued with Brassier and Meillassoux at more length in [[Writing/Decision Was Never Final|Decision Was Never Final]].)

## The noumenon's new clothes

The speculative turn has its own image problem, and Peter Wolfendale named it. In "The Noumenon's New Clothes" (*Speculations* III, 2012), he takes apart Graham Harman's object-oriented ontology, in which every real object "withdraws" from every relation, so that nothing ever touches anything else directly. Wolfendale's charge is that Harman doesn't escape Kant; he "revives and transforms Kant's noumenal realm", and dresses it as an exotic place you can visit but never enter:

> A world in which you can travel as much as you like, but you'll always be a tourist. No matter how hard you try, you'll never see the real island, only beaches full of German holidaymakers and chintzy gift shops. You might get the occasional taste of it—a wiff of the exotic food the real islanders eat as you pass by, or a stolen glimpse of the real lives of the inhabitants over a whitewashed wall—but that's all you'll ever get.  
—Peter Wolfendale, "The Noumenon's New Clothes (Part 1)" (2012)

The parody is affectionate and devastating, and it lands on something an essay about images ought to fear. Harman's arguments, Wolfendale says, tend to be dramatisations rather than demonstrations, and the drama does the work:

> This has a peculiar effect that can best be described as performative phenomenology. The revelation of invisibility is an artefact of the way in which it is introduced. [...] The supposed demonstration of epistemic inaccessibility is actually an elaborate numbing of our epistemic faculties, performed by multiplying the incompatible aspects of the mysterious withdrawn tool. Single hands don't clap after all.

In other words, the withdrawn object is staged as an image you are never allowed to see. Harman's own theory of metaphor fits the pattern. For him a metaphor works by *allure*: comparing a tree to a flame makes the tree's familiar qualities strange, and through that strangeness a withdrawn reality slides obliquely into appearance. Metaphor is precious, on this view, because the literal never reaches the real.

The parent essay argues the opposite, that metaphor is always literal: "I see what you mean" reports a real composition between the eye's practice and the mind's, not a figure for something that can't be said. So Wolfendale is an ally here. But his warning applies to us as much as to Harman. *What Is an Image?* is full of drama: suns in puddles, an ox's eye on a windowsill, Thomas's finger in the wound. Every one of those images has to carry an argument, or it is doing exactly what Wolfendale describes, numbing the reader into agreement with a beautiful picture. His own ambition, he says, is to draw the arguments "out of hiding and expose them to the light of reason". The solar metaphor again. Nobody gets out of it; the question is only whether you use it to show something or to hide it.

## The mirror

Back to the half of the word that the School of Speculative Documentary leaves out.

The mirror isn't a better watchtower. It doesn't let you see further. What it does is put you in the picture. You can't look into a mirror without being looked back at, and you can't see what's behind you in it without seeing yourself first. Paul's promise, read in full, isn't only that we'll see clearly: "now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known." Knowledge, in the mirror half of speculation, runs both ways.

Photography has always half-known this. Susan Sontag worried that to photograph people is "to violate them, by seeing them as they never see themselves, by having knowledge of them they can never have" ([[Books/On Photography|On Photography]], 1977). That's the watchtower: knowledge taken from a height, without return. But a trace isn't a one-way thing. The light that marks a sensor has first touched the person in front of it, and the person behind the camera is standing in the same light. Everyone with a phone is now on both sides of that exchange many times a day; the photographer is no longer a special case, just the most self-conscious one.

A speculative documentary that took the mirror half seriously would keep everything Pinckers asks for: conjecture, contamination, multiple and mutable realities. It wouldn't need to be any less uncertain. It would just stop pretending that the uncertainty belongs only to the subject, or only to the photographer. And it would stop pretending that its constructions float free of what they're constructed from. The arche-fossil, the delayed starlight and the photograph all say the same thing: there was something there, and it left this. Speculation, in the mirror sense, is reasoning from the mark you're already carrying.

## Coda

The speculative turn wanted thought to touch reality again, independently of us. The image was always the evidence that it could, and the evidence that it didn't need us to. The watchtower looks out at what it can't reach. The mirror shows that we were reached already.

I don't think we have to choose between them. The watchtower is where you go to guess; the mirror is where you go to find out what the guessing has done to you. Kant's moon stays large on the horizon, Spinoza's sun stays in the water, and the starlight keeps arriving from wherever it left. None of that is a veil. It's what being in the world, among its traces, looks like.
