# Critique of Pure Reason

> **Immanuel Kant**, *Kritik der reinen Vernunft* (First Edition [A], 1781; Second Edition [B], 1787)  
> Standard academic translation: Paul Guyer and Allen W. Wood, *The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant* (1998)[^kant-cpr-ed].

<div className="dropcap">

Immanuel Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (*Kritik der reinen Vernunft*, 1781/1787) stands as the watershed dividing early modern philosophy from contemporary thought. Prompted by David Hume's skeptical dismantling of induction and causation—which Kant famously credited with "interrupting my dogmatic slumber"—the *Critique* investigates the very possibility, scope, and objective limits of human cognition. Its decisive thesis, the **Copernican Revolution**, reverses the classical realism of knowledge: instead of our cognition conforming to objects as they exist in themselves, *objects as phenomena must conform to the synthetic, constitutive structures of human cognition*.

</div>

---

## I. Foundational Framework: The Synthetic *A Priori*

Kant establishes a two-dimensional taxonomy of cognitive judgments:

| | **Analytic** (*Explicative*) | **Synthetic** (*Ampliative*) |
|---|---|---|
| **A Priori** (*Independent of experience; universal and necessary*) | "All bachelors are unmarried." (Predicate contained in subject concept; verified via Non-Contradiction) | **The Central Problem of Epistemology**: "Every alteration has a cause." "7 + 5 = 12." Geometry, pure physics |
| **A Posteriori** (*Contingent upon sensory empirical experience*) | *Logically impossible* (A judgment whose truth requires experience cannot be purely conceptual) | "This body is heavy." Empirical observations, contingent historical facts |

### The Cardinal Question of the Critique
$$\text{"How are synthetic } a \text{ priori judgments possible?"} \quad (B19)$$

If pure mathematics and pure natural science formulate judgments that are both non-analytic (ampliative) and yet strictly necessary and universal (*a priori*), what cognitive mechanisms make them objectively valid for all empirical reality?

## II. Transcendental Aesthetic: The Pure Forms of Sensibility

The *Aesthetic* (from Greek *aisthesis*, sensation) investigates the receptive faculty of **Sensibility** (*Sinnlichkeit*), through which objects are given to the mind as sensible intuitions (*Anschauungen*):

$$\text{"Thoughts without content are empty; intuitions without concepts are blind."} \quad (A51 / B75)$$

Kant proves that Space and Time are neither:
1. Mind-independent physical substances or containers (the Newtonian absolutist thesis), nor
2. Relational abstractions derived from the comparison of physical bodies (the Leibnizian relational thesis).

### The Aesthetic Deductions
* **Space** is the *a priori* form of all **outer sense** (*äußerer Sinn*): the singular subjective condition under which external perception is possible. Pure Euclidean geometry is synthetic *a priori* because it articulates the structural properties of this pure form of spatial intuition.
* **Time** is the *a priori* form of all **inner sense** (*innerer Sinn*): the condition of all representations whatsoever (including thoughts, mental states, and successive outer impressions). Arithmetic (the successive addition of units in time) is grounded in this pure temporal intuition.

**Consequence: Transcendental Idealism**: Space and time have *empirical reality* (they are objectively valid for every appearance that can ever appear to us) but *transcendental ideality* (they are nothing when abstracted from the subjective conditions of human sensibility).

## III. Transcendental Analytic: The Categories of the Understanding

While Sensibility *receives* intuitions, the **Understanding** (*Verstand*) *thinks* objects through concepts. The *Transcendental Analytic* isolates the pure elementary concepts of the understanding (**The Categories**).

### 1. The Clue to the Discovery of all Pure Concepts (Metaphysical Deduction)

Kant derives the twelve Categories systematically from the traditional table of logical forms of judgment:

| Domain | Forms of Judgment | Pure Concepts of Understanding (Categories) |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| **Quantity** | Universal, Particular, Singular | **Unity, Plurality, Totality** |
| **Quality** | Affirmative, Negative, Infinite | **Reality, Negation, Limitation** |
| **Relation** | Categorical, Hypothetical, Disjunctive | **Inherence & Subsistence (Substance), Causality & Dependence, Community (Reciprocity)** |
| **Modality** | Problematic, Assertoric, Apodictic | **Possibility/Impossibility, Existence/Non-existence, Necessity/Contingency** |

### 2. The Transcendental Deduction of the Categories (B-Deduction)

The *Transcendental Deduction* is the argumentative core of the *Critique*. Kant demonstrates why categories derived from pure logic have legitimate, necessary jurisdiction over empirical sensory objects:

1. **The Transcendental Unity of Apperception**:  
   $$\text{"The 'I think' must be able to accompany all my representations."} \quad (B131)$$  
   A manifold of sensory impressions can only constitute conscious experience for a single unified subject if that subject can synthesize them into an objective unity.
2. **Synthesis of the Manifold**: Sensation delivers an unstructured stream of disparate raw data. The mind synthesizes this manifold through three stages: apprehension in intuition, reproduction in imagination, and recognition in a concept.
3. **Objective Validity**: An "object of experience" is precisely that in the concept of which a manifold of intuition is united. Because the Categories are the rules of this conceptual synthesis, *no experience is possible except under the governance of the Categories*.

### 3. The Schematism and Analogies of Experience

* **The Schematism**: Categories (purely intellectual) and intuitions (sensible) are completely heterogeneous. How can a Category apply to an intuition? Kant introduces the **Transcendental Schema**: a pure temporal determination produced by the imagination (e.g., the schema of Cause is *regular succession in time according to a rule*).
* **The Second Analogy of Experience**: Kant's refutation of Hume. We can only distinguish an objective sequence of events (a ship sailing downstream) from a subjective sequence of perceptions (looking at the roof then the basement of a house) because we presuppose an *a priori* causal rule:  
  $$\text{"All alterations occur in accordance with the law of the connection of cause and effect."} \quad (B232)$$

## IV. Phenomena versus Noumena

Kant's synthesis establishes a decisive epistemic boundary:

* **Phenomena (Appearances, *Erscheinungen*)**: Objects as they appear to human cognitive faculties, structured by space, time, and the twelve categories. Empirical science and synthetic *a priori* knowledge are completely valid within this phenomenal domain.
* **Noumena (Things-in-themselves, *Dinge an sich*)**: Reality as it exists independently of human cognitive apparatus. The concept of a noumenon is strictly a **limiting concept** (*Grenzbegriff*, A255/B311): we know *that* things-in-themselves exist (as the ground of appearances), but we can have zero theoretical cognition of what they are like, because human understanding cannot operate without sensible intuition.

## V. Transcendental Dialectic: The Critique of Pure Metaphysics

The *Transcendental Dialectic* diagnoses the inevitable cognitive illusions that arise when pure Reason (*Vernunft*) attempts to apply the categories beyond the bounds of sensible experience to comprehend the **Unconditioned** (*das Unbedingte*):

```
                       THE THREE TRANSCENDENTAL IDEAS
                         ┌────────────────────────┐
                         │    THE UNCONDITIONED   │
                         └───────────┬────────────┘
               ┌─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐
               │                     │                     │
      PSYCHOLOGICAL IDEA     COSMOLOGICAL IDEA      THEOLOGICAL IDEA
      The Thinking Subject       The World-Whole         The Being of Beings
         (The Soul)                (Cosmos)                    (God)
               │                     │                     │
               ▼                     ▼                     ▼
         PARALOGISMS             ANTINOMIES             THE IDEAL
      Critique of Rational   Critique of Rational  Critique of Ontological/
          Psychology               Cosmology        Cosmological Proofs
```

### 1. The Paralogisms: The Illusion of the Substantial Soul
Rational psychologists (such as Descartes) infer that because the "I think" is the formal subject of all thought, the soul must be a simple, immaterial, immortal substance. Kant shows this is a formal fallacy (*subreptio*): one confuses the formal, logical condition of unity (apperception) with an intuition of an underlying metaphysical object.

### 2. The Four Antinomies of Pure Reason
When reason attempts to conceive the cosmos as a completed totality, it falls into four intractable contradictions where both thesis and antithesis can be proven with equal geometric rigor:

| Antinomy | Thesis (Rationalist / Dogmatic) | Antithesis (Empiricist / Skeptical) | Resolution |
|----------|---------------------------------|-------------------------------------|------------|
| **1. Composition** | The world has a beginning in time and a spatial boundary | The world is infinite in time and space | Both False (World-in-itself is not a given empirical totality) |
| **2. Division** | Composite substances consist of simple indivisible parts (atoms) | No composite thing consists of simple parts; all is infinitely divisible | Both False |
| **3. Freedom & Nature** | Causality according to nature is not the only causality; **Transcendental Freedom** is necessary | There is no freedom; everything happens solely according to laws of nature | **Both True**: Determinism governs phenomena; Freedom is thinkable in the noumenal realm |
| **4. Necessary Being** | There exists an absolutely necessary being as part or cause of the world | There exists no absolutely necessary being anywhere | **Both True**: In the phenomenal world, all is contingent; noumenally, a necessary ground is possible |

The resolution of the Third Antinomy rescues moral philosophy: while human action viewed empirically as a phenomenon in time is 100% causally determined, the moral agent considered noumenally possesses transcendental autonomy, grounding the [[Deontology|Categorical Imperative]].

### 3. The Ideal of Pure Reason: The Impossibility of Speculative Proofs of God
Kant systematically refutes all three traditional speculative proofs for God's existence:
1. **The Ontological Argument** (Descartes, Anselm): Refuted because **"Existence is not a real predicate"** (*Sein ist offenbar kein reales Prädikat*, A598/B626). Adding "exists" to a concept adds no conceptual content; it merely posits an instance in reality.
2. **The Cosmological Argument** (The First Cause): Dependent on the category of causality, which is valid only within experience, and ultimately collapses back into the flawed ontological argument.
3. **The Physico-Theological (Teleological) Argument** (The Argument from Design): Demonstrates at best a cosmic architect working with pre-existing matter, not an infinite creator.
