# The Myth of Sisyphus

> **Albert Camus**, *Le Mythe de Sisyphe: Essai sur l'absurde* (1942)  
> Standard translations: Justin O'Brien (Hamish Hamilton, 1955; Vintage Books, 1991)[^camus-ms-ref].

<div className="dropcap">

Written in Paris amidst the bleak humiliation of the Nazi occupation of France, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (*Le Mythe de Sisyphe*) is Albert Camus's foundational philosophical statement. Its opening sentence establishes the gravity of its inquiry: *"There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide."* Across four tightly constructed essays, Camus inquires whether the recognition that human life lacks transcendent purpose, divine sanction, or ultimate rational intelligibility makes self-inflicted death necessary. By systematically dismantling physical suicide and religious-metaphysical evasion ("philosophical suicide"), Camus establishes a radical philosophy of **lucid defiance**, crowning his thesis with the immortal image of Sisyphus smiling at his rock.

</div>

## 1. Overview & Historical Context

Camus drafted *The Myth of Sisyphus* concurrently with his novel *The Stranger* (*L'Étranger*) and his play *Caligula*, forming a complementary trilogy exploring the emergence of the absurd across philosophy, fiction, and theater. 

The immediate intellectual rival of the text was the growing wave of existential philosophy in Europe: on one flank, the religious existentialists ([[Søren Kierkegaard|Kierkegaard]], Lev Shestov, Karl Jaspers); on the other, the phenomenological ontology of [[Edmund Husserl|Husserl]] and [[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]]. Camus recognized their profound diagnostic power in exposing the collapse of classical rationalism, but vehemently attacked their eventual retreat into transcendent faith or hypostatized abstractions. *Sisyphus* was designed as a defense of classical Mediterranean lucidity against all mysticism.

## 2. Structural Architecture

The treatise is organized into four core parts, followed by an appendix on Franz Kafka:

```
                            THE MYTH OF SISYPHUS
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ PART I: AN ABSURD REASONING                                                 │
│ • Absurdity and Suicide                                                     │
│ • Absurd Walls (The feeling of absurdity in daily routine)                  │
│ • Philosophical Suicide (Critique of Kierkegaard, Shestov, Jaspers, Husserl)│
│ • Absurd Freedom (Revolt, Freedom, Passion)                                 │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ PART II: THE ABSURD MAN                                                     │
│ • Don Juanism (Exhaustion of love without transcendence)                    │
│ • Drama (The Actor living multiple ephemeral lives)                         │
│ • Conquest (The Political actor choosing immediate struggle)                │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ PART III: ABSURD CREATION                                                   │
│ • Philosophy and Fiction (Art as supreme rebellion without redemption)      │
│ • Kirilov (Dostoevsky's logical suicide transformed into divine freedom)    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ PART IV: THE MYTH OF SISYPHUS                                               │
│ • The Legend and Punishment of the Titan                                    │
│ • The Descent (Consciousness, Scorn, Happiness)                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 3. Core Axioms & Deductions

Camus constructs his argument through a series of logical progressions:

1. **Axiom of the Encounter**: The absurd does not reside in man alone, nor in the world alone, but in their tragic juxtaposition: the confrontation between the human appetite for clarity and unity, and the irrational, stubborn silence of an indifferent universe.
2. **The Condition of Lucidity**: The value of life is inextricably bound to maintaining clear, unevaded conscious awareness (*lucidité*).
3. **The Deduction of Non-Suicide**: Physical suicide destroys the human subject. By eliminating one of the two terms of the collision, suicide cancels the absurd rather than resolving it. Suicide is capitulation, an admission that life has overcome man.
4. **The Rejection of Philosophical Suicide**: Positing an afterlife, divine mystery, or absolute historical destiny to compensate for meaninglessness requires an irrational leap of faith. This amputates reason to preserve comfort.
5. **The Three Consequences**: To live authentically in the absurd without escape mandates three commitments:
   * **Revolt**: Continuous defiance of fate, keeping the contradiction permanently alive.
   * **Freedom**: Living entirely in the present moment without teleological baggage.
   * **Passion**: Maximizing the quantity and intensity of conscious experiences (*"What counts is not the best living but the most living"*).

## 4. Key Arguments & Landmark Formulations

### 1. The Absurd Walls
Camus observes that the feeling of the absurd can strike anyone at any street corner:
* **The Mechanical Routine**: *"Rising, tram, four hours in the office or factory, meal, tram, four hours of work, meal, sleep, and Monday Tuesday Wednesday Thursday Friday and Saturday according to the same rhythm..."* One day, the question **"Why?"** arises. Weariness heralds the awakening of consciousness.
* **The Strangeness of Nature**: Looking at a landscape and realizing that nature is radically inhuman and alien, stripped of all sentimental projection.
* **The Body as Stranger**: Glancing into a mirror and seeing an unfamiliar physical animal staring back.
* **The Horror of Time**: Realizing that time is not a ladder to the future, but our deadliest enemy, leading inexorably to the physical dissolution of the grave.

### 2. The Indictment of Philosophical Suicide
Camus methodically cross-examines rival existentialists:
* **Kierkegaard**: Having diagnosed the absurd with peerless brilliance, Kierkegaard panics and makes the "leap of faith" (*Springet*), demanding that reason embrace the divine scandal. Camus calls this intellectual deicide.
* **Husserl**: Begins with the strict phenomenological description of phenomena, but ends by elevating subjective meanings into eternal metaphysical essences.
Camus insists: *"I want to know whether I can live with what I know, and with that only."*

### 3. Sisyphus at the Foot of the Mountain
In the climactic essay, Camus turns to Homeric myth. Sisyphus, having stolen the secrets of the gods and chained Death itself, is sentenced to push a colossal rock to the peak of a mountain, only to see it instantly tumble back into the abyss.

Camus directs our attention to **the pause at the summit**—the moment Sisyphus turns and walks slowly down the slope to retrieve the stone:
* This hour of descent is the hour of **consciousness**. If each step was sustained by the hope of eventual success, Sisyphus would be tragic.
* Instead, Sisyphus knows the full extent of his wretched condition. His consciousness transforms torment into victory. His rock is his thing, his fate belongs to him, and his defiance silences the gods:
> *"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."* (*Il faut imaginer Sisyphe heureux.*)

## 5. Aporias, Paradoxes & Internal Tensions

* **The Quantitative Ethic vs. Moral Evaluation**: Camus famously writes that what matters is the *quantity* of experiences, not the *quality*. Critics (including Jean-Paul Sartre) noted that this offers no normative compass: if meaning is illusory, on what basis can one condemn the actions of a tyrant or a murderer over those of a physician? Camus was forced to resolve this tension later in *The Rebel* by grounding ethics in mutual solidarity.
* **The Paradox of Happiness**: If the condition of existence is fundamentally fractured and irrational, how can consciousness achieve genuine "happiness" rather than bitter resignation? Camus asserts Sisyphus's joy by aesthetic fiat rather than formal deduction.

---

## 6. Critical Objections & Historical Reception

1. **Sartre's Critique**: While admiring the literary power of the work, Sartre observed that Camus was not a systematic metaphysician, but a classical French moralist in the tradition of Montaigne and Pascal. Sartre argued that Camus failed to understand Hegelian dialectics and treated human history as an external natural landscape rather than human praxis.
2. **Theological Objections**: Christian thinkers (such as Jacques Maritain and Gabriel Marcel) contended that Camus's depiction of the "silence of God" was dogmatic and ignored the experiential reality of divine grace and relational encounter.
