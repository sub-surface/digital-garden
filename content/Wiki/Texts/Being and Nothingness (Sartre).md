# Being and Nothingness

> **Jean-Paul Sartre**, *L'Être et le Néant: Essai d'ontologie phénoménologique* (1943)  
> Standard translations: Hazel E. Barnes (Philosophical Library, 1956; Washington Square Press, 1993); Sarah Richmond (Routledge, 2018)[^sartre-bn-ref].

<div className="dropcap">

Published by Gallimard in 1943 in Nazi-occupied Paris, *Being and Nothingness* (*L'Être et le Néant*) is the monumental foundational text of 20th-century French existential phenomenology. Subtitled *An Essay on Phenomenological Ontology*, the treatise draws upon the methodologies of [[Edmund Husserl]] and [[Martin Heidegger]] while decisively transforming them into a dramatic anatomy of human consciousness, freedom, alienation, and desire. Across more than seven hundred pages, Sartre demonstrates that consciousness is not an inert physical substance, but a dynamic, self-surpassing "hole of nothingness" at the heart of reality—an entity that is radically condemned to choose its own being.

</div>

---

## 1. Overview & Historical Context

Drafted while Sartre was teaching lycée in Le Havre and Paris following his release from a German prisoner-of-war camp, *Being and Nothingness* emerged out of Sartre's intense engagement with German philosophy:
* From **Husserl**, Sartre took the intentionality of consciousness: that consciousness is always consciousness *of* something, and has no interior contents.
* From **Heidegger's** *Being and Time* (1927), Sartre took the fundamental analytic of human existence and the priority of *Dasein*, while stripping Heidegger's thought of its historical-poetic destiny in favor of radical Cartesian subjectivity.
* In polemical opposition to **Spinoza, Leibniz, and materialist determinism**, Sartre sought to demonstrate that human action cannot be explained by prior causal mechanisms: consciousness is an uncaused fissure in the causal chain of the world.

## 3. Core Axioms & Ontological Deductions

### 1. The Dual Ontological Spheres
Sartre begins by dividing reality into two irreducible domains:
* **Being-in-itself (*L'en-soi*)**: Non-conscious, solid, opaque matter. It is completely self-identical, uncreated, without reason, and superfluous (*de trop*). It is what it is.
* **Being-for-itself (*Le pour-soi*)**: Consciousness. Consciousness is not an object among objects; it is an intentional relation that is perpetually detached from itself.

### 2. The Origin of Nothingness (*Le Néant*)
How does non-being or absence enter the world?
* Non-being cannot come from the in-itself, which is pure, undifferentiated positive fullness.
* Non-being enters the world **through human consciousness**. Consciousness possesses the capacity to "nihilate" (*néantiser*)—to introduce lack, absence, and question into the world.
* **Pierre in the Café**: Sartre enters a café to meet his friend Pierre. The café is filled with patrons, tables, and sounds. Yet for Sartre, the café is organized around a single, acute absence: *Pierre is not here*. This negative judgment is not an illusion; it is an objective phenomenological discovery made possible by the nihilation of the For-itself.[^sartre-bn-ref]

### 3. Freedom as Ontological Void
Because consciousness is not a thing, it has no fixed nature:
$$\text{"The essence of Dasein lies in its existence."}$$
A chasm of nothingness separates the past self from the present moment. Past habits, biological drives, and social stations exert zero mechanical force upon the next decision. Human beings are ungrounded freedom, forced to create their own essence through action.

## 4. Key Arguments & Landmark Formulations

### 1. Bad Faith (*Mauvaise Foi*)
To escape the terror of anguish (*l'angoisse*), consciousness attempts to deceive itself:
* A human being pretends to be an in-itself—an object governed by fixed laws.
* Sartre analyzes the celebrated case of the **café waiter** who moves with exaggerated, mechanical stiffness. The waiter is play-acting his role to escape the terrifying truth that being a waiter is not an inherent nature, but a free choice renewed each second.

### 2. The Look (*Le Regard*) and the Keyhole
In Part III, Sartre presents his decisive thought experiment demonstrating the reality of the Other:
1. Imagine an individual crouching in a hotel hallway, peering through a keyhole out of jealousy. The subject is completely absorbed in their action: they *are* their gaze; there is no reflective self-consciousness.
2. Suddenly, footsteps echo behind them. **Someone is watching.**
3. In a flash, the subject is pierced by **The Look** (*Le regard*):
   * The subject suddenly experiences themselves as an **object**—a shameful, caught peeping-tom frozen in the consciousness of another.
   * Solipsism is refuted: the existence of the Other is not an abstract logical deduction, but an immediate affective reality experienced as **shame**.
   * My sovereignty is decentered; another person has captured my possibilities and rendered me a thing within their horizon.

```
                         THE DYNAMICS OF THE LOOK
        SUBJECT (Sovereign)                    THE OTHER (Watcher)
   ┌──────────────────────────┐             ┌──────────────────────────┐
   │ Autonomous consciousness │   THE LOOK  │   Piercing gaze enters   │
   │  Centers world on itself ├────────────►│Freezes subject into object│
   └────────────┬─────────────┘             └────────────┬─────────────┘
                │                                        │

                                    ▼

                        ("Hell is other people")
```

### 3. Existential Psychoanalysis
In the final part, Sartre rejects Freudian psychoanalysis (which traces behavior back to unconscious libidinal complexes) in favor of **Existential Psychoanalysis**:
* Human choices are not symptoms of a hidden biological past; they are expressions of an overarching **fundamental project** chosen by the individual.
* The ultimate goal of human consciousness is the impossible project of becoming the **In-itself-For-itself** (*l'en-soi-pour-soi*)—a being that possesses the conscious freedom of the For-itself with the solid, eternal security of the In-itself (the traditional definition of God). Because this synthesis is contradictory, Sartre famously concludes:
  $$\text{"Man is a useless passion."} \quad (\text{BN } 708)$$

## 5. Aporias, Paradoxes & Internal Tensions

1. **The Paradox of Bad Faith**: How can a singular, transparent consciousness deceive itself without splitting into an unconscious censor? Critics argue that Sartre's insistence on absolute mental transparency makes bad faith logically impossible.
2. **The Missing Ethics**: The final page of the book promises a forthcoming work dedicated to an existentialist ethics: *"We shall devote a future work to these questions."* Sartre never completed this treatise, because his pure ontology of radical solipsism provided no foundation for non-contradictory moral obligations toward others.

---

## 6. Critical Objections & Historical Reception

* **Maurice Merleau-Ponty**: In *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945), Merleau-Ponty argued that Sartre's freedom was too absolute. Human agency is never an unencumbered void; it is always anchored in the **lived body** and habitual motor intentionality.
* **Simone de Beauvoir**: Expanded and corrected Sartre in *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947), demonstrating that individual freedom can only achieve genuine realization when it actively wills the liberation of other people's freedom.
* **Structuralism & Foucault**: In the 1960s, Foucault and Lévi-Strauss attacked Sartre's sovereign subject, proving that human language and thought are dictated by underlying cultural and economic codes rather than individual existential choice.
