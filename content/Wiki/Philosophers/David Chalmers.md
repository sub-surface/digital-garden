## Biography Summary

<div className="dropcap">

David Chalmers is an Australian philosopher and cognitive scientist recognized as one of the leading figures in contemporary philosophy of mind. Originally trained in pure mathematics at the University of Adelaide and the University of Oxford, Chalmers transitioned to cognitive science, completing his doctorate at Indiana University under Douglas Hofstadter. In 1994, at the inaugural *Tucson Consciousness Conference*, Chalmers delivered a landmark lecture distinguishing between the "easy problems" and the **Hard Problem of consciousness**, catapulting subjective phenomenal experience (*qualia*) back to the center of philosophy and neuroscience. He currently serves as University Professor of Philosophy and Neural Science at New York University and co-directs the PhilPapers Foundation with David Bourget.

</div>

## Key Philosophical Contributions

### 1. [[The Hard Problem of Consciousness]]
Chalmers drew a foundational distinction that defined modern cognitive science:
* **The Easy Problems**: Explaining cognitive functions, mechanisms, and behaviors (sensory discrimination, motor control, verbal reportability, attention). These can be resolved by cognitive psychology and neurobiology.
* **The Hard Problem**: Explaining why cognitive processing is accompanied by an inner, qualitative, subjective experience. Why does information processing in the visual cortex feel like anything at all?

### 2. Philosophical Zombies and Naturalistic Dualism
In *The Conscious Mind* (1996), Chalmers formulated the **Zombie Argument** against physicalism:
1. It is logically conceivable that there could be a **Philosophical Zombie**—an exact physical, functional, and behavioral replica of a human being that possesses zero subjective experience.
2. If zombies are metaphysically conceivable, then consciousness does not logically supervene on physical properties alone.
3. Therefore: **Physicalism is false.**

Chalmers defended **Naturalistic Dualism**: consciousness is an irreducible, fundamental feature of nature (analogous to mass, charge, or spacetime) governed by fundamental psychophysical bridging laws.

### 3. The Extended Mind Thesis (with Andy Clark)
In their seminal 1998 paper *"The Extended Mind"*, Chalmers and Andy Clark challenged intracranialism (the view that the mind stops at the skull):
* If an external physical artifact (such as a notebook, smartphone, or digital garden) performs a function that would unhesitatingly be considered cognitive if performed inside the head, then that artifact is literally part of the cognitive system.
* Illustrated through the famous thought experiment of **Otto and Inga**: Otto's notebook containing addresses functions as his biological memory.

### 4. Virtual Realism (*Reality+*, 2022)
Examining simulation arguments and immersive digital technologies, Chalmers argues that virtual realities are not illusions:
* Virtual objects (code, 3D polygons, simulated physics) are genuinely real digital entities.
* Life in a simulated virtual world can be just as authentic, meaningful, and valuable as life in physical reality.

## Major Works

1. *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory* (1996) — Formulation of the Hard Problem and naturalistic dualism.
2. *"The Extended Mind"* (with Andy Clark, 1998) — The active externalism thesis in cognitive science.
3. *Constructing the World* (2012) — Systematic epistemological project following Carnap's Aufbau.
4. *Reality+: Virtual Worlds and the Problems of Philosophy* (2022) — Epistemology and metaphysics of virtual reality.
