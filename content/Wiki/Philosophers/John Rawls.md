# John Rawls

<div className="dropcap">

**John Rawls** (1921–2002) was an American moral and political philosopher whose magnum opus, *A Theory of Justice* (1971)[^rawls-tj-ed], transformed twentieth-century Anglo-American normative political philosophy. Widely regarded as the most influential political theorist of the twentieth century, Rawls rescued normative political theory from the perceived dead-ends of logical positivism and utilitarian hegemony.

</div>

Rawls reconstructed the classical social contract tradition of Locke, Rousseau, and [[Immanuel Kant]], transposing it into a rigorous decision-theoretic model to defend a robust egalitarian liberalism known as **Justice as Fairness** (*justice as fairness*).

[^rawls-tj-ed]: John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 1971; rev. ed. 1999). See [[Bibliography#Rawls-TJ-1971]].

---

## 1. The Subject of Justice: The Basic Structure

Rawls famously begins *A Theory of Justice* by asserting the primacy of justice among political virtues:

> "Justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought. A theory however elegant and economical must be rejected or revised if it is untrue; likewise laws and institutions no matter how efficient and well-arranged must be reformed or abolished if they are unjust." (*TJ*, §1, p. 3)

For Rawls, the primary subject of justice is not the moral behavior of individual agents, but the **Basic Structure of Society**:
* The way in which major political and economic institutions fit together into one system of social cooperation.
* These institutions include the political constitution, legally recognized forms of property, the organization of the economy, and the family structure.
* Because the basic structure exerts a profound and inescapable influence on individuals' life prospects from birth, distributing foundational advantages and burdens, its fairness is of paramount moral urgency.

## 2. The Original Position and the Veil of Ignorance

To determine which principles of justice are objectively fair, Rawls introduces his signature thought experiment: the **Original Position** (*status quo ante*)[^rawls-veil-ref].

```
                     THE ORIGINAL POSITION
      ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │               THE VEIL OF IGNORANCE               │
      │                                                   │
      │  Parties do NOT know:                             │
      │  - Their class, race, gender, or social status    │
      │  - Their natural talents, intelligence, strength  │
      │  - Their particular conception of the good        │
      │  - Their psychological propensities / risk tolerance│
      │  - The economic/political state of their society  │
      │                                                   │
      │  Parties DO know:                                 │
      │  - General laws of human psychology & economics   │
      │  - Circumstances of justice (moderate scarcity)   │
      │  - Need for "primary social goods" (rights, income)│
      └─────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┘
                                │
                                ▼
                   MAXIMIN RATIONAL CHOICE
                                │
                                ▼
                 TWO PRINCIPLES OF JUSTICE
```

[^rawls-veil-ref]: See the dedicated concept article on [[The Veil of Ignorance]] for an in-depth formal breakdown of maximin reasoning versus Bayesian expected utility.

Under the **Veil of Ignorance**, rational representatives are deprived of all contingent, morally arbitrary information about themselves. Because no one knows their place in society, no party can propose principles tailor-made to advantage their personal faction, race, wealth, or gender. Choice under the veil models **pure procedural justice**: the fairness of the initial conditions guarantees the fairness of the principles agreed upon.

## 3. The Two Principles of Justice

Rawls argues that rational self-interested contractors in the original position would reject [[Utilitarianism]] (which permits sacrificing individual rights for aggregate utility) and choose two lexicographically ordered principles:

### Principle 1: The Equal Liberty Principle
> "Each person is to have an equal right to the most extensive basic liberty compatible with a similar liberty for others." (*TJ*, §11)

These basic liberties include political liberty (the right to vote and hold public office), freedom of speech and assembly, liberty of conscience, freedom of thought, freedom of the person, the right to hold personal property, and freedom from arbitrary arrest. This principle enjoys strict **Lexical Priority** over economic distribution: basic civil and political liberties cannot be traded away for greater economic prosperity.

### Principle 2: The Social and Economic Principle
Social and economic inequalities must satisfy two conditions:
1. **Fair Equality of Opportunity**: They must be attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity (those with similar abilities and motivation should have equal chances of access regardless of social background).
2. **The Difference Principle**: They must be to the greatest benefit of the **least-advantaged members of society** (the maximin criterion).

The **Difference Principle** recognizes that natural endowments (intelligence, physical prowess) and social accidents of birth are morally arbitrary lotteries. Rather than equalizing outcomes through leveling or coercion, the difference principle harnesses inequalities: talents and wealth accumulation are permissible only if their exercise improves the absolute position of the worst-off socioeconomic stratum.

## 4. Reflective Equilibrium

Epistemologically, Rawls grounds his normative framework not in Cartesian foundationalism or divine command, but in the method of **Reflective Equilibrium** (*reflective equilibrium*)[^reflective-eq-ref]:

```
                     REFLECTIVE EQUILIBRIUM
                 ┌────────────────────────────┐
                 │    Considered Judgments    │
                 │  (e.g., Religious bias and │
                 │   slavery are unjust)      │
                 └─────────────▲──────────────┘
                               │
                       Mutual Adjustment
                               │
                 ┌─────────────▼──────────────┐
                 │   Philosophical Principles │
                 │  (e.g., Difference Princ., │
                 │   Original Position)       │
                 └────────────────────────────┘
```

1. We begin with our most confident, considered moral judgments (e.g., religious persecution, racial discrimination, and slavery are unjust).
2. We formulate general theoretical principles that systematically explain and account for these convictions.
3. Where discrepancies arise between principles and judgments, we work back and forth—sometimes pruning the principle, sometimes re-evaluating our judgment—until our principles and judgments cohere in stable reflective equilibrium.

[^reflective-eq-ref]: Formulated by Rawls in *A Theory of Justice*, §4 and §9. See also Norman Daniels, *Justice and Justification: Reflective Equilibrium in Theory and Practice* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1996).

## 5. Political Liberalism and Overlapping Consensus

In his second major work, *Political Liberalism* (1993), Rawls addressed a fundamental challenge: in a free, democratic society characterized by the "fact of reasonable pluralism," how can a stable and just society of free and equal citizens endure when citizens hold deeply irreconcilable religious, philosophical, and moral worldviews ("comprehensive doctrines")?

Rawls answered by reframing Justice as Fairness not as a comprehensive moral philosophy (like Kantianism or Mill's utilitarianism), but as a freestanding **Political Conception of Justice**:
* **Overlapping Consensus**: Diverse comprehensive doctrines (liberal Christians, Muslims, secular humanists, Kantians) can each endorse the constitutional principles of justice from within their own theological or ethical frameworks.
* **Public Reason**: When debating constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice in the public forum, citizens, judges, and legislators must justify their positions using shared political values and reasons accessible to all reasonable citizens, rather than appealing directly to sectarian religious scriptures.

## 6. Critical Debates and Philosophical Legacy

Rawls's work sparked decades of fruitful debate that defined modern political philosophy:
* **Libertarian Critique**: [[Robert Nozick]] (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974) argued that Rawls's difference principle treats individuals' talents and labor as collective assets, violating self-ownership and requiring continuous, unjustifiable state redistribution.
* **Communitarian Critique**: Michael Sandel (*Liberalism and the Limits of Justice*, 1982) and Charles Taylor criticized the original position's conception of the "unencumbered self," arguing that human identity is inextricably embedded in communitarian and cultural traditions.
* **Marxist and Radical Critique**: G.A. Cohen argued that Rawls's difference principle makes too many concessions to capitalist incentives, failing to achieve genuine socialist equality of ethos.
* **Capabilities Approach**: Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum argued that focusing on primary goods overlooks the varied abilities of individuals to convert resources into actual human freedoms and functioning.

## PhilPapers Survey Profile

*Projected stance based on Rawls's corpus:*

### Metaphysics & Mind
* **Abstract objects:** Constructivism / Anti-realism (*"Political, not Metaphysical"*; normative concepts are constructed for public justification rather than discovered in an independent realm)
* **Free will:** Compatibilism / Practical Autonomy (moral agents possess the reflective capacity to revise desires and act from principles of justice)
* **Personal identity:** Moral Personality (defined by two moral powers: the capacity for a sense of justice and the capacity for a conception of the good)

### Epistemology
* **Epistemic justification:** Coherentism / Reflective Equilibrium (wide reflective equilibrium mutually adjusting considered judgments, theoretical principles, and social theories)
* **External world:** Pragmatic Realism
* **Knowledge:** Anti-foundationalist Constructivism (rejecting rational intuitionism and naturalistic reductions)

### Value Theory
* **Distributive justice:** Liberal Egalitarianism / Maximin Priority (the Difference Principle maximizes the life prospects of the least-advantaged socioeconomic stratum)
* **Experience machine:** Don't enter (hedonism misrepresents human rational life-plans and autonomous agency)
* **Meta-ethics:** Moral Constructivism (Kantian constructivism; objectivity is procedural rather than metaphysical)
* **Moral judgment:** Cognitivism (principles of justice are objective public standards of right)
* **Normative ethics:** Deontology / Contractarianism (priority of the Right over the Good; lexical priority of basic civil liberties)
* **Political philosophy:** Egalitarian Liberalism / Property-Owning Democracy (rejects welfare-state capitalism in favor of structural dispersion of productive wealth)
* **Trolley problem:** Deontological Side-Constraints (strictly prohibits sacrificing fundamental liberties or individual persons for aggregate social utility)

### Logic, Language, & Science
* **Rational choice:** Maximin decision rule under Knightian uncertainty (rejecting Bayesian expected utility maximization in the Original Position)
* **Science:** Empirical Realism (general findings of sociology, psychology, and economics serve as background inputs under the Veil of Ignorance)

### Metaphilosophy & Religion
* **Aim of philosophy:** Realistic Utopia; providing a shared basis for public justification in a democratic society marked by reasonable pluralism
* **God:** Agnosticism / Secular Public Reason (comprehensive religious doctrines belong to civil society; public reason governs constitutional essentials)
* **Philosophical methods:** Ideal theory, modeling via hypothetical contract (*the Original Position*), reflective equilibrium
