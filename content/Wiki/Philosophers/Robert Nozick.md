# Robert Nozick

<div className="dropcap">

**Robert Nozick** (1938–2002) was an American analytic philosopher and the Joseph Pellegrino University Professor at Harvard University. Renowned for his intellectual brilliance, playful thought experiments, and philosophical breadth, Nozick became world-famous with his 1974 National Book Award-winning treatise *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*[^nozick-asu-ref], the definitive philosophical statement of **Libertarian Political Theory**.

</div>

Published as a direct, uncompromising response to his Harvard colleague [[John Rawls]]'s *A Theory of Justice* (1971), Nozick's work defended the moral inviolability of individual rights, rejected state wealth redistribution, and devised two of modern philosophy's most influential thought experiments: [[The Experience Machine]] and the **Wilt Chamberlain Argument**.

---

## 1. The Experience Machine: Refuting Hedonism

In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, Nozick challenged ethical and psychological [[Utilitarianism|hedonism]] through **The Experience Machine**[^nozick-asu-ref]:

> "Suppose there were an experience machine that would give you any experience you desired. Superduper neuropsychologists could stimulate your brain so that you would think and feel you were writing a great novel, or making a friend, or reading an interesting book. All the time you would be floating in a tank, with electrodes attached to your brain. Should you plug into this machine for life...?" (*ASU*, 42–45)

```
                    THE EXPERIENCE MACHINE DILEMMA
       ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
       │             THE TANK / VIRTUAL EXPERIENCE              │
       │  Subjective Experience: Identical to writing great     │
       │  novels, scaling Everest, being loved, winning honors. │
       │  Actual Reality: Floating naked in a warm tank with    │
       │  electrodes stuck in your skull.                       │
       └───────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼  WOULD YOU PLUG IN?
       ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
       │  MOST PEOPLE REFUSE TO PLUG IN FOR LIFE BECAUSE:       │
       │  1. We want to DO certain things, not just feel like   │
       │     we did them.                                       │
       │  2. We want to BE a certain sort of person, not an     │
       │     indeterminate blob floating in a tank.             │
       │  3. We crave contact with a DEEPER REALITY.            │
       └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Nozick concluded that human beings care about more than the subjective internal feel of experiences: we desire genuine, veridical contact with reality, action, and authentic self-development. See the dedicated analysis in [[The Experience Machine]].

[^nozick-asu-ref]: Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (New York: Basic Books, 1974). See [[Bibliography#Nozick-ASU-1974]].

## 2. Moral Side Constraints and the Minimal State

Nozick begins *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* with his famous opening salvo:
> "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights). So strong and far-reaching are these rights that they raise the question of what, if anything, the state and its officials may do." (*ASU*, ix)

Drawing on [[Immanuel Kant]]'s Formula of Humanity, Nozick interprets rights as **Moral Side Constraints**:
* Rights do not function as goals to be maximized (which would allow sacrificing an innocent person's rights to minimize overall rights violations in society).
* Rather, rights are absolute side constraints that set impenetrable boundaries around each person's moral sphere, reflecting the fundamental **separateness of persons**.

From this, Nozick demonstrates that only a **Minimal State** (a "night-watchman state" limited to the functions of protection against force, theft, fraud, and enforcement of contracts) is morally justifiable. Any more extensive state—such as one using taxation for redistributive welfare or public healthcare—violates individuals' negative rights.

## 3. The Entitlement Theory of Justice

Against Rawls's [[The Veil of Ignorance|Difference Principle]], which evaluates justice according to structural end-state patterns of distribution, Nozick formulated the **Historical Entitlement Theory**:

$$\text{A distribution is just if and only if it arises from another just distribution by legitimate means.}$$

The theory comprises three principles:
1. **Principle of Justice in Acquisition**: How unowned natural resources can legitimately come to be privately held (appropriation that leaves "enough and as good" for others, following Locke).
2. **Principle of Justice in Transfer**: How holdings can legitimately pass from one person to another (voluntary exchange, gifts, inheritance).
3. **Principle of Rectification of Injustice**: How past historical violations of acquisition or transfer must be redressed.

### The Wilt Chamberlain Argument
Nozick demonstrated the impossibility of maintaining any egalitarian or "patterned" principle of justice (*"to each according to their need/merit"*) via the famous **Wilt Chamberlain** scenario:
1. Suppose society achieves an initial, perfectly equal distribution $D_1$.
2. Famed basketball star Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract: for every home game, twenty-five cents from the price of each admission ticket drops directly into a special box labeled for Chamberlain.
3. Over the season, one million spectators cheerfully drop their quarters into the box, freely choosing to watch him play.
4. Society lands in distribution $D_2$: Chamberlain now possesses $250,000, vastly wealthier than everyone else. The initial egalitarian pattern is shattered.
5. Nozick asks: Is $D_2$ unjust? Each spectator was entitled to their quarter; each gave it voluntarily; no third party was robbed. To maintain an egalitarian pattern, the state must continuously and coercively prohibit capitalist acts between consenting adults (*ASU*, 160–164).

## 4. Epistemology: Tracking the Truth

In *Philosophical Explanations* (1981), Nozick revolutionized analytic epistemology by proposing the **Truth-Tracking Theory of Knowledge** to solve [[The Problem of Induction|Gettier problems]]:

$$\text{S knows that P if and only if:}$$
$$\text{1. P is true; } \quad \text{2. S believes that P;}$$
$$\text{3. If P were not true, S would not believe that P (Sensitivity);}$$
$$\text{4. If P were true in slightly different circumstances, S would still believe that P (Adherence).}$$

This counterfactual modal approach allowed Nozick to address radical Cartesian skepticism without relying on epistemic closure.
