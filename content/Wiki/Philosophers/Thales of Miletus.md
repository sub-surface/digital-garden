# Philosopher: Thales of Miletus

> "Water is the principle of all things, and all things are full of gods."

## Quick Info
* **Dates:** c. 624 – 548 BCE
* **Nationality:** Ionian Greek (Milesian)
* **Philosophical Tradition:** Pre-Socratic Philosophy / Milesian Naturalism
* **Areas of Specialization (AOS):** Cosmology, Mathematics, Astronomy
* **Notable For:** Identifying water as the *arche*, predicting the solar eclipse of 585 BCE, originating non-mythological scientific inquiry

---

## Biography Summary

<div className="dropcap">

Thales of Miletus was an ancient Greek philosopher, mathematician, and astronomer traditionally hailed by Aristotle and subsequent historiography as the first philosopher in the Western tradition. Living in the affluent Ionian seaport of Miletus (modern-day Turkey), Thales was celebrated as one of the Seven Sages of Ancient Greece. Breaking decisively with Homeric and Hesiodic mythopoetic traditions—which explained natural phenomena through the arbitrary interventions of anthropomorphic gods—Thales initiated the search for rational, naturalistic principles underlying the cosmos. Renowned as an engineer and astronomer, he famously predicted the solar eclipse of May 28, 585 BCE, which halted a battle between the Medes and Lydians.

</div>

## Key Philosophical Contributions

### 1. Water as the Material Principle (*Arche*)
According to Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, Thales was the originator of philosophy because he was the first to seek a single, universal material substrate (**arche**) explaining all physical diversity:
* **The Hypothesis**: Thales posited that the primary substance of all things is **Water** (*hydor*).
* **Rational Observation**: Aristotle suggests Thales observed that the nourishment of all things is moist, that heat itself is generated from moisture, and that the seeds of all living creatures have a moist nature. Furthermore, water exists visibly in three distinct states of matter: solid (ice), liquid (water), and vapor (steam/mist).
* **The Earth on Water**: Thales hypothesized that the Earth rests or floats upon a vast cosmic ocean like a log, explaining earthquakes as mechanical disturbances caused by subterranean waves rather than the anger of Poseidon.

### 2. Hylozoism: "All Things Are Full of Gods"
Aristotle reports that Thales believed the universe is alive and animated:
* Observing that the mineral lodestone (magnetite) and amber rubbed with fur move iron particles, Thales deduced that magnetic minerals possess a "soul" (*psyche*)—defined as the inherent capacity to initiate self-motion.
* His aphorism *"all things are full of gods"* (*panta plere theon einai*) was not a retreat to religious myth, but an early pantheistic or hylozoic assertion that active, motive power is immanent throughout the material natural world rather than bestowed by Olympian deities.

### 3. Geometrical and Applied Mathematics
Greek tradition attributes several fundamental geometrical theorems to Thales:
* A circle is bisected by its diameter.
* The base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.
* When two straight lines intersect, the vertically opposite angles are equal.
* An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle (**Thales's Theorem**).
* Thales is famously recorded by Hieronymus of Rhodes as having calculated the height of the Egyptian pyramids by measuring their shadows at the exact hour of the day when a man's shadow equals his height.

## Significance in Phil Chat

Thales is invoked as the historical starting point of the philosophical enterprise:
* When channel participants debate whether philosophy is distinct from natural science or merely speculative physics in its infancy, debaters cite Thales's transition from Homeric myth to naturalistic hypothesis.
* Thales's classic biographical anecdote (recounted by Plato in the *Theaetetus*, where Thales fell into a well while gazing up at the stars, mocked by a Thracian maidservant) is regularly cited in *The Phil Chat Times* as the primordial archetype of the socially detached academic philosopher.
