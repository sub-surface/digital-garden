# Philosopher: Baruch Spinoza

> "God or Nature (*Deus sive Natura*)."

## Quick Info
* **Dates:** 1632 – 1677
* **Nationality:** Dutch (Portuguese-Jewish descent)
* **Philosophical Tradition:** Early Modern Rationalism
* **Areas of Specialization (AOS):** Metaphysics, Ethics, Philosophy of Mind
* **Notable For:** Substance Monism, the geometric method (*more geometrico*), radical immanence, determinism

## Biography Summary

<div className="dropcap">

Baruch Spinoza was a Dutch philosopher of Portuguese Sephardic Jewish descent, considered one of the foremost exponents of 17th-century rationalism alongside René Descartes and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Expelled from the Amsterdam Jewish congregation in 1656 for his "abominable heresies," Spinoza lived a quiet, ascetic life as a lens grinder while producing works that laid the foundation for the Enlightenment and modern biblical criticism. His magnum opus, the *Ethics*, demonstrated using Euclid's axiomatic geometric method, overturned Cartesian dualism in favor of an uncompromising substance monism.

</div>

## Key Philosophical Contributions

### Substance Monism (*Deus sive Natura*)
Spinoza rejected Descartes' division between thinking substance (*res cogitans*) and extended substance (*res extensa*). For Spinoza, there exists only one infinite, uncaused substance: God or Nature. Mind and matter are not two distinct substances interacting mysteriously in the pineal gland, but two infinite attributes through which the singular substance expresses itself. Finite things—including human beings—are merely "modes" (affections) of this single substance.

### Radical Immanence
God is not a transcendent clockmaker creating the universe from outside, but the immanent cause of all things (*natura naturans* expressing itself as *natura naturata*). Everything follows from the necessary laws of nature with geometric certainty; free will in the libertarian sense is an illusion born of ignorance of causes.

### Conatus and Affect Theory
Every mode of substance possesses an inherent drive to persist in its own being (*conatus essendi*). Human emotions (affects) are transitions toward greater or lesser power of acting. Joy (*laetitia*) is the passage to a greater perfection; sadness (*tristitia*) is the passage to a lesser. Reason does not suppress passion from above, but redirects desire toward understanding, which constitutes true beatitude and freedom.
