# Parmenides of Elea

<div className="dropcap">

**Parmenides of Elea** (fl. c. 500 – 450 BCE) was a Greek philosopher from the southern Italian colony of Elea (Velia) whose single surviving philosophical poem initiated Western metaphysics, rigorous deductive logic, and the philosophical distinction between appearance (*doxa*) and rational reality (*aletheia*).

</div>

Before Parmenides, the Ionian cosmologists ([[Thales of Miletus]], Anaximander, and [[Heraclitus of Ephesus]]) speculated on which observable element generated the changing cosmos. Parmenides swept aside observational cosmology by applying unrelenting deductive reason to the pure concept of **Being** itself, concluding that change, motion, plurality, and generation are logically impossible illusions.

---

## 1. The Proem: The Chariot Journey to the Goddess

Parmenides cast his philosophical revelation in the elevated epic hexameters of Homer and Hesiod. The **Proem** (fragment DK 28 B 1) recounts an ecstatic allegorical journey:

* The philosopher is drawn in a chariot steered by the Daughters of the Sun (*Heliades*).
* He leaves behind the dark realm of mortal opinion and ascends to the lofty gates where the paths of Night and Day divide.
* Justice (*Dike*) unlocks the massive lintel gates, and a nameless Goddess welcomes him, promising to reveal two diametrically opposed paths:

> "It is necessary for you to learn all things: both the unshaken heart of well-rounded Truth [*Aletheia*], and the opinions of mortals [*Doxa*], in which there is no genuine conviction." (DK 28 B 1.28–30)[^parmenides-proem]

[^parmenides-proem]: Hermann Diels and Walther Kranz, eds., *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker* (Berlin: Weidmann, 1951), fragment DK 28 B 1. Translation in KRS, *The Presocratic Philosophers* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1983), 242. See [[Bibliography#Diels-Kranz]].

## 2. The Two Routes of Inquiry

In fragment DK 28 B 2, the Goddess presents the foundational logical bifurcation of all thought:

```
                      THE ROUTES OF INQUIRY
                    ┌─────────────────────────┐
                    │ What can be investigated │
                    └────────────┬────────────┘
                                 │
           ┌─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┐
           │                                           │
     ┌─────▼───────────────────┐                 ┌─────▼───────────────────┐
     │      FIRST ROUTE        │                 │      SECOND ROUTE       │
     │      (THAT IT IS)       │                 │    (THAT IT IS NOT)     │
     └─────────────┬───────────┘                 └─────────────┬───────────┘
                   │                                           │
       Logical Status: Necessary                   Logical Status: Impossible
     Path of Persuasion & Truth                  "A path entirely unlearnable"
        (Follows Aletheia)                           (Cannot speak or know
                                                       Non-Being, B 2.7–8)
```

1. **The First Path**: *"That it is, and that it is not possible for it not to be"*—the path of persuasion, which attends upon Truth.
2. **The Second Path**: *"That it is not, and that it is necessary for it not to be"*—a path which the Goddess declares to be utterly impassable and unlearnable.

Why is the second path impossible? Because **Non-Being** (*to me on*) cannot be thought, known, or articulated:

$$\text{For you could neither know what is not (for it cannot be done)} \quad \text{nor could you speak of it.} \quad (\text{DK 28 B 2.7–8})$$

This entails the celebrated Parmenidean axiom on the unity of mind and reality:

> "For it is the same thing that can be thought and that can be."  
> (*To gar auto noein estin te kai einai*, DK 28 B 3)

## 3. The Deduction of Being (*The Way of Truth*, DK 28 B 8)

In the monumental fragment B 8, Parmenides unfolds a chain of deductive proofs (*semeia*, signposts) deriving the essential attributes of **What-Is** (*To Eon*). Each proof proceeds by reductio ad absurdum:

### A. Ungenerated (*ageneton*) and Imperishable (*anolethron*)
If What-Is came into being, it must have arisen either from What-Is or from What-Is-Not:
* It cannot arise from What-Is-Not, for What-Is-Not does not exist and cannot generate anything (*ex nihilo nihil fit*).
* Nor could any necessity compel it to sprout sooner or later, beginning from nothing.
* Therefore, generation (*genesis*) is extinguished and destruction (*olethros*) is unheard of (B 8.21).

### B. Timeless and Present (*Nun estin*)
What-Is does not have a past or future:
* It never *was*, nor *will it be*, since it **is now, all together, one, continuous** (*nun estin homou pan, hen, syneches*, B 8.5).
* Temporal passage requires that What-Was is no longer and What-Will-Be is not yet; but "is not" cannot be admitted.

### C. Indivisible (*adiaireton*) and Homogeneous
It is not more in one place and less in another, which would prevent it from holding together:
* It is entirely full of What-Is.
* Hence it is all continuous, for What-Is draws near to What-Is (B 8.22–25).

### D. Motionless and Complete (*Teleion*)
Bound by Necessity (*Ananke*) within the limits of great bonds:
* To move would require moving into empty space (*the void*); but the void is What-Is-Not, which does not exist.
* It is complete on all sides, like the bulk of a **well-rounded sphere** (*eukuklou sphaires*, B 8.43), equally poised in all directions from the center.

## 4. The Way of Seeming (*Doxa*)

Following the deduction of True Being, the Goddess introduces the second part of the poem: the cosmology of mortal opinions (*Doxa*):

> "Here I halt for you my trustworthy account and thought about truth; henceforth learn the opinions of mortals, listening to the deceitful arrangement of my words." (DK 28 B 8.50–52)

Mortals have made the fatal mistake of naming two contrary forms where they should name only one:
1. **Light / Fire**: Subtle, gentle, luminous, identical with itself.
2. **Night / Earth**: Dense, dark, heavy, and cold.

Scholars debate why Parmenides included the *Doxa*. The predominant view is that having established the true Eleatic monism through pure reason, the philosopher must understand how mortal perception mistakenly constructs the phenomenal world of generation, change, and death.

---

## 5. The Eleatic Legacy and Zeno's Paradoxes

Parmenides' radical monism posed the decisive crisis of early Greek philosophy:

1. **Zeno of Elea**: Parmenides' favorite pupil constructed the famous paradoxes of motion (the Achilles, the Dichotomy, the Arrow, the Stadium) to show that the pluralists' assumption of space, time, and motion leads to even greater logical absurdities than Eleatic monism.
2. **The Pluralist Response**: Empedocles (four roots), Anaxagoras (seeds and Nous), and the Atomists Democritus and Leucippus all accepted Parmenides' principle that *nothing can come from nothing*, but preserved the reality of change by proposing that composite objects are mixtures and separations of unchanging, eternal micro-entities (roots, seeds, or uncuttable atoms moving through the void).
3. **Plato's Patricide**: In the dialogue *Sophist* (241d), Plato famously acknowledged that to explain error, false speech, and images, one must commit "patricide" against "father Parmenides" by establishing that in some sense *What-Is-Not is*, and *What-Is is not* (the realm of difference and relation).
