# Philosopher: René Descartes

> "Cogito, ergo sum. (I think, therefore I am.)"

* **Dates:** 1596 – 1650

* **Areas of Specialization (AOS):** Epistemology, Metaphysics, Mathematics
* **Notable For:** *Meditations on First Philosophy*, *Discourse on Method*, Cartesian coordinate system, substance dualism

## Biography Summary

<div className="dropcap">

René Descartes was a French philosopher, mathematician, and scientist universally recognized as the foundational figure of modern Western philosophy. Educated by the Jesuits at La Flèche and later serving in military campaigns across Europe, Descartes sought to overthrow Aristotelian scholasticism and establish natural philosophy on an indubitable, mathematically transparent foundation. His development of the Cartesian coordinate system unified algebra and Euclidean geometry. In his landmark philosophical works—*Discourse on the Method* (1637) and *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641)—Descartes pioneered radical epistemological skepticism, inaugurating the modern subject-centered "turn to the mind."

</div>

## Key Philosophical Contributions

### 1. Methodological Doubt & The Archimedean Point
To discover a bedrock upon which to rebuild the sciences, Descartes resolved to systematically doubt any belief that could be even slightly questioned:
* **Sensory Illusion**: Senses occasionally deceive us regarding distant or minute objects.
* **The Dream Argument**: There are no definitive waking marks distinguishing waking reality from vivid dreams.
* **The Evil Demon (*Malin Génie*)**: An omnipotent, deceptive demon could systematically manipulate our cognitive faculties so that even basic mathematical truths ($2 + 3 = 5$) are false.

Amidst total doubt, Descartes discovered his indubitable truth: even if a demon deceives him about everything, the very act of doubting proves that he exists as a thinking entity:
$$\text{Cogito, ergo sum} \implies \text{I think, therefore I am}$$

### 2. The Criterion of Truth: Clear and Distinct Perception
From the *Cogito*, Descartes extracted his universal criterion of truth: whatever he perceives clearly and distinctly must be true. To validate this criterion against the Evil Demon, Descartes deduced the existence of God:
1. The idea of an infinite, perfect being exists in his finite mind.
2. The cause of an idea must possess as much reality as the idea itself (Causal Adequacy Principle).
3. Therefore, God must exist as the cause of this innate idea.
4. Because deception is a defect, an all-perfect God cannot be a deceiver; therefore, our clear and distinct perceptions of mathematical and geometric truths are guaranteed.

### 3. Cartesian Substance Dualism
Descartes divided the created universe into two mutually exclusive, independently existing substances:
* **Thinking Substance (*Res Cogitans*)**: Unextended, non-physical, indivisible, conscious minds possessing free will.
* **Extended Substance (*Res Extensa*)**: Spatial, extended, mathematically quantifiable, divisible matter governed entirely by mechanistic, deterministic laws of physics.

This introduced the notorious **Mind-Body Interaction Problem**: how can an immaterial, non-spatial mind causally interact with a physical, spatial body? Descartes hypothesized that interaction occurs through the pineal gland in the brain, an answer that dissatisfied his successors (prompting Spinoza's monism and Malebranche's occasionalism).

## Major Works

1. *Discourse on the Method* (1637) — Intellectual autobiography, rules for directing the mind, and formulation of the *Cogito*.
2. *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641) — Six meditations establishing radical doubt, the existence of God, and substance dualism.
3. *Principles of Philosophy* (1644) — Comprehensive textbook synthesizing Cartesian metaphysics and mechanistic physics.
4. *Passions of the Soul* (1649) — Moral psychology and analysis of mind-body interaction via the pineal gland.
