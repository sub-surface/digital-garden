# Philosopher: Jean-Paul Sartre

> "Man is condemned to be free; because once thrown into the world, he is responsible for everything he does."

## Quick Info
* **Dates:** 1905 – 1980
* **Nationality:** French
* **Philosophical Tradition:** 20th-Century Continental Philosophy / Existential Phenomenology
* **Areas of Specialization (AOS):** Phenomenological Ontology, Existential Ethics, Political Philosophy
* **Notable For:** *Being and Nothingness*, *Existentialism is a Humanism*, *Nausea*, *No Exit*, declined the Nobel Prize in Literature (1964)

## Biography Summary

<div className="dropcap">

Jean-Paul Sartre was the preeminent public intellectual of postwar Europe, whose philosophical treatises, novels, plays, and political activism popularized existentialism across the globe. Educated at the École Normale Supérieure, Sartre formed a lifelong intellectual and romantic partnership with [[Simone de Beauvoir]]. In the early 1930s, he studied phenomenology in Berlin under the influence of [[Edmund Husserl]] and [[Martin Heidegger]], transforming their descriptive methods into his philosophical novel *Nausea* (1938) and his masterwork *Being and Nothingness* (1943). Captured as a prisoner of war during the fall of France, Sartre returned to occupied Paris to participate in the Resistance, later founding the influential intellectual journal *Les Temps Modernes*. In the postwar era, Sartre moved toward revolutionary Marxism, engaging in ferocious polemics with [[Albert Camus]] and Raymond Aron. In 1964, he famously refused the Nobel Prize in Literature to preserve his intellectual independence. Over 50,000 Parisians accompanied his funeral cortege to Montparnasse Cemetery in 1980.

## Key Philosophical Contributions

### 1. Phenomenological Ontology: In-Itself vs. For-Itself
In *Being and Nothingness*, Sartre divides reality into two fundamentally distinct ontological categories:
* **Being-in-itself (*L'en-soi*)**: Non-conscious, inert matter. It is fully self-identical, opaque, solid, and without reason or justification (*de trop*, superfluous). A stone or a table simply *is* what it is.
* **Being-for-itself (*Le pour-soi*)**: Conscious human reality. Consciousness is not an object; it is an intentional void, a "hole of nothingness" at the heart of Being. Consciousness is defined by its capacity to negate, question, imagine, and detach from what is:
  $$\text{Consciousness is a being which is not what it is, and is what it is not.}$$

### 2. Radical Freedom and Anguish (*L'Angoisse*)
Because consciousness is a nihilation of given facticity, it is ungrounded by nature, biology, or past causes:
* Humans are not determined by their psychological history or social station; in every moment, a chasm of nothingness separates our past from our present choice.
* This absolute, inescapable responsibility induces **Anguish (*L'angoisse*)**: the vertigo of knowing that there are no objective moral values written in heaven to justify our choices. We must invent our values and bear sole authorship of their consequences.[^sartre-bn]

### 3. Bad Faith (*Mauvaise Foi*)
To escape the unbearable weight of anguish, human beings constantly flee into **Bad Faith**:
* In bad faith, a person lies to themselves to evade their radical freedom, pretending they are a passive object governed by circumstance, biology, or social station.
* Sartre illustrates this with his celebrated description of the **café waiter**: the waiter moves with exaggerated, mechanical precision, play-acting the role of a waiter to escape his freedom and treat himself as a thing possessing an inherent essence.

### 4. The Look (*Le Regard*) and Intersubjectivity
Before encountering the Other, the subject reigns as the center of an unfolding world. However, when another person looks at me:
* I experience myself as an **object** in their subjective universe. My sovereignty is stolen; I am captured and defined from outside.
* This produces shame, pride, and fundamental interpersonal conflict. In his play *No Exit* (*Huis Clos*), Sartre summarizes this dynamic in the famous line: *"Hell is other people"* (*L'enfer, c'est les autres*). Genuine communion is fraught because each subject inevitably attempts to objectify the other or surrender themselves in masochistic submission.

### 5. The Marxist Turn: Fused Groups and Scarcity
In his later *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960), Sartre attempted to reconcile existential freedom with historical materialism:
* He admitted that concrete historical freedom is radically conditioned by economic **scarcity** (*la rareté*).
* He traced how alienated individuals trapped in bureaucratic routines can coalesce during revolutionary moments into **Fused Groups** (*groupes en fusion*), reclaiming collective agency against oppressive social systems.

## Literary Engagement (*La Littérature Engagée*) & The Visceral Flesh

For Sartre, philosophy and literature were inextricably fused:
* **The Committed Writer (*L'Écrivain Engagé*)**: In *What is Literature?* (*Qu'est-ce que la littérature?*, 1948), Sartre argued that writing is an act of historical action:
  > *"To speak is to act; anything which one names is already no longer quite the same, it has lost its innocence."*
  Prose is not an autonomous aesthetic bauble, but a direct appeal to the freedom of the reader to transform the social world.
* **The Visceral Phenomenology of Contingency**: Sartre’s philosophical breakthroughs were born not in formal syllogisms, but in visceral bodily revolts. In his 1938 novel *Nausea* (*La Nausée*), Antoine Roquentin's metaphysical crisis begins when staring at the black, knotted root of a chestnut tree in the municipal park of Bouville: stripped of its linguistic label ("root") and its functional utility ("tree"), the object exposes its naked, gelatinous, obscene contingency (*de trop*). Sartre’s phenomenology dwells relentlessly in physical textures—slime (*le visqueux*), stickiness, nausea, and rot—as the direct sensory manifestation of ungrounded matter.
* **Reception & Critique**: In *Adventures of the Dialectic* (1955), [[Maurice Merleau-Ponty]] critiqued Sartre's early existentialism as an "ultrabolshevism" trapped in Cartesian dualism—opposing an absolutely free consciousness to an absolutely inert world, unable to account for historical mediation. Later, [[Michel Foucault]] observed that Sartre was *"the magnificent and pathetic effort of a man of the nineteenth century endeavoring to think the twentieth century."*

## Significance in Phil Chat

Sartre's philosophy is an active point of friction in server debates:
* **The Counter-Spawnpill**: In disputes against biological determinism ([[Spawnpill]]), Sartre's distinction between **Facticity** (unchosen baseline facts) and **Transcendence** (the projective choice of how to inhabit those facts) is constantly deployed by the Continental wing.
* **Bad Faith in Discord Culture**: Chatters regularly diagnose peer performances through *mauvaise foi*—calling out interlocutors who pretend to be bound by rhetorical roles, debate club rules, or ironclad algorithmic logic rather than acknowledging their personal stakes in the argument.

## Major Works

1. *Nausea* (*La Nausée*, 1938) — Landmark existential novel dramatizing the visceral horror of bare material contingency.
2. *Being and Nothingness* (*L'Être et le Néant*, 1943) — Magnum opus of existential phenomenological ontology.
3. *No Exit* (*Huis Clos*, 1944) — Existentialist drama on intersubjectivity, bad faith, and the tortures of the Look.
4. *Existentialism is a Humanism* (*L'existentialisme est un humanisme*, 1946) — Famous public lecture defending existentialism against Marxist and Catholic critics.
5. *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (*Critique de la raison dialectique*, 1960) — Structural treatise synthesizing existentialism with Marxist historical materialism.
