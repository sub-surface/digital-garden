# Laozi

<div className="dropcap">

**Laozi** (literally "Old Master", c. 6th – 4th century BCE) was the foundational philosopher of classical **Daoism** (*Daojia*) in ancient China. Tradition attributes to him the authorship of the **Daodejing** (*Tao Te Ching*, "Classic of the Way and Its Virtue")[^laozi-ddj-ref]—an 81-chapter poetic text of enduring metaphysical depth and practical statecraft that remains among the most widely translated works in world literature.

</div>

According to the *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*) by Sima Qian, Laozi served as the royal keeper of archives at the Zhou court in Luoyang. Disillusioned by the moral degeneration and incessant warfare of the late Spring and Autumn period, he rode westward on a water ox toward the border pass of Hangu. At the gate, the warden Yinxi implored him to record his wisdom before departing into the wilderness; Laozi composed the five-thousand-character *Daodejing* and rode out into legend.

## 1. The Nameless Dao (*Chang Dao*)

The *Daodejing* opens with the most famous ontological paradox in Chinese thought:

$$\text{"道可道，非常道；名可名，非常名。"}$$
$$\text{(The Dao that can be spoken of is not the constant Dao; the name that can be named is not the constant name. Ch. 1)}$$

```
                      THE PARADOX OF THE DAO
                 ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                 │       THE ETERNAL DAO        │
                 │   - Nameless (Wuming)        │
                 │   - Ground of Heaven & Earth │
                 │   - Ineffable, boundless     │
                 └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                                │
                                ▼
                 ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                 │     THE MANIFEST WORLD       │
                 │   - Named (Youming)          │
                 │   - Mother of the Ten        │
                 │     Thousand Things (Wanwu)  │
                 │   - Dynamic differentiation  │
                 └──────────────────────────────┘
```

The **Dao** (literally "Path" or "Way") is not a personal creator deity, static Platonic Form, or transcendent celestial entity. It is the unnamable, undifferentiated metaphysical source, generative field, and immanent operational pattern of the cosmos:
* *"There is a thing confusedly formed, born before Heaven and Earth. Silent and void, it stands alone and does not change, goes everywhere without exhaustion. It may be regarded as the mother of the world. I do not know its name; I call it Dao."* (Ch. 25)

## 2. *Wu Wei*: Effortless and Non-Coercive Action

The primary ethical and practical doctrine of Laozi is ***Wu Wei*** (無為)—often mistranslated as "inactivity" or "laziness", but properly understood as **effortless action**, **non-forcing**, or **non-coercive responsive harmony**:
* *Wu wei* does not mean doing nothing; it means acting in accord with the natural disposition and grain of things, without imposing rigid, arbitrary human desires or violent coercion upon reality.
* *"The Dao never acts, yet nothing is left undone. If lords and kings can preserve it, the ten thousand things will transform of themselves."* (Ch. 37)

### The Metaphor of Water (*Shang Shan Ruo Shui*)
Laozi repeatedly selects **Water** as the supreme embodiment of the Dao in the phenomenal world:

> "The supreme good is like water. Water is good at benefiting the ten thousand things and does not compete with them. It dwells in places that all humans disdain, therefore it is near to the Dao." (Ch. 8)

Water is the softest and most yielding substance in the world, yet when attacking the hard and rigid (rock, stone, metal), nothing can surpass it. In the Daoist dialectic, **yielding overcomes rigidity**, softness overcomes hardness, and the female/receptive (*Yin*) overcomes the aggressive/domineering (*Yang*).

## 3. The Uncarved Block (*Pu*) and Spontaneity (*Ziran*)

Laozi critiques conventional moral systems (such as the rigid ritual codes of Confucius) as symptoms of moral decline:
* *"When the great Dao was abandoned, there appeared benevolence and righteousness. When knowledge and shrewdness arose, there appeared great hypocrisy."* (Ch. 18)

Laozi recommends returning to the **Uncarved Block** (***Pu***, 樸):
* A raw, unworked piece of timber that retains its natural wholeness before carpenters carve it into specialized tools, bowls, and furniture.
* *Pu* symbolizes the original, uncorrupted simplicity of human nature before social conditioning, competitive ambition, and artificial moral dogmas fragment the human spirit.
* True wisdom consists in aligning with ***Ziran*** (自然, "self-so-ness", natural spontaneity).

---

## 4. Political Philosophy of the Small State

In Chapter 80, Laozi articulates his radical political vision:
* The ideal community is small and peaceful, with few inhabitants.
* Weapons exist but are not displayed; vehicles and boats are present but rarely used; people eat simple wholesome food, wear plain clothes, and dwell happily in their homes.
* Neighboring villages can hear the crowing of each other's roosters and barking of each other's dogs, yet the people grow old and die without interfering with one another.

For Laozi, the best ruler is an invisible shepherd:
> "A leader is best when people barely know he exists... When his work is done, his aim fulfilled, they will say: 'We did it ourselves.'" (Ch. 17)
