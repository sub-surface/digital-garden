# Philosopher: David Hume

> "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them."

## Biography Summary

<div className="dropcap">

David Hume was a preeminent Scottish philosopher, historian, and essayist whose radical empirical skepticism dismantled dogmatic metaphysics and set the agenda for modern epistemology and cognitive science. Born into a modest gentry family in Edinburgh, Hume composed his masterwork, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–1740), in his twenties while living in France, famously lamenting that it "fell dead-born from the press." Undaunted, he recast his ideas into the highly accessible *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) and *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751). Hume's uncompromising naturalist account of human psychology awaked Immanuel Kant from his "dogmatic slumber" and laid the foundation for modern analytic philosophy.

</div>

## Key Philosophical Contributions

### 1. Impressions, Ideas, and the Copy Principle
In the *Treatise*, Hume divided all mental perceptions into two classes:
* **Impressions**: Our direct, vivid, forceful sensory experiences, passions, and emotions as they strike consciousness.
* **Ideas**: The faint, faded mental copies, memories, and reflections of prior impressions.

Through the **Copy Principle**, Hume established an empirical razor: any abstract concept that cannot be traced back to a specific simple impression is devoid of genuine meaning.

### 2. [[The Problem of Induction]] & Causation
Hume showed that we have no rational or sensory basis for asserting causal necessity:
* When one billiard ball strikes another, we observe only **constant conjunction**: event $A$ (motion of the first ball) followed contiguously in space and time by event $B$ (motion of the second ball). We never perceive any physical "necessary connection" or secret power linking them.
* Because deductive logic cannot prove that nature will continue its patterns tomorrow, and inductive appeals to past success are circular, our belief in causal laws is an instinctual habit of association, not a demonstration of reason.[^hume-ehu]

### 3. The Bundle Theory of Personal Identity
Challenging the Cartesian notion of an indivisible thinking soul, Hume turned his gaze inward:
> *"For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception."*[^hume-thn]
The mind is simply a bundle or theatre of fleeting, perpetually shifting perceptions, lacking any permanent underlying substrate.

### 4. Meta-Ethics: The Is-Ought Problem & Sentimentalism
* **Hume's Guillotine (The Is-Ought Problem)**: It is logically impossible to infer prescriptive moral obligations (*ought*) purely from factual descriptive premises (*is*). Morality cannot be deduced from natural facts alone.
* **Moral Sentimentalism**: Reason alone can never motivate action or prevent volition; it can only calculate means to an end. Moral distinctions arise from human sentiments of approval and disapproval: *"Morality, therefore, is more properly felt than judg'd of."*

## Major Works

1. *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–1740) — Hume's monumental comprehensive philosophical system.
2. *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) — Streamlined exposition of induction, causation, and miracles.
3. *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751) — Sentimentalist moral theory.
4. *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779, posthumous) — Classic critique of the argument from design.
