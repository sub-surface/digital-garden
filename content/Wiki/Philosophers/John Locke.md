# John Locke

<div className="dropcap">

**John Locke** (1632–1704) was an English philosopher, physician, and political theorist widely celebrated as the father of **[[British Empiricism]]** and the intellectual progenitor of modern liberal constitutional democracy. His dual masterworks of 1689—*An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*[^locke-essay-ref] and *Two Treatises of Government*[^locke-treatises-ref]—fundamentally remade both Western epistemology and political philosophy.

</div>

Educated at Christ Church, Oxford, and deeply immersed in the new mechanical experimental science of Robert Boyle and Isaac Newton, Locke mounted a systematic demolition of the Cartesian and scholastic doctrine of innate ideas, arguing that all human knowledge originates strictly in **Experience**.

---

## 1. The Critique of Innate Ideas and the *Tabula Rasa*

In Book I of the *Essay*, Locke launched an assault against [[Continental Rationalism|rationalist]] claims (championed by [[René Descartes]] and the Cambridge Platonists) that the human mind is imprinted at birth with innate speculative principles (e.g., the Law of Non-Contradiction) or innate practical moral axioms.

```
                      THE EMPIRICIST GENESIS OF MIND
      ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │                  THE TABULA RASA                       │
      │   "Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say,      │
      │    white paper, void of all characters, without        │
      │    any ideas:—How comes it to be furnished?"           │
      └───────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
                                  │
                                  ▼  ALL MATERIALS DERIVE FROM:
                               EXPERIENCE
                                  │
             ┌────────────────────┴────────────────────┐
             │                                         │
             ▼                                         ▼
         SENSATION                                 REFLECTION
    External perception via senses:           Internal perception of the mind's
    yellow, white, heat, cold,                own operations: thinking, doubting,
    soft, hard, bitter, sweet                 believing, reasoning, willing
```

Locke argued that if ideas were innate, they would be universally acknowledged by all humans. Yet children, idiots, and uneducated people have no conscious grasp of logical axioms like *"Whatever is, is"* (*Essay*, I.ii.4–5). The human mind begins as a **blank slate** (*tabula rasa* or "white paper void of all characters"), upon which sensory experience inscribes all knowledge.

[^locke-essay-ref]: John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, edited by Peter H. Nidditch (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1975). See [[Bibliography#Locke-Essay]].

## 2. Simple and Complex Ideas

Locke traces the mind's cognitive contents through two phases:
1. **Simple Ideas**: The uncompounded, basic sensory building blocks passively received by the mind through Sensation (e.g., the coldness and hardness of an ice cube) or Reflection (e.g., the feeling of willing). The mind cannot invent a single simple idea from scratch, just as a human cannot imagine a totally new primary color.
2. **Complex Ideas**: Formed when the active intellect combines, compares, and abstracts simple ideas:
   * **Substances**: Combinations of simple ideas representing distinct particular things (e.g., gold, a horse, a human being), supported by an unknown, underlying substratum (*"a something I know not what"*, *Essay*, II.xxiii.2).
   * **Modes**: Complex ideas that do not exist by themselves but are dependencies of substances (e.g., triangle, murder, gratitude).
   * **Relations**: Comparing one idea with another (e.g., father and son, bigger and smaller, cause and effect).

## 3. Primary versus Secondary Qualities

To align empiricist philosophy with the mechanical physics of Boyle and Newton, Locke drew a fundamental distinction between the properties of physical bodies:

```
               PRIMARY QUALITIES vs. SECONDARY QUALITIES
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────┐
 │          Primary Qualities           │         Secondary Qualities          │
 ├──────────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ Inherent in the body itself          │ Merely powers in the body to produce │
 │ Inseparable from matter at any scale │ sensations in us via primary qualities│
 │ Resemblance: Our ideas resemble the  │ No Resemblance: Nothing like our     │
 │ actual physical property             │ sensations exists in the object      │
 │ E.g., Size, Shape, Motion, Rest,     │ E.g., Color, Sound, Taste, Smell,    │
 │ Solidity, Texture, Number            │ Warmth, Cold                         │
 └──────────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────┘
```

A grain of wheat chopped into microscopic parts retains solidity, extension, figure, and mobility (Primary Qualities). But the sweetness of a pineapple, the redness of a rose, and the warmth of a fire (Secondary Qualities) do not exist in the physical objects themselves; they are subjective sensations generated in human consciousness by the microscopic impact of corpuscles upon our sensory organs.

## 4. Personal Identity as Continuity of Consciousness

In Chapter 27 of Book II (added to the second edition of the *Essay* in 1694), Locke formulated the modern theory of **Personal Identity**:
* Locke sharply distinguished between the identity of a **Man** (a biological human animal) and the identity of a **Person** (a thinking, intelligent being that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places).
* Personal identity is constituted neither by the persistence of an immaterial Cartesian soul (*res cogitans*) nor by an identical biological body, but by **Continuity of Consciousness and Memory**:

$$\text{Personal Identity extends as far backward as consciousness can be extended to any past action or thought.}$$

Locke proved this through his famous **Prince and the Cobbler** thought experiment (*Essay*, II.xxvii.15):
* If the soul and consciousness of a prince were transplanted into the body of a cobbler whose soul had departed, everyone sees that he would be the *same person* as the prince, though he would be a *different man*.
* This revolutionary psychological reduction of identity directly anticipated modern thought experiments on mind-uploading, brain transplants, and Derek Parfit's reductionism.

## 5. Political Philosophy: Natural Rights and the Social Contract

In the *Second Treatise of Government* (1689)[^locke-treatises-ref], Locke provided the philosophical foundation of modern constitutional government:
* **The State of Nature**: Unlike Hobbes's state of perpetual war of all against all, Locke's state of nature is a state of perfect equality and freedom governed by the natural Law of Reason, which dictates that no one ought to harm another in their **Life, Health, Liberty, or Possessions**.
* **The Labor Theory of Property**: A person acquires private property in unowned land and natural resources by **mixing their labor** with it, provided there is *"enough, and as good, left in common for others"* (the Lockean Proviso, *Second Treatise*, §27).
* **The Social Contract and the Right of Revolution**: Government is created by the voluntary consent of the governed for the sole purpose of protecting their pre-existing natural rights. If a sovereign becomes tyrannical and systematically violates life, liberty, or property, the government forfeits its legitimacy, the contract is dissolved, and the citizens retain the moral **Right of Revolution**.

[^locke-treatises-ref]: John Locke, *Two Treatises of Government*, edited by Peter Laslett (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1988).
