# Philippa Foot

<div className="dropcap">

**Philippa Foot** (née Bosanquet, 1920–2010) was a preeminent British moral philosopher who taught for decades at Somerville College, Oxford, and the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA). Alongside her close friends and contemporaries G.E.M. Anscombe, Iris Murdoch, and Mary Midgley, Foot spearheaded the resurgence of **Neo-Aristotelian [[Virtue Ethics]]** and revolutionized contemporary moral theory by inventing **[[The Trolley Problem]]**[^foot-trolley-ref].

</div>

Foot waged a lifelong philosophical battle against the subjectivist, emotivist orthodoxy of Oxford mid-century philosophy (represented by R.M. Hare and A.J. Ayer), demonstrating that moral evaluations are grounded in objective biological facts about human flourishing and species nature.

---

## 1. The Invention of the Trolley Problem (1967)

In her landmark 1967 paper *"The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect"*[^foot-trolley-ref], Foot introduced a hypothetical scenario that became the most widely discussed thought experiment in modern moral philosophy:

```
                      FOOT'S ORIGINAL TROLLEY SCENARIO
       ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
       │   THE RUNAWAY TRAM                                     │
       │   - Brakes fail on a tram careening down the tracks.   │
       │   - Straight ahead: 5 track workers bound to die.      │
       │   - Side spur: 1 track worker.                         │
       │   - Driver/bystander can turn the wheel to the spur.   │
       └───────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
       ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
       │   IS IT MORALLY PERMISSIBLE TO DIVERT THE TRAM?        │
       │   Intuition: Overwhelmingly YES.                       │
       └───────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
                                   │  CONTRASTED WITH:
                                   ▼
       ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
       │   THE HEALTHY HOSPITAL VISITOR                         │
       │   - 5 dying hospital patients need organs.             │
       │   - 1 healthy visitor enters for a check-up.           │
       │   - Can a surgeon kill the visitor to harvest organs?  │
       │   Intuition: Overwhelmingly NO.                        │
       └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Why is it permissible to steer the tram to kill one instead of five, but strictly impermissible for a surgeon to harvest organs from one innocent patient to save five?

Foot explained this divergence not by crude utilitarian numbers, but by distinguishing between **Negative and Positive Duties**:
* **Negative Duty**: The strict duty *not to inflict harm or kill* (non-maleficence).
* **Positive Duty**: The less stringent duty *to provide aid or save life* (beneficence).
* In the hospital, the surgeon faces a conflict between the negative duty not to kill the visitor versus the positive duty to save the five patients. The negative duty to avoid killing strictly trumps the positive duty to save.
* In the tram dilemma, the driver is already driving a lethal weapon; the choice is between harming five or harming one (a conflict between two negative duties), permitting the minimization of injury. See the full analysis in [[The Trolley Problem]].

[^foot-trolley-ref]: Philippa Foot, "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect," *Oxford Review* 5 (1967): 5–15. See [[Bibliography#Foot-Trolley-1967]].

## 2. The Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE)

Foot's paper also provided the definitive modern analysis of the classical Catholic **Doctrine of Double Effect**:
* There is a vital moral distinction between **intended consequences** (what an agent aims at as an end or chooses as a means) and **foreseen side-effects** (consequences that an agent foresees will occur but does not aim at).
* Diverting the trolley foreseeably results in the death of the one worker, but the agent does not *aim* at his death as a means (if the worker miraculously jumped off the track, the goal of saving the five would still be achieved).
* In contrast, pushing a man off a footbridge or carving up a healthy visitor intentionally uses their bodily destruction as an indispensable physical means, which is strictly prohibited.

## 3. *Natural Goodness* (2001)

In her culminating masterpiece, *Natural Goodness* (2001)[^foot-natural-ref], Foot articulated an objective, naturalistic foundation for virtue ethics modeled on the concept of **Species Life-Forms** (*Aristotelian Categoricals*):
* To evaluate a living creature as "good" or "defective" is not an arbitrary personal endorsement; it is an objective assessment relative to the characteristic life-cycle of its species.
* A wolf that abandons its pack or a bee that refuses to dance to signal nectar is objectively defective: its behavior impairs the natural flourishing of its life-form.
* Similarly, human virtues (justice, courage, temperance, charity) are not social conventions or subjective feelings. Human beings are social, rational creatures who require virtues to raise offspring, sustain cooperation, establish trust, and flourish:

$$\text{"Virtues are natural excellences necessary for human beings to achieve their characteristic flourishing (eudaimonia)."}$$

[^foot-natural-ref]: Philippa Foot, *Natural Goodness* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 2001). See [[Bibliography#Foot-Natural-2001]].
