# Philosopher: Ludwig Wittgenstein

> "Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language."

## Biography Summary

<div className="dropcap">

Ludwig Wittgenstein was an Austrian-British philosopher widely regarded as one of the most significant and enigmatic thinkers of the twentieth century. Born into one of the wealthiest industrial dynasties in Vienna, Wittgenstein studied aeronautical engineering in Manchester before moving to Cambridge to study the foundations of mathematics under Bertrand Russell. After serving on the front lines of World War I, where he completed his groundbreaking *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921), Wittgenstein gave away his inherited fortune and worked as an elementary school teacher and architect, believing he had solved all philosophical problems. Returning to Cambridge in 1929, he repudiated his early doctrines, creating an entirely new philosophy of language published posthumously as *Philosophical Investigations* (1953).

## Key Philosophical Contributions

Wittgenstein's thought is divided into two distinct, fundamentally opposed epochs:

### 1. The Early Philosophy: *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921)
In the *Tractatus*, Wittgenstein presented a rigid logical atomism that inspired the logical positivists:
* **The Picture Theory of Meaning**: A proposition is a logical "picture" of reality. Just as a scale model in a courtroom depicts a traffic accident, a proposition depicts a possible state of affairs (*Sachverhalt*) because they share an identical logical form.
* **The Boundary of Sense**: Language can state only empirical, factual states of affairs in the natural world. Metaphysical, ethical, and aesthetic statements do not state facts and are therefore strictly nonsensical (*unsinnig*).
* **Saying vs. Showing**: Ethics, aesthetics, and the mystical cannot be spoken in propositional language; they *show* themselves. The book concludes with its famous seventh proposition:
  $$\text{"Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." (Wovon man nicht sprechen kann, darüber muss man schweigen.)}$$[^witt-tlp]

### 2. The Late Philosophy: *Philosophical Investigations* (1953)
In the *Investigations*, Wittgenstein dismantled the *Tractatus*' assumption that language possesses a single, crystalline logical essence:
* **Meaning as Use**: For a large class of cases, *"the meaning of a word is its use in the language."* Words are not labels attached to mental ideas or external objects; they are tools in a toolbox, each with distinct functions.[^witt-pi]
* **Language-Games (*Sprachspiele*)**: Language consists of an indefinite, evolving multiplicity of activities woven into human life—giving orders, telling jokes, describing objects, praying, cursing, speculating. These activities are embedded in **Forms of Life** (*Lebensformen*).
* **Family Resemblance**: Words like "game" (*Spiel*) cannot be defined by a single essential feature shared by all games. Instead, they share a network of overlapping, criss-crossing similarities—like family resemblances across generations.
* **The Private Language Argument**: Wittgenstein demonstrated that a language whose words refer to private, inaccessible sensations (knowable only to the speaker) is impossible. Without public, communal criteria for correctness, the distinction between *seeming* to follow a rule and *actually* following a rule collapses.
* **Philosophy as Therapy**: Philosophical problems are not factual mysteries to be solved by empirical science, but deep intellectual cramps caused by language idling—*"when language goes on holiday."* The philosopher's task is to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle.

## Major Works

1. *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921) — Logical atomism, picture theory of language, and the limits of sense.
2. *Philosophical Investigations* (1953, posthumous) — Language-games, meaning-as-use, and the private language argument.
3. *The Blue and Brown Books* (1958) — Preliminary lecture notes for the *Investigations*.
4. *On Certainty* (1969) — Epistemological notes on hinge propositions and skepticism.
