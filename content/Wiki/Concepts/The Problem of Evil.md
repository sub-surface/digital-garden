# The Problem of Evil

<div className="dropcap">

**The Problem of Evil** is the most enduring and formidable intellectual challenge directed against classical theism. In its traditional formulation, attributed by Lactantius to Epicurus and articulated with skepticism by [[David Hume]] in *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779)[^hume-dialogues], the problem interrogates how the horrific reality of human and animal suffering can be reconciled with a creator who is simultaneously **Omnipotent** (all-powerful), **Omniscient** (all-knowing), and **Omnibenevolent** (wholly good).

</div>

As Hume famously crystallized the ancient riddle:
> "Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?" (*Dialogues*, Part X)

In modern analytic philosophy, the problem is divided into two distinct formulations: the **Logical Problem of Evil** (which claims theism is logically contradictory) and the **Evidential Problem of Evil** (which claims that the sheer quantity and gratuitousness of suffering renders theism vastly improbable).

## 1. The Logical Problem of Evil: Mackie's Inconsistent Triad

In his 1955 paper *"Evil and Omnipotence"*[^mackie-ref], Oxford philosopher J.L. Mackie argued that the core doctrines of traditional monotheism are logically incompatible:

```
                       MACKIE'S INCONSISTENT TRIAD
                        ┌────────────────────────┐
                        │      1. GOD IS         │
                        │     OMNIPOTENT         │
                        └──────────┬─────────────┘
                                   │
                    Can prevent all evil easily
                                   │
           ┌───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
           │                                               │
 ┌─────────▼──────────────┐                     ┌──────────▼─────────────┐
 │      2. GOD IS         │ ◄─── CONTRADICTION ──► │     3. EVIL EXISTS     │
 │    OMNIBENEVOLENT      │   (Cannot hold all 3)  │  (Suffering, cruelty,  │
 │ (Wants to end evil)    │                        │       disease)         │
 └────────────────────────┘                        └────────────────────────┘
```

The logical argument runs as follows:
1. God is omnipotent (God can eliminate or prevent any state of affairs that is logically possible).
2. God is wholly good (a wholly good being eliminates evil as far as it can).
3. Evil exists in the world.
4. If a wholly good being eliminates evil as far as it can, and an omnipotent being can eliminate all evil, then if God existed, evil would not exist.
5. Therefore, an omnipotent, wholly good God does not exist.

For Mackie, theism cannot merely retreat into claiming that "evil is a mystery"; a logical contradiction dismantles the coherent intelligibility of the theistic concept.

[^mackie-ref]: J.L. Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence," *Mind* 64, no. 254 (1955): 200–212. See [[Bibliography#Mackie-Evil-1955]].

## 2. Plantinga's Free Will Defense

The most widely accepted philosophical reply to the *logical* problem of evil was developed by Christian philosopher **Alvin Plantinga** in *The Nature of Necessity* (1974)[^plantinga-ref].

Plantinga noted that to show a logical contradiction between propositions (1), (2), and (3), the atheist must establish that there is *no possible state of affairs* in which God has a morally sufficient reason to permit evil. Plantinga offered a logically consistent model: **The Free Will Defense**.

```
                   PLANTINGA'S FREE WILL LOGIC
 ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ 1. Significant moral freedom requires the capacity to choose between   │
 │    genuine moral good and genuine moral evil.                          │
 ├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ 2. It is a logical impossibility even for an omnipotent being to       │
 │    *force* an agent to *freely* choose the good.                       │
 │    (Forced freedom is an oxymoron, like a four-sided triangle).        │
 ├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ 3. A world containing free creatures who perform moral goods is vastly  │
 │    superior to a clockwork automaton world with no freedom.            │
 ├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ 4. Through "Transworld Depravity," it is possible that in every world   │
 │    containing significantly free creatures, creatures go wrong and     │
 │    commit moral evil.                                                  │
 └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Because an omnipotent deity cannot perform logical contradictions (such as creating married bachelors or uncreated creatures), God cannot simultaneously grant creatures libertarian free will and guarantee that they will never perform evil actions. 

Philosophical consensus largely acknowledges that Plantinga successfully neutralized the *logical* problem of evil: the existence of God and the existence of evil are not strictly logically contradictory.

[^plantinga-ref]: Alvin Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1974), Chapter 9. See [[Bibliography#Plantinga-God-1974]].

## 3. The Evidential Problem of Evil: William Rowe's Fawn

With the defeat of the logical version, debate shifted decisively to the **Evidential (or Inductive) Problem of Evil**, formulated by **William L. Rowe** in 1979[^rowe-ref].

Rowe argued that even if God's existence is logically compatible with *some* evil (to preserve free will), the actual universe contains instances of **Gratuitous Suffering**—suffering so horrific and pointless that no outweighing good or prevention of worse evil could possibly justify it.

Rowe illustrated this with the poignant case of **The Fawn in the Forest Fire**:
> "In some distant forest lightning strikes a dead tree, resulting in a forest fire. In the fire a fawn is trapped, horribly burned, and lies in agony for several days before death relieves its suffering."

```
                     ROWE'S EVIDENTIAL ARGUMENT
 ┌───┬────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ 1 │ There exist instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent,        │
 │   │ omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some      │
 │   │ greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse.             │
 ├───┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ 2 │ An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent the occurrence of any   │
 │   │ intense suffering of that sort, unless it could not do so without      │
 │   │ thereby losing a greater good or permitting an equal/worse evil.       │
 ├───┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ 3 │ Therefore, there does not exist an omnipotent, omniscient,             │
 │   │ wholly good being.                                                     │
 └───┴────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The fawn's agonizing death in an uninhabited wilderness serves no disciplinary purpose, tests no human moral character, and results from natural causes (*natural evil*) completely detached from human free will.

[^rowe-ref]: William L. Rowe, "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism," *American Philosophical Quarterly* 16, no. 4 (1979): 335–341. See [[Bibliography#Rowe-Evil-1979]].

## 4. Theodicy Frameworks

Philosophers and theologians have offered competing **theodicies** (attempts to justify the ways of God to human reason):

### A. The Soul-Making Theodicy (Irenaeus / John Hick)
In *Evil and the God of Love* (1966), John Hick argued that God did not create humanity as finished, static beings in a hedonistic paradise ("a world like a luxury hotel"). Rather, the world is designed as an environment for **"Soul-Making"**:
* To develop genuine virtues (courage, compassion, self-sacrifice, endurance), creatures must inhabit a world with real dangers, natural hazards, and genuine challenges.
* Courage cannot develop without real danger; charity is impossible without real poverty and distress.

### B. Skeptical Theism
Skeptical theists (such as Stephen Wykstra and Peter van Inwagen) attack Premise 1 of Rowe's argument by highlighting human cognitive limitations:
* Just as an infant undergoing a painful surgical vaccination cannot comprehend the parent's protective medical rationale, finite human beings have no cognitive basis to conclude that because we *cannot see* God's purpose for a particular tragedy, *no purpose exists*.
* What appears to us as gratuitous evil may be an integral link in cosmic goods entirely beyond our intellectual horizon.

## 5. The Literary, Phenomenological, & Post-Holocaust Critique

While analytic philosophy treats the problem of evil as a logical puzzle or Bayesian probability game between three divine attributes, Continental thought, post-Holocaust philosophy, and literature diagnose theodicy itself as an act of moral violence:

### 1. Ivan Karamazov's Ticket: Dostoevsky's Anti-Theodicy
In Book V, Chapter 4 of Fyodor Dostoevsky's *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880)[^dostoevsky-bk], Ivan Karamazov mounts what remains the supreme literary refutation of all future theodicies. Recounting newspaper reports of Turkish atrocities and Russian parents beating a five-year-old girl and locking her in an outhouse to smear her tears and excrement across her chest, Ivan directly confronts his pious brother Alyosha:
* Ivan does not deny God's existence or the logical possibility of an ultimate cosmic harmony.
* Rather, he rejects the **moral legitimacy of the deal**:
  > *"Listen: if everyone must suffer, in order to buy eternal harmony with their suffering, pray tell me what have children got to do with it?... It’s not God that I do not accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return him the ticket."*
* To accept a cosmic "greater good" or "soul-making" balance sheet that justifies the agony of a single tortured child is to become a moral monster. True human dignity demands refusing God's final harmony.

### 2. Levinas: The Bankruptcy of Theodicy after Auschwitz
In his 1982 essay *"Useless Suffering"* (*La souffrance inutile*)[^levinas-suffering], Jewish-French phenomenologist [[Emmanuel Levinas]] declared that the 20th century—marked by the Gulag and the Shoah—renders theodicy impossible and grotesque:
* **Suffering is Intrinsic Evil**: Pain in the Other is pure, meaningless passivity, a surplus that cannot be redeemed or converted into a higher metaphysical lesson.
* **The Obscenity of Theodicy**: Any philosophical or theological framework that attempts to find a "rational purpose" or "pedagogical benefit" in another person's torment is an unbearable act of cruelty:
  > *"Theodicy is the source of all immorality."*
* **The Ethical Shift**: When theodicy collapses, ethics is born. The only justifiable response to evil is not theological explanation, but radical, asymmetrical responsibility: taking upon oneself the burden of easing the suffering of the neighbor without asking for justification.

### 3. Hannah Arendt: The Banality of Evil
In *Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil* (1963)[^arendt-eichmann], [[Hannah Arendt]] radically restructured Western metaphysics of evil:
* Classical philosophy and theology imagined evil as demonic, monstrous, or rooted in deep Satanic malice (Macbeth, Iago, Milton's Satan).
* Examining Nazi bureaucrat Adolf Eichmann in the glass booth in Jerusalem, Arendt discovered not demonic depth, but terrifying **thoughtlessness**:
  > *"It was sheer thoughtlessness—something by no means identical with stupidity—that predisposed him to become one of the greatest criminals of that period."*
* Modern evil is **banal**: it operates through cliches, bureaucratic procedure, professional specialization, and the abdication of individual critical judgment (*Urteilskraft*) within totally administered systems.

### 4. Paul Ricœur: The Symbolism of Evil
In *The Symbolism of Evil* (1967), [[Paul Ricœur]] demonstrated that treating evil as an abstract syllogism forgets how evil is actually experienced in human culture:
* Before evil was formalized into theology, it lived in primary symbolic and mythic language: **defilement** (*souillure*, stain/pollution), **sin** (broken covenant/estrangement), and **guilt** (the internalized burden of the law).
* Genuine philosophy of evil requires a hermeneutic retrieval of myth and tragedy (from the Babylonian *Enuma Elish* to Sophocles' *Oedipus*), recognizing that human finitude is always entangled with tragedy rather than resolved by algebraic theodicies.
