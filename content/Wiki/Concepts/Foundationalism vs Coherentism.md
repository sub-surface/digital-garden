# Foundationalism vs Coherentism

<div className="dropcap">

The debate between **Foundationalism** and **Coherentism** concerns the structural architecture of epistemic justification. Whenever an agent asserts a belief as justified knowledge, they must be capable of providing a justifying reason. But that reason is itself another belief requiring justification, immediately precipitating the ancient **Epistemic Regress Problem**.

</div>

How does human justification avoid collapsing into an infinite regress, arbitrary dogmatism, or circular reasoning? Epistemologists divide into rival camps over whether knowledge is structured like an architectural pyramid anchored in unshakeable bedrock foundations (**Foundationalism**), or like an interconnected web / raft floating on the open sea (**Coherentism**).

---

## 1. The Epistemic Regress Problem and Agrippa's Trilemma

The challenge was first codified by Pyrrhonian skeptics as the **Trilemma of Agrippa** (recorded by Sextus Empiricus in *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*, I.164–177)[^agrippa-ref]:

```
                     THE REGRESS OF JUSTIFICATION
                         Belief P (Target)
                               ▲
                        Justified by Q
                               ▲
                        Justified by R
                               ▲
                        Justified by S ...
                               │
            ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
            │                  │                  │
            ▼                  ▼                  ▼
    INFINITE REGRESS      CIRCULARITY          DOGMATISM
   Chain continues to   S is justified by P  Chain halts at a
   infinity; no belief  (Circular reasoning; belief with no
   is ever justified.   begs the question).  justification.
```

If none of the three branches of the trilemma can yield rational justification, the skeptic concludes that knowledge is impossible, and one must suspend judgment (*epoché*).

[^agrippa-ref]: Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*, translated by Julia Annas and Jonathan Barnes (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2000).

---

## 2. Foundationalism: The Architectural Pyramid

**Foundationalism** resolves the regress by rejecting the third horn (dogmatism) and positing a distinction between two kinds of beliefs:
1. **Basic Beliefs (Foundational)**: Beliefs that are justified immediately and non-inferentially, without needing support from any other beliefs.
2. **Non-Basic Beliefs (Superstructure)**: Beliefs whose justification derives inferentially (deductively or inductively) from basic beliefs.

```
                   THE FOUNDATIONALIST PYRAMID
                     ┌───────────────────────┐
                     │   Non-Basic Beliefs   │  (High-level scientific theories,
                     │  (Deductions/Inferences)│ historical knowledge, predictions)
                     └───────────▲───────────┘
                                 │
                     ┌───────────┴───────────┐
                     │     BASIC BELIEFS     │  (Immediate perceptual experiences,
                     │       (BEDROCK)       │   indubitable cogito, self-evidence)
                     └───────────────────────┘
```

### Classical Foundationalism: Descartes
In his *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), [[René Descartes]] championed radical foundationalism. Demolishing all dubious opinions, he arrived at the indubitable bedrock of the ***Cogito***:
> *"I think, therefore I am"* (*Cogito ergo sum*).

From this infallible foundation, Descartes deduced the existence of an all-perfect God, and from God's benevolence, the reliability of clear and distinct perceptions of the external physical world.

### The Myth of the Given
In his influential 1956 essay *"Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind"*, American analytic philosopher **Wilfrid Sellars** launched a famous assault on foundationalism known as the **Critique of the Myth of the Given**:
* Can a raw, non-conceptual sensory episode (e.g., photons hitting the retina or a bare patch of red) serve as the justifying reason for a conceptual propositional belief?
* Sellars argued that a raw physical sensation is a causal state, not an epistemic reason. Only something that is already propositional and situated within the *"logical space of reasons"* can justify another belief. Foundationalists confuse a *causal antecedent* of belief with a *justifying reason* for belief.

## 3. Coherentism: The Web and the Raft

**Coherentism** accepts the second horn of Agrippa's Trilemma, denying that circularity is vicious if the circle is sufficiently vast, rich, and mutually reinforcing. There are no foundational basic beliefs:

$$\text{A belief is justified if and only if it coheres within a comprehensive, harmonious system of beliefs.}$$

```
                      THE COHERENTIST WEB
                           [Belief A]
                          /    │    \
                 [Belief B]────┼────[Belief C]
                          \    │    /
                         [Belief D]
```

### Key Dimensions of Coherence
A set of beliefs coheres not merely by avoiding logical contradictions (consistency), but through:
1. **Mutual Entailment and Explanatory Power**: Beliefs explain and illuminate one another.
2. **Connectivity**: Dense mutual links connecting disparate domains.
3. **Simplicity and Comprehensiveness**: The system unifies broad sensory observations under elegant laws.

### Neurath's Boat
In his dispute with the foundationalist protocol sentences of [[Logical Positivism]], Otto Neurath famously proposed the nautical metaphor:
> "We are like sailors who must rebuild their ship on the open sea, without ever being able to dismantle it in dry-dock and reconstruct it from the best materials."

We cannot step outside our system of beliefs onto dry land to inspect its foundations; we can only repair individual planks of belief while relying on the remaining structure to keep us afloat. Similarly, W.V.O. Quine described knowledge as a seamless **Web of Belief** (*"Two Dogmas of Empiricism"*, 1951), where experience touches the periphery and adjustments reverberate through the interior.

## 4. Susan Haack's Synthesis: Foundherentism

To overcome the weaknesses of both extremes—foundationalism's vulnerability to the Myth of the Given and coherentism's "isolation problem" (the possibility of a beautifully coherent fictional fairy tale completely detached from empirical reality)—philosopher **Susan Haack** formulated **Foundherentism** in *Evidence and Inquiry* (1993)[^haack-ref].

```
                  THE CROSSWORD PUZZLE ANALOGY
            ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
            │          CLUES (Experiential Input)          │
            │   Like empirical sensory evidence touching   │
            │   the senses. Gives directional constraint.  │
            └──────────────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
            ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
            │        INTERSECTING ENTRIES (Coherence)      │
            │   Words intersect and support each other.    │
            │   If an entry clashes across words, we re-   │
            │   evaluate both the clue reading and answers.│
            └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Haack compares empirical justification to a **Crossword Puzzle**:
1. **The Clues** represent the non-belief, experiential inputs of perception (giving foundational anchor to the world).
2. **The Intersecting Entries** represent the mutual coherence of our beliefs with each other.
3. How reasonable it is to accept a filled-in word depends *both* on how well it fits its clue *and* on how well it meshes with the crossing letters provided by other words.

Foundherentism demonstrates that experiential evidence can directly modulate belief justification without requiring that basic beliefs be infallible or immune to revision.

[^haack-ref]: Susan Haack, *Evidence and Inquiry: Towards Reconstruction in Epistemology* (Oxford: Blackwell, 1993). See [[Bibliography#Haack-Evidence-1993]].
