# The Violinist

> **Judith Jarvis Thomson**, "A Defense of Abortion", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1, no. 1 (1971): 47–66[^thomson-violinist-ed].

<div className="dropcap">

Published in 1971, Judith Jarvis Thomson's **The Violinist** is universally recognized as the most influential and widely debated thought experiment in contemporary applied ethics and rights theory. Prior to Thomson, the ethical debate concerning abortion centered almost exclusively on whether the fetus constitutes a human person with a right to life. Thomson restructured the entire debate by granting the conservative premise *for the sake of argument*—assuming that a fetus is a full moral person from conception—and demonstrating that a **right to life does not entail the right to the non-consensual use of another person's body**.

</div>

## I. The Scenario

```
                              THE SOCIETY OF MUSIC LOVERS
                         ┌───────────────────────────────────┐
                         │   UNCONSCIOUS FAMOUS VIOLINIST    │
                         │      (Fatal Kidney Failure)       │
                         └─────────────────┬─────────────────┘
                                           │
                              SURGICALLY LINKED CIRCULATION
                              (Involuntary 9-month hookup)
                                           │
                         ┌─────────────────▼─────────────────┐
                         │         THE KIDNAPPED YOU         │
                         │   (Innocent party in hospital)    │
                         └───────────────────────────────────┘
```

You wake up in a hospital bed in the morning to discover you have been back-to-back connected to an unconscious man. He is a world-renowned, virtuoso violinist who has suffered catastrophic kidney failure. 

The Society of Music Lovers, having audited all available medical records, discovered that you alone possess the exact rare blood type capable of filtering his blood. The previous night, they kidnapped you and surgically linked your circulatory system to his: your kidneys are currently cleansing his bloodstream as well as your own.

The hospital director arrives and explains:
> *"We are deeply sorry the Society did this; had we known, we would have prevented it. But the deed is done. The violinist is now plugged into you. If you unplug him now, he will die immediately. It is only for nine months. By then he will have recovered from his ailment and can safely be detached."*

Thomson asks: **Is it morally obligatory for you to remain in that bed plugged into the violinist for nine months?**

Virtually all moral intuitions answer: **No.** While staying plugged in would be an act of heroic generosity, it is not your moral duty; you have a moral right to unplug yourself, even knowing that doing so results in the violinist's immediate death.

## II. The Logical Structure of the Dilemma

Thomson formalizes the classical anti-abortion syllogism and exposes its hidden premise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Premise 1:} & \quad \text{Every person has a right to life.} \\
\text{Premise 2:} & \quad \text{The violinist is a person.} \\
\text{Premise 3:} & \quad \text{Therefore, the violinist has a right to life.} \\
\text{Premise 4:} & \quad \text{You have a right to decide what happens in and to your own body.} \\
\text{Classical Claim:} & \quad \text{The right to life is strictly weightier than the right to bodily autonomy.} \\
\text{Conclusion:} & \quad \text{Therefore, you may not unplug yourself.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Thomson shows that the inference fails because it relies on an incoherent conception of what a **Right to Life** entails.

## III. What Does the Right to Life Entail?

Thomson analyzes two possible definitions of the right to life:

| Definition | Content | Application to the Violinist | Moral Verdict |
|------------|---------|------------------------------|---------------|
| **Positive Account** | The right to be given at least the bare minimum one needs for continued survival | The violinist needs your kidneys; therefore he has a right to your kidneys | **False**: You have no obligation to yield your internal organs |
| **Negative Account** | The right **not to be killed unjustly** (*Right against unjust killing*) | Unplugging denies him what he has no moral claim to use | **True**: Unplugging kills him, but does not kill him *unjustly* |

### 1. The Henry Fonda Counterexample
To illustrate that the right to life does not grant a claim on others' assistance:
> If I were mortally ill in California, and the only thing required to save my life were the soothing touch of **Henry Fonda's cool hand** on my fevered brow, I still have no moral right against Henry Fonda that he fly across the country to touch my forehead. If he refuses, he may be callous, but he does not violate my right to life.

### 2. The Right to Withhold Bodily Support
Unplugging the violinist does not kill him by active battery; it merely ceases the provision of bodily sustenance to which he has no entitlement. The violinist has a right to life against being stabbed or poisoned, but **no person has a right against you that you sustain their life at severe physical cost to yourself without your ongoing consent**.

## IV. Further Permutations & Objections

Thomson anticipates several key objections and constructs targeted companion thought experiments:

### 1. The People-Seeds (Voluntary Behavior & Risk)
*Objection*: The violinist scenario only models pregnancy resulting from non-consensual rape. What if pregnancy occurs despite contraception?

*Thomson's Response*: Imagine that human seeds drift through the air like pollen. You open your living room window to air the room, having fitted fine-mesh screens over the frames. In a rare freak occurrence, a defective screen allows a single seed to slip through and take root in your carpet:
* Did you give that person-plant the right to use your house simply because you opened your window knowing that screens sometimes fail?
* Thomson argues: **No.** Taking reasonable preventive measures demonstrates that you did not confer consent or bodily invitation.

### 2. The Limits of Samaritanism
Thomson distinguishes between levels of moral conduct:
* **The Good Samaritan**: Goes out of his way at substantial personal risk to rescue a dying stranger.
* **The Minimally Decent Samaritan**: Does what is basic, decent, and requires minimal personal sacrifice.
* Thomson observes that under the law in virtually all Western democracies, no man is legally compelled to be even a Minimally Decent Samaritan (one is not legally obligated to cross the street to pull a drowning baby from a three-inch puddle). Restricting bodily autonomy would force women by law to be **Splendid Samaritans**—a legal and moral double-standard imposed on no other class of citizens.

## V. The Phenomenological, Care Ethics, & Literary Critiques

While Thomson's thought experiment became the gold standard of analytic rights theory, feminist phenomenologists, care ethicists, and literary critics have challenged the fundamental ontological assumptions baked into the violinist scenario:

### 1. The Phenomenological Critique: Pregnancy as Split Subjectivity
In *Throwing Like a Girl: And Other Essays in Feminist Philosophy and Social Theory* (1990)[^young-pregnant], feminist phenomenologist **Iris Marion Young** argued that Thomson's analogy profoundly mischaracterizes female embodiment:
* **The Chassis Fallacy**: Thomson pictures the pregnant person as an adult Cartesian ego whose physical body is a private house or medical chassis into which a strange, fully formed adult intruder has been mechanically plugged with rubber hoses.
* **The Lived Body of Gestation**: From the perspective of [[Maurice Merleau-Ponty|Merleau-Ponty's]] lived body (*corps propre*), pregnancy is an **ontological split-subjectivity**:
  > *"Pregnancy challenges the Cartesian picture of a sovereign, bounded subject inside a self-enclosed container. The fetus is neither an external stranger occupying one's house, nor a simple part of one's own body (like an appendix); it is an emerging life within one's own bodily horizon."*
* By reducing pregnancy to an involuntary dialysis session with an alien stranger, analytic rights theory erases the bodily reality of gestation in favor of an artificial contract model between two detached adults.

### 2. Care Ethics & The Communitarian Critique
In *In a Different Voice* (1982)[^gilligan-voice], **Carol Gilligan** argued that Thomson's argument epitomizes the masculine "justice orientation" of modern liberal rights theory:
* **The Atomistic Presupposition**: Society is envisioned as a collection of isolated, unencumbered monads who owe nothing to one another unless bound by prior contractual agreement (Michael Sandel's critique of the "unencumbered self").
* **Relational Ontology**: An **ethics of care** maintains that human beings are always already embedded in unchosen relationships of vulnerability, dependency, and bodily interdependence. Morality cannot be exhausted by negative rights against battery; it requires attending to concrete relational contexts and responsibilities that precede formal legal contracts.

### 3. The Literary Uncanny & Medical Body Horror
At a literary level, the power of Thomson's scenario derives from its grotesque, uncanny surrealism:
* The sudden morning awakening in an antiseptic hospital room, surgically bound back-to-back to an unconscious violinist by the mysterious "Society of Music Lovers," echoes the nightmarish bureaucratic apparatuses of [[Franz Kafka]] (*In the Penal Colony*) and the visceral medical estrangement of David Cronenberg's body horror (*Dead Ringers*).
* The scenario dramatizes modern medicine's capacity to reduce the living human flesh into a passive biomechanical resource—an extraction site where the boundaries of personal identity are violated by institutional fiat.
