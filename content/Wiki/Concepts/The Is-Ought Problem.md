# The Is-Ought Problem

<div className="dropcap">

**The Is-Ought Problem** (frequently termed **Hume's Guillotine** or **Hume's Law**) is one of the most celebrated and intractable problems in meta-ethics. First formulated by Scottish Enlightenment philosopher [[David Hume]] in Book III of *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739)[^hume-treatise-ref], the problem asserts that no valid logical deduction can move from purely descriptive premises concerning how things *are* to normative or prescriptive conclusions concerning how things *ought* to be.

</div>

Any argument that attempts to infer a moral obligation, right, or duty purely from natural, scientific, metaphysical, or empirical facts commits a logical non sequitur unless an unstated normative premise is already assumed.

---

## 1. Hume's Original Formulation

Hume presented his challenge in a famous passage near the end of Book III, Part I, Section I of the *Treatise*:

> "In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*.  
> This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this *ought*, or *ought not*, expresses some new relation or affirmation, 'tis necessary that it shou'd be observ'd and explain'd; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, **how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it**." (*THN* 3.1.1.27)[^hume-treatise-ref]

```
                     HUME'S GUILLOTINE: THE LOGICAL GAP
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────┐     ┌──────────────────────────────────────┐
 │         DESCRIPTIVE DOMAIN           │     │          NORMATIVE DOMAIN            │
 │               ("IS")                 │     │              ("OUGHT")               │
 ├──────────────────────────────────────┤     ├──────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ - Biological facts (evolution, DNA)  │     │ - Moral obligations and duties       │
 │ - Physical laws (gravity, neurons)   │ ──X │ - Human rights and justice           │
 │ - Sociological customs and laws      │     │ - Concepts of intrinsic goodness     │
 │ - Psychological drives and pleasures │     │ - Prescriptive imperatives           │
 └──────────────────────────────────────┘     └──────────────────────────────────────┘
                          ▲
                          │
             LOGICAL LEAP IMPERMISSIBLE
        (No "ought" in the conclusion without
           an "ought" in the premises)
```

In standard deductive logic, a valid conclusion cannot contain novel terms or relations that are wholly absent from its premises. Because "ought" introduces a fundamentally distinct evaluative modality, descriptive statements alone cannot logically entail it.

[^hume-treatise-ref]: David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, edited by David Fate Norton and Mary J. Norton (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2000), 302. See [[Bibliography#Hume-Treatise]].

## 2. The Naturalistic Fallacy and G.E. Moore

In *Principia Ethica* (1903)[^moore-ref], British philosopher G.E. Moore formulated a closely allied critique termed the **Naturalistic Fallacy**:
* Moral philosophers repeatedly attempt to define the fundamental ethical concept **"Good"** in terms of some natural property (e.g., *Good is pleasure*, *Good is evolutionary adaptation*, *Good is the desire of the majority*).
* Moore argued that "Good" is a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property, analogous to the color "Yellow." Just as you cannot explain what yellow is to someone who has never seen it by describing light wavelengths, you cannot define the good through physical descriptions.

### The Open Question Argument
Moore proved this through the **Open Question Argument**:
1. Take any proposed naturalistic definition of Good: *"Good means that which produces pleasure."*
2. Consider the question: *"Action X produces pleasure, but is X good?"*
3. If "good" literally meant "pleasure," this question would be a closed, trivial tautology (like asking: *"John is an unmarried man, but is he a bachelor?"*).
4. Because the question remains genuinely **open**, meaningful, and intelligible, the concept of goodness cannot be identical with pleasure or any other natural property.

[^moore-ref]: G.E. Moore, *Principia Ethica* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1903).

## 3. Searle's Counter-Example: Institutional Facts

The most celebrated attempt to breach Hume's Guillotine in modern philosophy was mounted by **John Searle** in his 1964 paper *"How to Derive 'Ought' from 'Is'"*[^searle-ref].

Searle distinguished between **Brute Facts** (physical facts existing independently of human conventions, like rocks and gravity) and **Institutional Facts** (facts existing only within a framework of constitutive social rules, like money, marriage, and baseball):

```
                     SEARLE'S DERIVATION FROM "IS" TO "OUGHT"
 ┌───┬───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┬──────────┐
 │ 1 │ Jones uttered the words: "I hereby promise to pay you $5" │ Is-fact  │
 ├───┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┤
 │ 2 │ Jones promised to pay Smith $5                            │ Is-fact  │
 ├───┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┤
 │ 3 │ Jones placed himself under an obligation to pay Smith $5  │ Is-fact  │
 ├───┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┤
 │ 4 │ Jones is under an obligation to pay Smith $5              │ Is-fact  │
 ├───┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┤
 │ 5 │ Jones ought to pay Smith $5                               │ OUGHT!   │
 └───┴───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┴──────────┘
```

Searle argued that within the constitutive rules of the human institution of promising, making a promise *definitionally* means placing oneself under an obligation; therefore, premise 5 follows validly from premise 1 without importing an external moral premise.

Critics (such as R.M. Hare and Antony Flew) replied that Searle's argument still conceals an unstated normative premise: *"One ought to uphold the institution of promising"* or *"One ought to do what one is under an obligation to do."* If an agent simply rejects the institution of promising (as a nihilist or outlaw might), no "ought" binds them.

[^searle-ref]: John R. Searle, "How to Derive 'Ought' from 'Is'," *The Philosophical Review* 73, no. 1 (1964): 43–58.

## 4. Meta-Ethical Ramifications

The is-ought gap lies at the center of the fundamental divide in meta-ethics:
* **Moral Non-Cognitivism & Emotivism**: [[Logical Positivism]] (A.J. Ayer, C.L. Stevenson) and expressivism (Simon Blackburn, Allan Gibbard) conclude that moral judgments are not factual propositions at all; they are emotional attitudes, prescriptions, or endorsements.
* **Moral Realism**: Realists argue either that moral properties are non-natural irreducible realities, or that synthetic naturalism can explain ethical facts without requiring a priori deductive derivation from non-moral premises.
* **Error Theory**: J.L. Mackie (*Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977) argued that moral statements do purport to describe objective facts, but because objective prescriptivity does not exist in the natural universe ("the argument from queerness"), all positive moral claims are systematically false.
