# Movement: Phenomenology

<div className="dropcap">

**Phenomenology** is a twentieth-century philosophical movement founded by [[Edmund Husserl]] and substantially transformed by [[Martin Heidegger]], [[Maurice Merleau-Ponty]], [[Jean-Paul Sartre]], and [[Emmanuel Levinas]]. Guided by the methodological maxim *"to the things themselves"* (*zu den Sachen selbst*), phenomenology brackets metaphysical assumptions regarding the objective, external existence of the world to provide a rigorous descriptive science of how phenomena appear to conscious experience from the first-person vantage point.

</div>

---

## 1. Foundational Concepts (The Husserlian Framework)

Edmund Husserl (1859–1938) developed phenomenology in his *Logical Investigations* (1900–1901) and *Ideas I* (1913) as a rigorous foundation for both natural science and philosophy:

1. **Intentionality**: Originating in the scholastic psychology of Franz Brentano, intentionality is the defining characteristic of consciousness: consciousness is never an empty container, but is always directed toward an object—consciousness is always consciousness *of* something.[^husserl-ideas]
2. **The Natural Attitude**: The unexamined, everyday presupposition that the physical world exists mind-independently around us, possessing the properties physics attributes to it.
3. **The Phenomenological Reduction (*Epoche*)**: Methodological bracketing of the natural attitude. The philosopher does not deny the existence of the external world (as in Cartesian skepticism or subjective idealism), but suspends all ontological judgment to examine pure phenomena as they are given to conscious awareness.
4. **The Lifeworld (*Lebenswelt*)**: Introduced in Husserl's late work *The Crisis of European Sciences* (1936), the lifeworld is the pre-theoretical, directly experienced horizon of practical everyday life upon which all abstract mathematical and scientific formalizations are built.

## 2. The Existential & Hermeneutic Turn

Husserl's successors rejected his goal of a transcendental, presuppositionless science of pure consciousness, turning phenomenology toward historical existence, bodily embodiment, and linguistic interpretation:

### Martin Heidegger (1889–1976): Fundamental Ontology
In *Being and Time* (1927), Heidegger redirected phenomenology from epistemology to ontology. We are not detached Cartesian spectators observing external objects, but **[[Dasein]]**—entities whose Being is an issue for them, fundamentally defined as **Being-in-the-world** (*In-der-Welt-sein*):
* Objects are encountered primarily not as theoretical entities with physical properties (*vorhanden*, present-at-hand), but as tools embedded in holistic practical contexts of involvement (*zuhanden*, ready-to-hand).
* Dasein is characterized by thrownness (*Geworfenheit*), fallenness (*Verfallenheit*), and anxiety (*Angst*), facing its inescapable mortality as Being-towards-death (*Sein-zum-Tode*).

### Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961): The Embodied Mind
In *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945), Merleau-Ponty argued that consciousness is not an ethereal disembodied mind, but is inherently embodied. The body is not a biological machine observed from outside, but the **lived body** (*le corps propre*)—the anchor through which we inhabit space, perceive objects, and engage the world.

### Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980): Existentialist Freedom
Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) distinguished between Being-in-itself (*en-soi*, non-conscious, inert matter) and Being-for-itself (*pour-soi*, conscious human reality). Consciousness is a "nihilating" void that introduces lack and choice into reality. Human beings are "condemned to be free"; pretending that one has no choice constitutes **bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*).

### Emmanuel Levinas (1906–1995): Ethics as First Philosophy
In *Totality and Infinity* (1961), Levinas redirected phenomenology from ontology toward ethics. Breaking with both Husserl's perceptual spectator and Heidegger's impersonal Being (*Sein*), Levinas demonstrated that the primary philosophical encounter is the **Face of the Other** (*le visage d'autrui*):
* The Other cannot be comprehended, conceptualized, or reduced to an object within my cognitive horizon (which Levinas terms totalitarian violence).
* The naked vulnerability of the Face speaks an absolute, primordial ethical imperative: *"Thou shalt not kill"* (*Tu ne commettras pas de meurtre*). Ethics—as asymmetrical responsibility to the neighbor—is first philosophy.

### The Deconstructive Rupture: Derrida's Critique of Voice
In *Speech and Phenomena* (1967), [[Jacques Derrida]] interrogated Husserl's foundation of phenomenology. Derrida revealed that Husserl’s attempt to base meaning on pure, unmediated self-presence in the silent inner monologue of the ego (*the living present*) is constitutively impossible: the phenomenological "present" is always already fractured by the **trace**, retention, and the differential spacing of [[Jacques Derrida#Différance|différance]].

## 3. Impact on Contemporary Cognitive Science

Phenomenology experienced an extensive contemporary revival through the rise of **Embodied, Embedded, Extended, and Enactive (4E) Cognitive Science** (Varela, Thompson, Clark, Gallagher). Thinkers challenging classical computationalism (the mind as software) draw heavily on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty to demonstrate that cognition cannot be separated from sensorimotor action within an environmental niche.
