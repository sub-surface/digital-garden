# Virtue Ethics

<div className="dropcap">

**Virtue Ethics** is a major tradition in normative moral philosophy that emphasizes the cultivation of moral character (*ēthos*), virtues of excellence (*arête*), and practical wisdom (*phronēsis*), rather than the adherence to universal rule-based duties ([[Deontology]]) or the calculation of quantitative consequences ([[Utilitarianism]]).

</div>

Where modern moral theories typically ask: *"What is the right action to perform in situation X?"*, virtue ethics poses the deeper, existential question: *"What kind of person should I become?"* An act is right not because it complies with an abstract moral rule or maximizes pleasure, but because it is what a virtuous person, possessed of practical wisdom, would characteristically do in the circumstances.

---

## 1. The Classical Aristotelian Foundation

The definitive historical foundation of virtue ethics was articulated by [[Aristotle]] in the *Nicomachean Ethics* (*Ethica Nicomachea*)[^aristotle-ne-ref]:

```
                     ARISTOTELIAN TELOS AND VIRTUE
                 ┌────────────────────────────────────┐
                 │     ERGON (Human Function)         │
                 │ Rational activity of the soul      │
                 └─────────────────┬──────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
                 ┌────────────────────────────────────┐
                 │       ARETÊ (Virtue / Excellence)  │
                 │   Dispositions of character (ēthē) │
                 │   Habituated via praxis            │
                 └─────────────────┬──────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
                 ┌────────────────────────────────────┐
                 │    EUDAIMONIA (Flourishing / Well-Being)
                 │ The supreme, self-sufficient telos │
                 └────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Function Argument (*Ergon*)
Aristotle observes that every craft, art, and tool has a characteristic function (*ergon*): a flutist's function is to play the flute well; an eye's function is to see clearly; a knife's function is to cut cleanly. If specific organs and occupations have functions, does the human being as such have a characteristic function?

Aristotle locates the distinctive human *ergon* in **the active life of the soul in accordance with reason** (*NE* I.7, 1097b24–1098a18). True human flourishing—***Eudaimonia*** (often translated as "happiness," but more accurately denoting "flourishing" or "living well")—is defined as:

$$\text{Eudaimonia} = \text{An activity of the soul in conformity with virtue (excellence), over a complete life.}$$

### The Doctrine of the Mean (*Mesotēs*)
Moral virtue (*ēthikē arētē*) is a stable disposition or state of character (*hexis*) concerned with choice, lying in a **mean** relative to us, determined by a rational principle:

$$\text{Vice of Deficiency} \quad \longleftarrow \quad \mathbf{\text{Virtue (The Mean)}} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \text{Vice of Excess}$$

| Sphere of Action / Feeling | Vice of Deficiency | **Virtuous Mean** | Vice of Excess |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fear and Confidence** | Cowardice (*deilia*) | **Courage** (*andreia*) | Rashness (*thrasytēs*) |
| **Bodily Pleasures** | Insensibility | **Temperance** (*sōphrosynē*) | Licentiousness / Self-Indulgence |
| **Wealth and Giving** | Stinginess / Illiberality | **Generosity** (*eleutheriotēs*) | Prodigality / Wastefulness |
| **Anger** | Inirascibility / Pusillanimity | **Mildness / Good Temper** | Irascibility / Hot-Headedness |
| **Social Honesty** | Self-Deprecation / Irony | **Truthfulness** (*alētheia*) | Boastfulness / Arrogance |

The mean is not an arithmetic midpoint, but context-sensitive: it means feeling passions or taking actions *"at the right times, with reference to the right objects, towards the right people, with the right motive, and in the right way"* (*NE* II.6, 1106b21).

### Practical Wisdom (*Phronēsis*)
Virtue cannot be reduced to a mechanical algorithm or codified decision procedure. It requires **Practical Wisdom** (*phronēsis*): the cultivated, perceptive capacity to discern the salient moral features of complex human situations and deliberate correctly about what promotes the good life.

[^aristotle-ne-ref]: Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, translated by Terence Irwin, 2nd ed. (Indianapolis: Hackett, 1999). See [[Bibliography#Aristotle-NE]].

## 2. Cross-Cultural Resonances: Confucian Virtue Ethics

Long before the modern Western revival, classical Chinese philosophy developed an analogous virtue tradition:
* **Kongzi (Confucius)**: Emphasized the cultivation of ***Ren*** (benevolence, humaneness) and ***Li*** (ritual propriety) through lifelong moral self-cultivation.
* **Mengzi (Mencius)**: Argued for the innate goodness of human nature (*xing shan*), identifying four moral "sprouts" (*siduan*): compassion (sprout of benevolence), shame (sprout of righteousness), deference (sprout of propriety), and sense of right and wrong (sprout of wisdom). Like physical plants, these sprouts require careful habituation and a supportive community to blossom.

## 3. The Twentieth-Century Revival

After centuries of eclipse by Enlightenment deontology and Victorian utilitarianism, modern virtue ethics was dramatically resurrected in the mid-twentieth century by a series of landmark British philosophical critiques:

### G.E.M. Anscombe and "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958)
In her incendiary paper *"Modern Moral Philosophy"*[^anscombe-ref], G.E.M. Anscombe made three radical claims:
1. All major modern moral philosophers from Butler to Mill are fundamentally misguided because they deploy concepts of moral obligation, duty, and "ought" that are derived from an ancient divine law framework (a divine lawgiver), while having abandoned belief in the divine lawgiver. Stripped of this theological architecture, notions of absolute moral obligation are psychologically and philosophically incoherent.
2. Moral philosophy should be put entirely on hold until we develop an adequate **philosophy of psychology** (clarifying concepts of intention, desire, action, and human flourishing).
3. We should abandon "moral obligation" and return to the rich vocabulary of virtues and vices pioneered by Aristotle (e.g., unjust, ungenerous, cowardly, intemperate).

[^anscombe-ref]: G.E.M. Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy," *Philosophy* 33, no. 124 (1958): 1–19. See [[Bibliography#Anscombe-MMP-1958]].

### Alasdair MacIntyre and *After Virtue* (1981)
Alasdair MacIntyre diagnosed modern moral discourse as being in a state of catastrophic disorder:
* Contemporary debates over abortion, war, and economic justice are interminable shouting matches because they employ fragmentary concepts ripped from conflicting, incommensurable traditions.
* MacIntyre restored Aristotle through the concept of **Practices** (cooperative human activities with internal goods, such as medicine, chess, architecture), the **Narrative Unity of Human Life** (understanding a life as an unfolding quest), and living **Traditions**.

### Philippa Foot and Rosalind Hursthouse
Philippa Foot (*Virtues and Vices*, 1978; *Natural Goodness*, 2001) and Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*, 1999) formalized modern neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics as a complete, viable alternative to consequentialism and deontology, showing how virtue ethics provides genuine normative guidance in concrete dilemmas such as abortion and euthanasia.

## 4. Care Ethics and Feminist Perspectives

In the 1980s, feminist philosophers (Carol Gilligan, *In a Different Voice*, 1982; Nel Noddings, *Caring*, 1984) formulated the **Ethics of Care**:
* Critiqued traditional ethical frameworks for prioritizing abstract, masculine, individualistic rights and universal rules over concrete human relationships.
* Revalued empathy, attentiveness, emotional responsiveness, and the maintenance of caring networks as primary moral virtues.
