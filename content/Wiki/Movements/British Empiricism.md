# Movement: British Empiricism

<div className="dropcap">

**British Empiricism** designates the philosophical movement that developed in the British Isles during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, centrally defined by [[John Locke]], [[George Berkeley]], and [[David Hume]], with foundational precursors in [[Francis Bacon]] and [[Thomas Hobbes]]. British Empiricism holds that human consciousness begins as an unscribed slate (*tabula rasa*), and that all ideas, concepts, and substantive knowledge are derived from sensory impression and reflective experience.

</div>

---

## 1. Core Tenets

Empiricism developed as an explicit challenge to the scholastic appeal to authority and the [[Continental Rationalism|Continental Rationalist]] doctrine of innate ideas:

1. **The Tabula Rasa**: The mind contains no innate principles or ideas prior to sensory contact with the external world. As Locke famously asked: *"Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas; how comes it to be furnished? To this I answer, in one word, from experience."*[^locke-essay]
2. **The Primacy of Perception**: Complex ideas are compounded, abstracted, and constructed entirely out of simple sensory impressions. Hume made this explicit via the **Copy Principle**: every simple idea is a faded copy of a prior simple impression.[^hume-treatise]
3. **Epistemic Modesty & Sceptical Limits**: Human knowledge is strictly bounded by the limits of actual and possible experience. A priori deductive metaphysics claiming to demonstrate the absolute nature of God, the immortality of the soul, or the ultimate substrate of matter is dismissed as speculative fiction.
4. **Naturalized Causation**: Causality is not an intelligible logical necessity perceived by pure intellect, but an empirical relation discovered through repeated observation of constant conjunction.

---

## 2. Historical Trajectory: Locke, Berkeley, and Hume

The development of British Empiricism followed a rigorous internal dialectic from realistic common sense to radical philosophical skepticism:

### John Locke (1632–1704): Representative Realism
In *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690), Locke systematically demolished the doctrine of innate ideas. He distinguished between:
* **Primary Qualities**: Inseparable from physical bodies (solidity, extension, figure, motion, number). These qualities resemble the physical configurations that produce them.
* **Secondary Qualities**: Powers in bodies to produce subjective sensations in perceivers (colors, sounds, tastes, scents), having no resemblance to the physical particles themselves.

### George Berkeley (1685–1753): Immaterialism & Subjective Idealism
In *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710), Bishop Berkeley pointed out that Locke's representative realism contained an insurmountable contradiction: if we only ever perceive ideas, we cannot know that mind-independent material bodies exist behind them. Berkeley collapsed primary qualities into secondary qualities, asserting **Immaterialism**: to exist is to be perceived (*esse est percipi*). Physical objects are collections of ideas sustained by the continuous perception of God.

### David Hume (1711–1776): Radical Empiricism & Skeptical Crisis
In *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) and the *Enquiry* (1748), Hume pursued empiricism to its logical terminus:
* **The Problem of Induction**: We cannot rationally justify inferring that the future will resemble the past. Neither deductive reasoning nor inductive appeals to past success can demonstrate the uniform course of nature without circularity.[^hume-induction]
* **The Bundle Theory of the Self**: Introspection never reveals an enduring, substantial "I", but only a fleeting bundle of ever-changing perceptions.
* **Hume's Fork**: Propositions divide exhaustively into "Relations of Ideas" (analytic, tautological truths discoverable by pure thought, like mathematics) and "Matters of Fact" (contingent empirical claims verified by observation). If a theological or metaphysical volume contains neither, Hume advised: *"Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."*

## 3. Legacy and Kant's Awakening

Hume's radical skepticism threatened to undermine the objective validity of scientific law and physical causality. It was precisely Hume's critique of causation that, in Kant's famous testimony, *"first interrupted my dogmatic slumber and gave a completely different direction to my researches in the field of speculative philosophy."* Kant's transcendental idealism was conceived to rescue science from Humean skepticism while accepting empiricism's demand that concepts without sensory intuitions remain empty.
