# Movement: Pragmatism

<div className="dropcap">

**Pragmatism** is a philosophical tradition originating in the United States in the late nineteenth century, principally developed by Charles Sanders Peirce, William James, and [[John Dewey]], and later revitalized by [[Richard Rorty]] and [[Susan Haack]]. Pragmatism holds that the meaning, validity, and truth of concepts are determined by their practical consequences, experiential operations, and problem-solving utility within ongoing human inquiry.

</div>

---

## 1. Core Tenets

Pragmatism arose as a reaction against both Cartesian foundationalism and Hegelian absolute rationalism. While varying across its classical and contemporary practitioners, the movement shares several commitments:

1. **The Instrumental Nature of Thought**: Concepts, theories, and beliefs are instruments for coping with experience and resolving problematic situations, rather than passive reflections of an external, mind-independent reality.[^dewey-logic]
2. **Fallibilism and Inquiry**: All human knowledge is provisional, subject to revision, and grounded in community inquiry. Doubt is not a Cartesian methodological exercise but a real irritation prompted by practical breakdown (Peirce's "The Fixation of Belief").
3. **Anti-Representationalism**: Rejection of the "spectator theory of knowledge" (the metaphor of mind as a mirror of nature). Truth is not a static correspondence between a proposition and an uninterpreted world, but a property of beliefs that survive empirical friction and communal scrutiny.
4. **Naturalized Anti-Skepticism**: Organisms interact continuously with their environment; philosophical problems cannot be formulated from a transcendent vantage point outside historical human practices.

## 2. Classical Epoch: The Foundational Triumvirate

### Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914)
Peirce formulated the original **Pragmatic Maxim** in 1878: *"Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object."*[^peirce-cp] Peirce insisted on logical precision, semiotics (the tripartite sign: sign-vehicle, object, interpretant), and an objective ideal of truth as the ultimate opinion that an indefinite community of inquirers would reach in the long run.

### William James (1842–1910)
James popularized pragmatism in psychology and moral philosophy, emphasizing the "cash-value" of ideas. In *Pragmatism* (1907), James argued that true ideas are those that guide us into satisfactory relations with other parts of our experience. He defended the rationality of adopting religious and existential commitments where evidence is inconclusive (*The Will to Believe*).

### John Dewey (1859–1952)
Dewey developed **Instrumentalism** and naturalistic experimentalism. In *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938), Dewey replaced the metaphysical concept of "truth" with **[[Warranted Assertibility]]**—the condition of propositions that have emerged successfully from a competent process of inquiry and have addressed the unsettled situation that prompted them.[^dewey-lti]

## 3. Neo-Pragmatism and Contemporary Debates

In the late twentieth century, the tradition split into two prominent wings:

* **Linguistic Neo-Pragmatism**: [[Richard Rorty]] combined Deweyan pragmatism with the post-positivism of Quine, Sellars, and Donald Davidson. In *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979), Rorty attacked epistemology as a foundational enterprise, advocating that philosophy be understood as an edifying, ongoing cultural conversation.
* **Epistemic Reconstruction**: [[Susan Haack]] contested Rorty's conversational relativism, defending a rigorous pragmatist realism. Haack developed **Foundherentism**—synthesizing foundationalist experiential evidence with coherentist mutual support using the metaphor of an intersecting crossword puzzle.[^haack-evidence]

---

## 4. Major Criticisms

Pragmatism has faced persistent challenges from classical realists and analytic logicians:
* **The Relativism Objection**: Bertrand Russell and early correspondence theorists argued that equating truth with utility or peer agreement erases the distinction between what is *convenient* to believe and what is *actually the case*.
* **The Fact-Value Boundary**: Realists object that inquiry cannot define what reality is; things remain true even if no inquiry ever discovers them or if all inquiry falsely rejects them.

## 5. In Phil Chat

Pragmatism—specifically in its Deweyan and Rortian formulation—is the central doctrine defended by [[Quigley|Anthony Quigley]] across eight volumes of *The Phil Chat Times*. 

During the [[The Warranted Assertibility Wars|Warranted Assertibility Wars]] (Volume VII), Quigley deployed Deweyan inquiry against [[hughchungus|Hugh's]] correspondence realism. Quigley's argument—that truth is what survives communal challenge prior to dinner, measured by [[The Six Criteria of Warrant]]—became both the server's most heavily audited defense and the etiology of [[Quigley Derangement Syndrome]]:

> *"I recognize the distinction that this is incorrect... Except I won."*
