# Movement: Existentialism

<div className="dropcap">

**Existentialism** is a major philosophical and cultural movement that came to prominence in nineteenth- and twentieth-century European thought. Beginning with the radical individualist critiques of [[Søren Kierkegaard]] and [[Friedrich Nietzsche]], and formulated as a systematic phenomenological discipline by [[Jean-Paul Sartre]], [[Simone de Beauvoir]], and [[Karl Jaspers]] in the shadow of the Second World War, existentialism takes as its starting point the concrete, lived experience of the individual human subject. Its unifying axiom—*"existence precedes essence"*—asserts that human beings are not designed according to any pre-existing divine blueprint or biological nature; rather, we first exist in the world, encounter ourselves, and must define who we are through autonomous choice and action.

## 1. Historical Lineage & Major Strands

The existentialist tradition splits along significant thematic and theological fault lines:

### A. Theistic & Christian Existentialism
* **Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855)**: The father of modern existentialism. Opposing Hegel's totalizing rationalist system, Kierkegaard argued that truth is subjectivity (*Sandheden er Subjektiviteten*). Human existence is defined by anxiety (*Angst*) before infinite possibility, requiring a radical, non-rational **Leap of Faith** into relation with God.
* **Gabriel Marcel & Martin Buber**: 20th-century religious existentialists who rejected Sartre's solipsistic portrayal of human relations, developing relational and dialogical ontologies (the I-Thou encounter).

### B. Atheistic & Phenomenological Existentialism
* **Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980)**: Systematized atheistic existentialism in *Being and Nothingness* (1943). If God does not exist, there is at least one being whose existence comes before its essence: man. Humans are *"condemned to be free"*—burdened with absolute responsibility for their choices without any moral guarantees or transcendent excuses.
* **Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986)**: The philosophical pioneer of existentialist ethics and feminism. In *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947) and *The Second Sex* (1949), Beauvoir situated existential freedom within material, historical, and patriarchal constraints, demonstrating that one's freedom is meaningless unless it aims at the liberation of the freedom of others.

## 2. Core Concepts & Philosophical Axioms

1. **Existence Precedes Essence**: Manufactured objects have an essence (a purpose, plan, or definition) that precedes their physical fabrication. Humans, however, arrive in the world with no innate purpose. We construct our essence through the sum total of our historical actions.
2. **Radical Freedom & Anguish (*L'Angoisse*)**: Freedom in existentialism is not a luxury or a political right; it is the ontological structure of consciousness. Because we cannot avoid choosing (even choosing not to choose is a choice), we experience **Anguish**—the vertigo of knowing that we alone must author our values without external justification.
3. **Facticity vs. Transcendence**:
   * **Facticity (*Facticité*)**: The given facts of our situation that we cannot change (our birth, past actions, physical body, historical epoch).
   * **Transcendence (*Transcendance*)**: The projective capacity of human consciousness to imagine alternatives, transcend circumstances, and choose future actions.
4. **Bad Faith (*Mauvaise Foi*)**: The moral and psychological failure to live authentically. Bad faith occurs when an individual flees the anguish of freedom by pretending they are a passive object governed by external forces, biology, or social roles (e.g., "I had no choice," or acting as a robotic functionary).
5. **The Look (*Le Regard*)**: The intersubjective crisis analyzed by Sartre. Encountering the gaze of another conscious being freezes my sovereign subjectivity into an object within their world, inaugurating fundamental human conflict (*"Hell is other people"*).

## 3. Key Disputes: Existentialism vs. Structuralism

In the 1960s, existentialism's hegemony over European intellectual life was challenged by **Structuralism** and **Post-Structuralism** (Claude Lévi-Strauss, [[Michel Foucault]], [[Jacques Lacan]], [[Louis Althusser]]):
* While existentialism centered on the sovereign, self-legislating individual choosing their destiny, structuralism demonstrated that human subjects are fundamentally constituted by unconscious linguistic, economic, and institutional structures.
* Foucault declared the "Death of Man"—the demise of the 19th-century humanist subject that existentialism had attempted to save.
* In response, Sartre spent his later career writing the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960), seeking a monumental synthesis between existentialist agency and Marxist historical materialism.
