# Movement: German Idealism

<div className="dropcap">

**German Idealism** refers to the philosophical epoch that emerged in Germany between the publication of [[Immanuel Kant|Immanuel Kant's]] *Critique of Pure Reason* in 1781 and the death of [[G.W.F. Hegel]] in 1831. Propelled by J.G. Fichte, F.W.J. Schelling, and Hegel, the movement sought to overcome Kant's dualism between the sensible appearance (*phenomenon*) and the unknowable thing-in-itself (*noumenon*), developing systematic metaphysical accounts of the world as the historical self-realization of Reason or Spirit (*Geist*).

</div>

---

## 1. The Post-Kantian Problematic

Kant's "Copernican Revolution" established that the mind actively structures experience through a priori concepts and pure intuitions of space and time. However, Kant preserved an unbridgeable chasm:
1. **The Inaccessible Noumenon**: The "thing-in-itself" (*Ding an sich*) causes sensations but cannot be known or conceptualized.
2. **The Subject-Object Divide**: Theoretical reason (nature, determinism) and practical reason (freedom, moral law) remained dualistically opposed.

The German Idealists judged Kant's thing-in-itself to be a self-contradictory relic. If the category of causality applies only to appearances, a thing-in-itself cannot be cited as the "cause" of sensory inputs. Philosophy, they argued, must derive the entirety of reality—both nature and mind—from a single, self-grounding principle.

---

## 2. The Developmental Dialectic

### J.G. Fichte (1762–1814): The Absolute Ego
Fichte radicalized Kantian practical philosophy in his *Wissenschaftslehre* (Doctrine of Science, 1794). He eradicated the thing-in-itself by grounding reality in the primordial activity of the **Ego** (*das Ich*):
* The Ego unconditionally posits itself (*I = I*).
* To become self-conscious, the Ego must encounter resistance; it therefore posits an external world (*das Nicht-Ich*, the non-Ego) as the field of moral duty.
* Reality is fundamentally moral striving (*Streben*).

### F.W.J. Schelling (1775–1854): The Identity Philosophy
Schelling rejected Fichte's reduction of nature to a passive obstacle for moral striving. In his *Naturphilosophie* (Philosophy of Nature), Schelling argued that nature is not dead matter, but "slumbering Spirit":
* Nature is visible Spirit; Spirit is invisible Nature.
* The Absolute is the original, indifferent identity of subjective mind and objective nature.
* Art, rather than abstract philosophy, serves as the ultimate organon wherein the conscious and unconscious harmonize.

### G.W.F. Hegel (1770–1831): Absolute Idealism
Hegel achieved the systematic climax of the movement. In the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) and the *Science of Logic* (1812–1816), Hegel famously declared: *"The real is rational, and the rational is real."*
* **The Dialectical Method**: Thought and reality advance through internal contradiction, negating finite determinations and preserving them in a higher synthesis (*Aufhebung*—sublation).
* **The Master-Slave Dialectic**: Self-consciousness is not an isolated Cartesian solitary mind, but requires recognition from another self-consciousness, attained through a struggle to the death.
* **Spirit (*Geist*) and World History**: History is the progressive consciousness of freedom. Absolute Spirit realizes itself successively through art, religion, and ultimately self-comprehending speculative philosophy.

---

## 3. Legacy and Fracturing

Following Hegel's death, the idealist consensus fractured into opposing schools:
* **Right/Old Hegelians**: Defended orthodox Protestant theology and Prussian state institutions.
* **Left/Young Hegelians**: Radical critics—including Ludwig Feuerbach and [[Karl Marx]]—who inverted Hegelian idealism into historical and dialectical materialism: *"It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness."*
* **Existential & Pessimistic Resistance**: Arthur Schopenhauer condemned Hegelian optimism, elevating the blind, irrational Will as the true thing-in-itself. [[Søren Kierkegaard]] attacked Hegel's abstract "System" for abolishing the agonizing subjective choice of the individual.
