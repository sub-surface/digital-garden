# Deontology

<div className="dropcap">

**Deontology** (from Greek *deon*, "obligation" or "duty", and *logos*, "study of") is one of the three principal traditions of contemporary normative ethics, standing alongside [[Utilitarianism]] (consequentialism) and [[Virtue Ethics]]. Deontological theories maintain that morality is fundamentally a matter of duties, obligations, and rights that constrain what agents are permitted to do, irrespective of the goodness or badness of the resulting consequences.

</div>

Where consequentialists argue that the moral rightness of an act is determined solely by the optimality of its outcomes (the ends justify the means), deontologists insist that certain acts are **intrinsically impermissible** (such as murder, torture, or bearing false witness), while others are **strictly required**, even if violating them would produce a net increase in aggregate happiness.

## 1. Kantian Deontology: Autonomy and Pure Duty

The foundational formulation of deontological ethics was established by [[Immanuel Kant]] in his 1785 *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (*Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten*)[^kant-gmm-ref].

```
                          KANT'S MORAL ARCHITECTURE
                           ┌───────────────────────┐
                           │      GOOD WILL        │
                           │(Only thing good w/out │
                           │     qualification)    │
                           └──────────┬────────────┘
                                      │
                               Acting from DUTY
                          (Not mere inclination/fear)
                                      │
                                      ▼
                        THE CATEGORICAL IMPERATIVE
                      (Universal, unconditional law)
                                      │
                ┌─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┐
                │                                           │
        FORMULATION I                               FORMULATION II
    Universal Law Formula                       Formula of Humanity
 "Act only on that maxim which             "Treat humanity, in yourself
 you can will to be a universal law"       and others, never merely as
                                           a means, but always as an end"
```

### The Good Will
Kant opens the *Groundwork* with a celebrated thesis:
> "It is impossible to think of anything at all in the world, or even beyond it, that could be considered good without limitation except a **good will**." (*GMM*, Ak. 4:393)

Talents of the mind (intelligence, wit), qualities of temperament (courage, perseverance), and gifts of fortune (wealth, power, health) are undeniably desirable, but they can become extremely evil if the will that directs them is corrupt. A good will is not good because of what it effects or accomplishes; it is good entirely in itself through its willing from **Duty** (*Pflicht*).

### The Categorical Imperative
Kant distinguishes between two types of rational imperatives:
1. **Hypothetical Imperatives**: Conditional commands based on contingent subjective desires (*"If you want to pass the examination, you ought to study"*).
2. **The Categorical Imperative (CI)**: An unconditional, categorical command binding on all rational beings simply in virtue of their rational nature (*"You ought not to lie"*).

Kant presents several interconnected formulations of the single Categorical Imperative:

#### 1. The Formula of Universal Law
> "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." (Ak. 4:421)

To test whether a personal maxim (e.g., *"Make a lying promise to secure money when in distress"*) is morally permissible, one asks: could this maxim be conceived and willed as a universal law of nature without contradiction? In the case of lying promises, universalizing the maxim destroys the very institution of promising: no one would believe any promise, yielding a **contradiction in conception**.

#### 2. The Formula of Humanity as an End in Itself
> "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end." (Ak. 4:429)

Human beings possess **dignity** (*Würde*) rather than a mere market price (*Preis*), because they are rational agents capable of moral autonomy. To enslave, deceive, or coerce an individual is to instrumentalize their agency, reducing them to a mere tool for someone else's satisfaction.

## 2. Intuitionist Deontology: W.D. Ross and *Prima Facie* Duties

Kant's absolutism—famously arguing in his 1797 essay *"On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives"* that it is impermissible to lie even to a murderer inquiring about the whereabouts of one's friend—led twentieth-century deontologists to develop more flexible frameworks.

In *The Right and the Good* (1930)[^ross-ref], Scottish philosopher **W.D. Ross** formulated a pluralist deontological intuitionism based on ***prima facie* duties** (duties that are non-absolute, binding other things being equal, but capable of being overridden by weightier conflicting duties):

1. **Duties of Fidelity**: Keeping explicit promises and telling the truth.
2. **Duties of Reparation**: Making amends for previous wrongful harms.
3. **Duties of Gratitude**: Acknowledging and reciprocating services rendered by others.
4. **Duties of Justice**: Ensuring distribution of pleasure or happiness accords with merit.
5. **Duties of Beneficence**: Improving the condition of others in terms of virtue, intelligence, or pleasure.
6. **Duties of Self-Improvement**: Improving one's own virtue and knowledge.
7. **Duties of Non-Maleficence**: Not injuring others (a duty Ross deemed more stringent than beneficence).

When duties collide (such as the duty of truth-telling versus the duty of non-maleficence when shielding a friend from a murderer), practical judgment (*phronesis*) determines one's **actual duty** (*duty proper*) in the concrete situation.

[^kant-gmm-ref]: Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), Ak. 4:421. See [[Bibliography#Kant-GMM]].

[^ross-ref]: W.D. Ross, *The Right and the Good* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1930). See [[Bibliography#Ross-Right-1930]].

## 3. Contemporary Contractualism: T.M. Scanlon

In contemporary analytic ethics, deontological constraints are often justified through **Contractualism**, pioneered by T.M. Scanlon in *What We Owe to Each Other* (1998):

$$\text{An act is wrong if its performance could only be justified by principles}$$
$$\text{which other rational persons could reasonably reject.}$$

Unlike the utilitarian who aggregates welfare across persons, Scanlonian contractualism respects the **separateness of persons**:
* Principles are evaluated from the standpoint of individual contractors.
* An individual suffering severe violation of rights can reasonably reject a principle, even if that principle generates aggregate benefits for millions of other citizens.

## 4. Deontological Dilemmas: The Trolley Problem

Deontological commitments are tested directly in contemporary normative ethics through thought experiments like [[The Trolley Problem]][^foot-trolley]:
* **The Switch Variant**: Most deontologists permit turning the runaway trolley away from five workers onto a side track killing one, invoking the **Doctrine of Double Effect** (the death of the one is a foreseen, unintended side effect of saving the five).
* **The Footbridge Variant**: Deontology strictly prohibits pushing a heavy stranger off a footbridge to physically block the trolley, because pushing the man treats him *merely as a physical means* to halt the train, violating the Formula of Humanity.

[^foot-trolley]: First formulated by Philippa Foot in "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect" (1967). See [[Bibliography#Foot-Trolley-1967]].
