# Movement: Madhyamaka

<div className="dropcap">

**Madhyamaka** (Sanskrit: "The Middle Way") is one of the foundational philosophical schools of Mahayana Buddhism, established in the second century CE by the Indian philosopher [[Nagarjuna]] in his magnum opus, the *Mulamadhyamakakarika* (Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way), and elaborated by thinkers such as Aryadeva, Buddhapalita, Candrakirti, and Shantideva. Madhyamaka charts a dialectical path between the extremes of eternalism (reification) and annihilationism (nihilism), demonstrating that all phenomena are entirely empty (*sunya*) of independent, inherent existence (*svabhava*).

</div>

---

## 1. Foundational Tenets

### 1. Dependent Origination (*Pratityasamutpada*) and Emptiness (*Sunyata*)
Nagarjuna equates dependent origination directly with emptiness:
$$\text{Dependent Origination} \equiv \text{Emptiness} \equiv \text{The Middle Way}$$
Because all entities, events, thoughts, and categories arise only in dependence upon causes, conditions, parts, and conceptual designation, they possess no intrinsic essence (*svabhava*). Emptiness is not a barren nothingness or physical void, but the absence of autonomous, self-sufficient, non-relational existence.

### 2. The Doctrine of Two Truths (*Satyadvaya*)
Madhyamaka resolves the apparent paradox between the emptiness of reality and everyday empirical function by distinguishing between two levels of truth:
* **Conventional Truth (*Samvriti-Satya*)**: The pragmatic, transactional reality of everyday life, language, moral cause-and-effect (karma), and scientific laws. Conventions work, but only relationally.
* **Ultimate Truth (*Paramartha-Satya*)**: The realization that all phenomena, including the Buddha's teachings and emptiness itself, are devoid of inherent nature. Emptiness itself is empty (*sunyata-sunyata*)—not an absolute metaphysical substrate or transcendent godhead.

### 3. The Tetralemma (*Catushkoti*)
Nagarjuna utilizes a rigorous four-cornered negation to deconstruct all metaphysical assertions. Concerning any proposition $P$, Madhyamaka denies that:
1. $P$ is the case.
2. Not-$P$ is the case.
3. Both $P$ and Not-$P$ are the case.
4. Neither $P$ nor Not-$P$ is the case.

By negating all four corners, the philosopher dismantles conceptual fixation (*prapanca*), arriving at cognitive quietude and peace (*nirvana*).

## 2. Western Comparative Parallels

Madhyamaka has attracted significant interest from modern Western philosophers due to its rigorous anti-foundationalism:
* **Wittgensteinian Anti-Metaphysics**: Nagarjuna's insistence that language functions conventionally without mirroring metaphysical substances parallels Ludwig Wittgenstein's account of language-games and meaning-as-use.
* **Derridean Deconstruction**: Jacques Derrida's *différance*—the idea that signifiers have meaning only through differential traces rather than stable presence—shares close structural affinities with Madhyamaka dependent origination.
* **Pragmatist Deflationism**: Both Dewey and Rorty share Nagarjuna's rejection of truth as correspondence to a mind-independent metaphysical baseline.

---

## 3. Major Lineages: Svatantrika vs. Prasangika

The school subsequently divided over philosophical methodology:
* **Svatantrika** (Bhaviveka): Argued that Madhyamikas can and should construct autonomous positive syllogisms (*svatantra-anumana*) to prove emptiness to opponents.
* **Prasangika** (Candrakirti): Insisted that a Madhyamika holds no positive thesis of their own, but operates purely by reductio ad absurdum (*prasanga*), showing that the opponent's own premises lead to unavoidable self-contradiction.
