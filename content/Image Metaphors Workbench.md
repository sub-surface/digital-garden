### Restating your Spinoza, so you can correct it before I build on it
1. **Expression is done by finite modes.** Substance expresses itself in attributes, and attributes in modes (Deleuze, *Expressionism*). Each particular thing expresses God's essence *in a certain and determinate way* (E1P25C [C]), so the singular thing is not a by-product of necessity but where necessity happens, singularly.
2. **Eternity describes the parallelism.** E2P7, "the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things", is not a correspondence between two series in time. It is one order expressed under two attributes, and that order *as such* is eternal. So "eternity" is not "forever in time"; it is the name for the identity of the order across attributes. A finite event is eternal as an expression in that order (*sub specie aeternitatis*, E5P29S), and durational, therefore experienced as contingent, as an encounter among modes.
3. **The harmony:** necessity and contingency aren't two verdicts on the same thing. Necessity is how the event belongs to the eternal order it expresses; contingency is how it is *encountered* in duration, from the side of its essence (E2Ax1, E4D3), which does not involve existence. People get caught out because they read the parallelism as two series running side by side in time, and then necessity looks like a clockwork that crowds out contingency.
4. **For images:** an image (Spinoza's *imaginatio*, an affection) is an expression by a finite mode (a body affected), and it is durational: the event as encountered. That is why Spinoza files contingency under the imagination (2p44s). The image is where the eternal order is met *as* encounter. Correct me where I've flattened it.

**Flusser, *Towards a Philosophy of Photography*** (Reaktion 2000, pp. 8–10, 25–8; full excerpts in the Commonplace):

**The palinode as a technique, with a sight story attached:**

## 0.1 The theses (split)

One essay can't carry them all, so they're sorted here. ★ = this essay's spine.

1. ★ **The given image.** Philosophy has used the image as its figure for knowing so fluently that it never asked what an image is. The image has been *given* (Sellars): not false, taken for granted. That is a kind of stupidity, not error (Deleuze), and philosophy's use is to harm stupidity.
2. ★ **The copy theory is a symptom.** Because philosophy *used* images as copies, it could only *theorise* them as copies. The alternatives were there all along: Spinoza's images "even though they do not reproduce the shapes of the things" (2p17s), Bergson's photograph "already developed in the very heart of things", D&G's machines "in anything more than a metaphorical sense".
3. ★ **What an image is: an event, a cut, a trace.** Images are what they are, when they are. They are machines (breaks in flows of light) that leave causal traces, which may be taken up as likeness (icon) or as relation (diagram). Likeness is something an image may *do*, not what it *is*. M87 is an image with no possible likeness and a full causal story of emergence.
4. ★ **Illusions are real occurrences.** Spinoza's sun in water (4p1s), Kant's horizon moon (A297), the mirage. Recognising an illusion is not getting behind it. Error is misattributed cause, not a gap in being. So images can mislead without being unreal, and testimony and myth stop being opposites.
5. ★ **Realism without authority, realism without finality.** To be real is not to be known (Kant Bxxvi, read in best faith). The world doesn't speak (Rorty's point, granted), but it images, and images are not assertions. That removes the "final image" charge (acheiropoieta, Peniel's "face to face", Paul's eschaton) from realism. Willowism adds that every actual event keeps the eternal truth that it might not have happened, so this realism is fallibilist all the way down.
6. ★ **The image and the possible.** Deleuze condemns the possible because it is "an image of the real… retroactively fabricated in the image of what resembles it" (D&R p. 212): the copy theory in modal form. Generative images are that possible made into a machine: Steyerl's "likenesses replaced with likelinesses". The speculative documentary, "a construction that resists probability" (Pinckers), is the counter-machine.
7. **The photograph and contingency.** *(Corrected; see 0.4.)* The photograph makes contingency permanent: it fixes *that it happened* and keeps visible *that it might not have*. Barthes ("pure contingency") and Meillassoux ("contingency is the only necessity") meet here.
8. **The image and the wound** (second refrain). The first wound in the essay is the eye's, from the sun. Sought wound (Thomas), sacrificial wound (Bataille's bull, Descartes' ox), the wound that arrives (Land). They meet in the punctum and at Peniel.
9. **Speculation is named after a mirror** that its users forgot (Paul's *speculum*; the SRs; Pinckers' watchtower without the mirror).
10. *Elsewhere:* Plato as the first theory of the acheiropoieton (**Begotten, Not Made**); Sontag's nominalism and the dispassionate image (**The Dispassionate Image**); Willowism's two tracks in full (**Willowism**); the diagram as an icon of relations (**On Diagrams** rewrite).

## 0.3 Techniques beyond the refrain

You asked for ways to elevate the essay. In rough order of payoff:

1. **Two refrains converging** (decided). The structure performs the argument: from seeing to being touched.
2. **The stations device (Benjamin's arcades).** One is already four stations; make it explicit, each with a ⟡ and a date, like the windows of an arcade. It honours the "arcades of philosophy" frame without overusing it.
3. **The composites as argument, not illustration.** Each composite placed where its diagram's thinker is discussed, captioned as a claim, never a label (avoiding anchorage). The figures carry a strand of the argument the prose can't.
4. **A lipogram paragraph.** Once, in Four: a paragraph written with no visual verbs, flagged in a sidenote ("this paragraph contains no seeing"). It shows that knowing without sight is possible and costly. Perec as patron.
5. **The translation table** (1 Cor 13:12 in sixty refractions) as a sidenote or linked note. It turns translation into an example of the thesis.
6. **Marginal verification marks.** Tufte-style margin notes saying where a quote was checked (edition, page) and where it wasn't. Gwern-style epistemic honesty, and it fits a fallibilist realism.
7. **The colophon as part of the work.** The essay about images as cuts was cut from a conversation; the colophon shows the cuts.
8. **Section epigraphs from the Bible translations.** Each section opens with a different rendering of 1 Cor 13:12 (Wycliffe, Darby's "dim window", NMB's "mysterious word"…), so the glass changes as the argument does. Elegant, but maybe one device too many. Pick this *or* the refrains-in-headings, not both.
9. **Running heads as questions** ("What is an image? (iv)"). A visual refrain in the typography. The site's article layout can carry it.

## 0.4 Corrections on record

- **Willowism (corrected after round 7).** I wrongly glossed your view as "the past becomes necessary". What you hold: an actual event *does not displace or negate the eternal truth that it might not have occurred*. Possible and actual stay on two tracks and never collapse. "Anything may do anything" names radical contingency *and*, in parallel, the radical autonomy of things to do what they do: an event obtains only when the thing actually does it. The payoff: **fallibilism without scepticism.** Knowledge always retains the possibility of error, so the pragmatist charge that realism is infallibilist (certain knowledge of actual things, clear and distinct ideas) misses this realism. "We recognise the freedom inherent to all things to do otherwise."
  - The scholastic tool for this is the distinction between *necessitas consequentiae* and *necessitas consequentis* (Boethius, *Consolation* V, prose 6; Aquinas). "If it happened, it happened" is necessary as a *consequence*; *what* happened is not thereby necessary. Boethius' example is **the sun rising** (simple vs conditional necessity) [C]. That's a sidenote with a sun in it.
  - Departures to name: Spinoza (who denies things could do otherwise; freedom as ignorance of causes, E1 Appendix) and Aristotle/Diodorus (necessity of the past). Allies: Whitehead ("decision" constitutes actuality, every occasion self-creating [C]), Bergson (freedom), Meillassoux (contingency as the only necessity, minus the mathematics).
  - So my thesis 7 is revised from "the photograph converts contingency into necessity" to "**the photograph makes contingency permanent**". *Ça-a-été* fixes that it happened, and the image, as a cut, shows the event as one that might not have been. That's truer to Barthes ("pure contingency") and to you.
- **Rhizome quote:** the D&R preface does contain the rhizome line; the essay's quote is accurate.
- **"To disappoint"** in [[On Diagrams]]: probably "to sadden" (N&P p. 106). Flagged, not edited.

## 0.10 Techniques (to use tastefully, i.e. a few, and each one earned)

1. **Short numbered sections** (*Camera Lucida*; Sontag's "Notes on 'Camp'"; Wittgenstein's remarks). Density with breathing room, and each section can end on a sentence that turns. Fits the hub.
2. **The refrain** (decided). "What is an image?" asked at section ends, answered differently each time; the wound refrain converging.
3. **One palinode.** Barthes §24 and Stesichorus: a section that recants an earlier position (the epigraph's first-year hope, or the copy theory the essay itself began in), with sight "restored". The essay's argument enacted as an event. Use once.
4. **The central excerpt, read slowly.** One long quotation per piece, unpacked phrase by phrase (Benjamin's ideal of a work built from quotations; Carson's close readings). Spinoza 4p1s in the hub, Nietzsche's madman in B.
5. **The withheld image.** Barthes never shows the Winter Garden photograph. One image the essay describes and does not show (perhaps one of your photographs). It honours the post-representational argument in form.
6. **The wordless passage.** Berger's *Ways of Seeing* has chapters made only of images. A short sequence of your composites with no captions, placed where the argument says that relations can be shown without being said.
7. **The steelman section.** Four's objection at full strength, in the opponent's best voice. Seriousness, not straw.
8. **Margin as counterpoint.** Gwern/Tufte sidenotes that sometimes *argue back* (one or two pastiche objections from the examining board, clearly marked). Ration it heavily.
9. **Ending on a figure.** Sebald, Marker's *Sans Soleil*: the last thing is an image or a scene, not a summary. (The eclipse, deferred.)
10. **Etymology as method,** rationed to three or four per piece where the word does argumentative work (*speculum*, *expressa*, *index*, *oida*).
11. **Recurring object.** One concrete thing re-examined in each section (Barthes' photographs; Ponge's objects). The puddle is the candidate: each section looks at it again and sees something new.
12. **Tone discipline:** direct address ("dear reader") at most twice; puns only where they carry argument (already your rule).

Techniques to avoid: thesis-forward scaffolding; slogans (R1); sustained pastiche; ornamental erudition (the hostile reader's "erudition or argument?").

## 0.13 Developing the seeds: the CMB, the "Novacene" problem, Virtual Worlds × Solar Inquiry (session 4c, 2026-10-15)

### The CMB belongs to the solar thread
Your 2024 note ("the first visible light in the universe") and the night reading meet here. Three facts carry it [C, verify the numbers]:
- The cosmic microwave background was released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, at recombination, when the universe cooled enough for light to travel freely. Cosmologists call that moment **the surface of last scattering**. **The oldest image is, technically, the last scattering**: the moment light stopped being bounced around by matter and began to *travel*. So the first image is the first light allowed to arrive somewhere else. That's an image as a delayed ray in its purest form.
- Penzias and Wilson found it in 1964–65 as **noise**: an unexplained hiss in a horn antenna that they first blamed on pigeon droppings. The oldest image was first received as a *poor image*, interference to be cleaned away.
- A small fraction (often quoted as about 1%) of the "snow" on an untuned analogue television is CMB. **The static of a dead channel contains the first light of the universe.** Steyerl's poor image and the oldest image in existence turn out to be literally mixed together in TV noise.

Where it fits:
- **Satellite B ("Delayed Rays") gets its last figure.** Nietzsche's starlight, Barthes' delayed ray, Land's "so much light", and finally the light from before there were any stars, received as noise. C36 becomes the satellite's ending.
- **The hub keeps Eddington** (eclipse at both ends). Don't spend the CMB in two places; that's the recontextualisation rule (§19).

### "Novacene" and hyperstition: agree it's a cliché, so the claim needs a better frame
"Hyperstition" has become a meme of itself (a self-fulfilling term for self-fulfilling ideas), and using it now signals a scene more than an argument. The *idea* in "how to bring about a future by photographing it" is good, though, and there are better-built frames for it:
- **Premediation** (Richard Grusin, *Premediation: Affect and Mediality After 9/11*, 2010 [C]): media pre-mediate possible futures so that when something happens, it has already been imaged. It is academic, precise, and about images rather than occult memetics. It pairs with Steyerl's "networked jumpiness" (affect modulated by images) already in the Commonplace.
- **Ritchin's *After Photography*** (2009): photographing possible futures to *prevent* them. That's the hopeful inverse.
- **Willowism's own terms (best):** an image can act *in virtue of possibility*. It doesn't make the future (no retrocausal magic); it changes which possibilities are attended to, rehearsed, feared, invested in. And because actuality never cancels the possibility of otherwise, an image of a future that doesn't arrive isn't false; it was a true image of a possibility. That is non-cliché, rigorous, and yours.
- **Deleuze's "people to come"** (which Steyerl's "constellation… yet to come" quotes): the image addressed to a public that doesn't exist yet.

**Recommendation:** drop "Novacene" and "hyperstition" as frames. Keep the claim as **C35 (revised): images act in virtue of possibility. They premediate; they don't predestine.** Home: the **Willowism** satellite (where the modal machinery lives), with a short appearance in B (mean images as the premediation machine of the probable vs the speculative documentary as the premediation of the improbable). That merges what would have been satellite E into D, which keeps the constellation tight.

### Virtual Worlds × Solar Inquiry → the shape of Satellite B
Your 2024 draft asks what photography is in "a world made entirely of images". The Solar Inquiry asks what light does before and after it becomes an image. Together they give B a thesis:

> **There are two kinds of light without a living source.** Light from a source that no longer exists: the photograph, the starlight, the CMB, the death of God ("the light of the stars requires time"). And light that never had a source: the generative image, a "likeliness" no photon ever struck (Steyerl). Virtual worlds used to mean the first (Plato's cave: shadows of real things). Now they increasingly mean the second.

That's a precise version of the "death of the image" that's neither lament nor slogan: the problem isn't that images lie, it's that **we're learning to live by light with no source**, where before we lived by light whose source had gone. And it lets the satellite be affirmative: the speculative documentary is a third kind, light whose source is a *possibility* (C35).

# 1. Where the essay stands

**What it is trying to do (as I read it):** show that philosophy's master metaphor for knowing is visual; that this metaphor is not innocent but carries a whole theory of knowledge with it (recognition, resemblance, inversion, the negative); that photography inherited the metaphor and turned it into an institution (testimony, documentary, evidence); and that the image, like language, finally dissolves into difference and repetition. The epigraph promises this last move and the essay hasn't reached it yet.

**What is already strong:**
- The epigraph. It is the thesis, and it is honest about its own history ("I spent my first year of uni trying to..."). The essay is the record of that failure becoming a position. Keep the confessional frame and pay it off at the end.
- The Mohist pinhole as a counter-myth to Plato's sun: a representational theory that *admits* inversion and loss, set against one that promises the unblemished original. This is the best idea in the draft and it should be made load-bearing.
- "The explanation lies in the point." A good line that is currently doing three jobs (optics, epistemology, punchline). Let it.
- The Deleuze ox-eye passage. In this essay it belongs at home: the camera obscura is the subject, so the ox-eye can carry more weight here than it does in A&D.

**What is structurally missing:**
1. **The strongest objection is never faced.** "It's just a figure of speech." Davidson, and Rorty himself, would say dead metaphors carry no commitments: saying "I see" no more commits you to ocularcentrism than saying "sunrise" commits you to geocentrism. Until the essay answers this, the Rorty jab at the end of Two reads as a gotcha rather than an argument, because Rorty has a ready reply. (See §4, section Four.)
2. **The opening list undermines itself, and nobody has noticed yet.** Half the verbs are not visual. *Grasp*, *get*, *comprehend*, *apprehend* are haptic: *prehendere*, to seize. *Intellegere* is *inter-legere*, to pick out from among, to read. *Cognoscere* is not visual at all. And the German tradition's master term for the concept, *Begriff*, comes from *greifen*: to grip. This is a gift, not a mistake: there is a haptic counter-lineage running under the visual one (Riegl's haptic, Deleuze's *Francis Bacon*, Merleau-Ponty's touching-touched). But the essay has to *notice* it, or a reader will notice it for you.
3. **The sun, the eclipse and the puddle are never tied together.** The header image is Escher's *Puddle*; the first paragraph is about not being able to look at the sun; the first quote is Plato's sun. The hidden key is *Phaedo* 99d–e (see below). Once that passage is in, the opening becomes a single figure instead of three.
4. **Spinoza is used as a way out without being stress-tested.** Spinoza has his own theory of images (E2P17S: *imagines rerum*), and it ranks them as the first, inadequate kind of knowledge. And at the climax of the *Ethics* he reaches for a visual metaphor: "the eyes of the mind, by which it sees and observes things, are the demonstrations themselves" (E5P23S). The essay's own escape route runs back through the metaphor it is escaping. That is content, not a problem: it is the essay's thesis appearing inside its counterexample.
5. **The "death of the image" is announced and never delivered.** "It has already died before…" is a promissory note. Sections Five and Six have to pay it.
6. **The essay is live.** `published: true`, `growth: becoming`, with an empty `# ⁖ Three` heading on the public site. Either accept that (it's a garden, "becoming" is honest) or put a short line under Three saying it's in progress. Your call; I haven't touched the file.

### ⁖ Three: The Image as Representation (~1,500, not started)

- Reply 2, **white mythology** (Derrida): the concept of concept is drawn from the field it would survey. The coin rubbed smooth still circulates.
- Reply 3, **inference** (Lakoff & Johnson; Sweetser): KNOWING IS SEEING licenses consequences (clarity = truth; point of view = perspective; the knower outside the scene). Dead metaphors keep doing logical work.

### ⁖ Five: The Death of the Image (~1,200, new)
Pay the promissory note from Zero ("it has already died before").

### ⁖ Six: Coda, Difference and Repetition (~600, new)

Return to the puddle: the world appears in Escher's puddle not because the puddle is a mirror but because it is a *difference in the ground*: a little water, a change of surface. The image is not a copy of the world; it is one of the world's own folds. The Mohists were right that the explanation lies in the point. What they called a point, Deleuze calls a difference. One last image the essay doesn't explain.

**⁛ Zero: overture** (~600, drafted). Light trims (see reductive-moves list in §10). End on *oida*: to know is to have seen.

**⁖ One: The History of the Image** (~1,800). Four stations: **sun** (Rep. 508b; Bataille's "Rotten Sun" for the sun you can't look at) → **puddle** (*Phaedo* 99d–e, plus Escher's woodcut as the essay's own image; see the Escher note in §11) → **point** (later Mohists; pseudo-Aristotle's eclipse crescents) → **ox** (Alhazen, Kepler, Descartes) → Deleuze's candle, aimed at Descartes *and* Hegel. Ends on "the issue": both the dualist and the dialectician read the inversion as a *loss* (copy vs original; the negative). Neither sees that the inversion is a *production*: something happened at the point.

**⁖ Two: The Image of Thought** (~1,800). Recognition as the target (D&R ch. 3). Plato's double (Rep. VI/VII vs X; *Sophist*'s eikon/phantasma) → Deleuze's overturning of Platonism: affirm the simulacrum. Spinoza: IIp7, E1A4 (knowledge = knowledge of causes), E2P17S (images contain no error), the 200-foot sun, and E5P23S's "eyes of the mind" as the figure that survives even here. Light as subject, camera as object (Bergson), said deliberately.

**⁖ Three: The Image as Representation** (~1,800). Mind as dark room (Locke; Crary). Inversion as ideology (Marx; Kofman). The photograph fixes the camera obscura and becomes an institution (evidence, *e-videre*). The tool used and not seen (Heidegger, Harman). The copy theory as symptom.

**⁖ Four: Only a Figure of Speech** (~2,000). The dead-metaphor objection at full strength (Davidson; Rorty's right of reply). Replies: Blumenberg, Derrida (heliotrope), Lakoff/Johnson. Then the linguistic turn as a change of medium, not an escape. Rorty: warrant ≠ truth, the foreclosure of cause. Derrida: deferral vs production. Then the other senses: touch (*prehendere*, *Begriff*, Whitehead's prehension), hearing (Evans & Wilkins, to be verified). Whitehead's two modes. Cause forces thought (Deleuze). Sellars' picturing as an ironic sidenote.

**⁖ Five: Speculation** (~2,000, new). The word's history: *theoria* → *speculatio* → *per speculum in aenigmate* → Kant's restriction of speculative reason, and his transcendental illusion modelled on optical illusion (the moon larger at the horizon, A297/B354) → Hegel's speculative mirror → Goldsmiths 2007. Then the four: Grant (Schelling's *Einbildungskraft*, imagination as nature's productive "forming-into-one"); Harman (allure, vicarious causation, aesthetics as first philosophy); Meillassoux and Brassier (the arche-fossil as index; solar death; thought's contingency separating thought and being). Deleuze as the most sophisticated: difference as genetic, the image of thought as what has to be overturned, not mirrored. Link to Decision Was Never Final for the genesis argument; don't redo it.

**⁖ Six: The Deaths of the Image** (~2,000). The laments: Talbot's "pencil of nature" → digitisation (Mitchell's *The Reconfigured Eye*, 1992; Willis's "living death of photography"; Dewdney's "zombie photography") → generation. The affirmations: Mitchell's pictorial turn, Levi Strauss. Operational images (Farocki), the poor image and the sea of data (Steyerl). Illusions are real (A14). ## Working thesis, v2

> The image has been philosophy's *given*: not false, but taken for granted, used as the figure of knowing so fluently that nobody asked what an image is. That is a kind of stupidity, not an error. Asked head-on, from how images are actually made (light cut by a machine, a causal trace, an individuation, a real occurrence even when it deceives), the image turns out to be neither a copy nor a final picture but one of the world's own events. That clears realism of the charge it inherited from the acheiropoietic image (that to be real is to be finally known), lets testimony and myth stop being opposites, and lets images, photographs *and* diagrams, icons of things *and* icons of relations, be taken seriously as modes of thought in their own right. In the end they dissolve, with language, into difference and repetition.

## New material

**"What is an image?" head-on.** The obvious interlocutor is W. J. T. Mitchell, *Iconology* (1986), ch. 1 is literally titled "What Is an Image?". He answers with a *family tree*: graphic, optical, perceptual, mental, verbal images, all branching from "image as likeness". **His answer is a diagram.** A gift for the diagram strand: the best-known attempt to say what an image is had to draw it. Others to place: Hans Belting, *An Anthropology of Images* (picture / medium / body); Bredekamp's image act (*Bildakt*: images act, they don't just stand for). Your answer, assembled from sessions 1–3: **an image is a cut in a flow (D&G's machine) that leaves a trace (Peirce's index) of a real occurrence (Spinoza's affection), which can be taken up as likeness (icon) or as a pattern of relations (diagram).** Likeness is one use of an image, not its essence. That is the head-on claim.

**Where it goes:** I'd make "What Is an Image?" its own short section right after the history (One). The history shows the question being avoided, then the essay finally asks it. That mirrors D&R ch. 3, where the postulates are diagnosed first and the new image of thought comes later.

**Given ≠ false (Sellars, used affirmatively).** The Myth of the Given is the claim that something non-inferential can *justify* on its own. Your image is given in that sense: it has been allowed to justify (the photo as proof, the clear idea as truth) without being interrogated. But Sellars never says the given is unreal. Light really impinges; it just doesn't adjudicate. That is exactly your mirage: real, causal, and still not a verdict. Brassier's Sellars returns here as the honest version of the point, and picturing stays the ironic sidenote.

**The final image (the realism charge).**
- The acheiropoietic lineage runs straight into photography's founding self-description: Talbot's *The Pencil of Nature* (nature draws itself, no hand involved), and Bazin, "The Ontology of the Photographic Image" (1945), which names the Shroud of Turin [C]. Photography was born as a secular acheiropoieton.
- The pragmatist charge is itself visual: Putnam's name for metaphysical realism is "the God's Eye point of view" (*Reason, Truth and History*, 1981) [C]. Rorty's anti-authoritarianism (*Solidarity or Objectivity?*) runs the same line. **The critique of realism-as-final-image is phrased as an optical claim.**
- **Irony with weight:** pragmatism's founder *had* a final image. Peirce's truth is "the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate" ("How to Make Our Ideas Clear", 1878) [C]. The end of inquiry is a convergence, and so a final picture. The realist you describe (being without being known) is *less* committed to a final image than Peirce was.
- **Your formula has a Kantian pedigree**, which is the opening for the SR section: "even if we cannot *know* these objects as things in themselves, we must yet be in a position at least to *think* them as things in themselves; otherwise we should be landed in the absurd conclusion that there can be appearance without anything that appears" (CPR Bxxvi–xxvii, Kemp Smith) [C]. To be real is not to be known: **that's Kant's own position**.

**SR's misreading of Kant: a contemporary frame.** Proposal: **deepfake panic is correlationism in public.** The current lament (no photograph can be trusted, so reality is lost) assumes that to be real is to be *finally imaged*: if the image can be faked, the real goes with it. That is the realism-as-final-image equation, held by its critics' audience. Meillassoux's charge against Kant makes a mirror-image mistake: it reads "unknowable in itself" as "relative to us", collapsing being into access. Kant had already separated them (Bxxvi). Both the panic and the misreading confuse the *limits of imaging/knowing* with the *limits of being*. Your realism (being without being known) is the cure for both, and it also lets the death-of-the-image section (Six) be affirmative rather than elegiac. It links to [[Decision Was Never Final]] for the genesis argument without repeating it.

**Land's fangs, Harman's withdrawal, immanence.** The sequence I'd suggest: Harman is right that nothing is exhausted by its relations (affirm), but withdrawal turns that into *hiding*: the real object behind every sensual one, Plato's sun in object-oriented clothes. Land's counter is that the outside isn't withdrawn, it's *coming*: the noumenon as what bites back, immanent and operative ("Machinic Desire": the outside as invasion from the future [C/?]). Deleuze's version is the calm one: the virtual is fully real, and it *differs* rather than withdraws. So the order is Harman (withdrawal) → Land (fangs: the real arrives) → Deleuze (the real produces). The reader is changed by Land and then given somewhere to stand.

## Kant: best-faith reading first (per your instruction)

Structure for the Kant passage in Five, before any SR:
1. **What Kant limits is *speculative* cognition** (Bxxvi, verified above), meaning theoretical knowledge that tries to reach beyond possible experience. The word the SRs adopt as a badge is the word for what Kant restricted.
2. **The restriction isn't a denial of the real.** "appearance without anything that appears" is "absurd". Empirical realism and transcendental idealism go together: things are thinkable in themselves, cognisable only as appearing. **To be real is not to be known** is Kant's position, not a rebuttal of it.
3. **Kant's illusions are real occurrences** (A297/B354, verified): the horizon moon persists after correction. The critique doesn't remove illusions; it stops us being *deceived* by them. That is exactly your mirage, and Spinoza's sun in water.
4. **Then the SR reading:** Meillassoux's "correlationism" (*After Finitude*, p. 5 in your copy: "the idea according to which we only ever have access to the correlation between thinking and being, and never to either term considered apart from the other") is a fair description of a *post-Kantian* tendency, but as a reading of Kant it collapses "unknowable in itself" into "relative to us". It merges the limit of *cognition* with a limit of *being*, which Bxxvi explicitly refuses. Best faith to Meillassoux: he is attacking the *strong* correlationists (Heidegger, Wittgenstein, and those who de-absolutise the in-itself), and treats Kant as the weak, honest version. If so, the SR complaint lands on Kant's heirs, not on Kant. That's the generous framing, and the accurate one.
5. **Brassier** then reads as taking Kant's *thinkable-not-knowable* to its extinction-scale limit: thought can think its own absence (solar death) because being was never thought's correlate. Contextualised this way, Brassier is more Kantian than he lets on, which links to [[Decision Was Never Final]] without repeating it.

# 16. New tensions and novel claims (session 3c)

What the extended reading surfaced, as either the essay's new contribution or a tension it has to manage.

**Novel claims (things I haven't seen put together elsewhere):**

1. **Plato's metaphysics is the first theory of the acheiropoieton.** Rep. VI: the sun is the Good's *begotten* likeness. Rep. X: a man turning a mirror can *make* the sun, and that is the painter. The line between good and bad images is begotten vs made, not image vs reality. That's the same line as the holy relic (not made by hands) vs the idol, and photography's founding self-image (nature draws itself; Bazin's Shroud) puts it on the begotten side. That's why the photograph inherits *both* religious authority (testimony) and religious fanaticism (Levi Strauss), and why the realism-as-final-image charge exists at all. **Your realism refuses the begotten/made distinction: every image is made, by light and by machines, and none the less real for it.**
2. **Every inversion in the essay is a surface effect of a difference in depth** (D&R p. 51 at full length: stereoscope, looking-glass, ox's eye). The Mohist inversion, the cave's shadows, Descartes' retina, Hegel's negative, Marx's ideology: each reads a flat opposition where there is a disparity in depth. That fills the "attentive readers" placeholder, and the stereoscope gives a photographic image of it (two flat, slightly different images; depth produced from their *disparity*, not their likeness).
3. **The causal image is not beneath thought.** Scruton and Walton, opposite analytic poles, agree that the photograph's relation to its subject is causal. Scruton concludes it isn't representation (so has no aesthetic or intellectual interest); Walton concludes it is transparent (so we literally see through it). Both assume thought lives in intention or in seeing. Your third option: the causal relation is where images *do* their thinking (Whitehead's causal efficacy, D&G's cuts, Spinoza's affections). **This places the essay precisely in the analytic literature, which the art-theory side rarely does.**
4. **The non-mimetic image has a 1677 pedigree.** Spinoza: images of things "even though they do not reproduce the shapes of the things". The copy theory was never the only theory; Spinoza had the alternative and it was ignored. That gives Three ("the copy theory is a symptom") real teeth.
5. **The glass is the essay's figure for speculation.** Paul's *speculum in aenigmate* → Jowett's Plato → Carroll's looking-glass in Deleuze → Walton's transparency → Sontag → your paper. The final image Paul promises ("face to face") is the realism the pragmatists fear; Walton's transparency is the realism they'd tolerate; your realism is a third: the glass is real, dark, and permanent, and that's not a deprivation.

**Tensions to manage:**

- **Sontag's nominalism.** Sontag (via Jacobs) uses "nominalist" as an accusation: photography fragments reality into "small units of an apparently infinite number" and undermines synthesis. But the Sellars/Brassier camp you draw on is *proudly* nominalist ("reality lacks propositional form"; only concrete particulars, Sellars' "absolute processes"). So: is photography's nominalism a fault (Sontag) or an ontological honesty (Sellars, and arguably Spinoza's singular things)? The affirmation rule suggests flipping Sontag: the photograph's fragmenting is not a failure to synthesise but a refusal of a false synthesis. **This needs your call; it's a real fork.**
- **Walton's "seeing" vs your decentring of sight.** Walton is your best analytic ally on causal realism, but he expands the empire of *seeing* (we see the past, see the dead). Use him and then turn him: transparency is the last and most sophisticated version of the visual metaphor, because it makes the image disappear *as* an image.
- **Mitchell's "leaving traces".** The trace is already part of the representational model (Locke's wax tablet). Your trace must be distinguished as *productive* (the cut makes something) rather than *receptive* (the surface takes a print). Otherwise a critic will say the trace is just representation with a smudge.
- **Paul's reciprocity.** "Then shall I know even as also I am known": the final image is mutual. Sontag's predation is one-way knowledge (knowing them as they never know themselves). Your machine/actualist view (Mullarkey: "different forms of public, not all of them human") makes looking *always* reciprocal in some register: the photographed affects the camera as much as the reverse. That has an ethical payoff for the documentary questions in your paper, and it may be what the essay offers documentary practice.
- **Perec's lipogram.** If "avoiding visual metaphors is like writing without the letter e", the essay should probably *try it once*: a single paragraph written with no visual verbs, flagged in a sidenote. A demonstration rather than an argument, and in the spirit of the speculative documentarians.

## Straight answers

**1. Refraction: you're right about the physics, and your version is better than Mullarkey's.**
- Refraction happens at an *interface*, where light crosses between media with different refractive indices. Snell's law, *n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂*, is a relation between two indices. **Refraction is a difference that makes a difference** (Bateson, literally): no difference in index, no bending. That is Deleuzian, not Mullarkey's monism of refraction.
- **The refractive index is the best model of the virtual I know.** Water's index (≈1.33) is fully real and fully determinate, and it determines how any ray *would* bend, but it is actualised only in the event of a crossing. And the bent ray doesn't *resemble* the index: "Actual terms never resemble the singularities they incarnate" (D&R p. 212, verified). So the index is real and not actual; the refraction is actual and contingent (it needs light to arrive); neither copies the other. **This is your intuition made precise, and it answers Mullarkey on his own optical ground:** refraction isn't "all there is to light", it's what happens when light meets a difference.
- **Spinoza fits exactly:** a body's capacity to affect and be affected (*potentia*) is its "index". The encounter is the crossing. The medium isn't passive: the light changes the medium too (heating, absorption, fluorescence). Reciprocity, which is your ethics answer, built into the optics.
- **Pun with weight:** *refractive index* and Peirce's *index* share the Latin *index*, the pointer, the forefinger. See the wound, below.
- **Vacuum:** you're right that calling non-refraction "refraction" is a negative move. Better: the vacuum has an index too (n = 1). That's not a lack, it's a value, the reference everything else differs from. **And even the vacuum bends light: gravitational lensing.** Mass curves spacetime and starlight bends around the sun. **Eddington's eclipse expedition of 1919** confirmed general relativity by *photographing* stars near the eclipsed sun and measuring their displacement. The sun, the eclipse, the photograph and the bending of light: it's the Phaedo's eclipse, two millennia later, where the image of the stars beside the darkened sun proves that light itself is bent by what it passes. No quantum anything; it's relativity. **And M87's ring is lensing too:** the "image of a black hole" is light bent around a mass. One physical story (light bent by difference) runs from Snell to Eddington to M87. That may be the essay's final figure: neither reflection nor inversion nor refraction-as-substance, but **light bent by what it passes, recording the difference as an image.** [Eddington/Dyson 1919: C; verify the plate details.]
- **Barad vs Mullarkey:** diffraction doesn't need quantum mechanics. Young's two-slit experiment (1801) is classical wave optics, and Haraway's original use of diffraction as a figure (*Modest_Witness*, 1997; "The Promises of Monsters", 1992) is optical, not quantum: "diffraction patterns record the history of interaction, interference, reinforcement, difference" [C, verify]. Use Haraway's diffraction, and footnote Barad as the quantum elaboration you're keeping at arm's length. That gives the coda a triad: **reflection** (mirror, copy: Plato, Rorty's target), **refraction** (medium as event: your version, against Mullarkey's), **diffraction** (interference, history of difference: Haraway). Since you're wary, the essay can say *why* it stops at classical optics: the image doesn't need quantum mystery to escape the mirror.

**2. Virtual/possible: the formulation needs fixing, and the fix gives you an argument.**
Your sentence: "the possible is necessarily true virtual relation to all things, the real is a necessarily actualised form of a virtuality — contingently anastrophised from molecular assembly". Three problems, straight:
- **Deleuze forbids putting the possible inside the virtual.** "The only danger in all this is that the virtual could be confused with the possible. The possible is opposed to the real… By contrast, the virtual is not opposed to the real; it possesses a full reality by itself" (D&R p. 211, verified). Pairs: *possible / real* (realisation), *virtual / actual* (actualisation). The virtual is already real. "The real is the actualised form of a virtuality" collapses real into actual; for Deleuze the real is *both* virtual and actual.
- **The gift: Deleuze's objection to the possible is that it's an image.** "to the extent that the possible is open to 'realisation', it is understood as an image of the real, while the real is supposed to resemble the possible… Such is the defect of the possible: a defect which serves to condemn it as produced after the fact, as retroactively fabricated in the image of what resembles it" (p. 212, verified; Bergson makes the same argument in "The Possible and the Real"). **The possible/real pair is the copy theory in modal form:** the possible is a picture of the real projected backwards. So "the better formulation" you're reaching for isn't just better, it is *your essay's critique applied to modality*. Possible-as-image is the dogmatic image of thought at the level of what can exist. That's a strong paragraph for Five or Seven, and I don't think anyone in the image-theory literature has made it explicitly.
- **"Necessarily" and "contingently" in one sentence is the E1P33 landmine** from A&D and Decision. Spinoza: nothing is contingent (E1P33). Deleuze's actualisation is "genuine creation", not necessitated. Your radical-contingency claim pulls toward Deleuze/Meillassoux and away from Spinoza. You've held that tension before; this essay needs to say which way it leans *for images*. My suggestion: images are where contingency shows. An image is an actualisation that need not have happened (the light had to arrive, the shutter had to open), and that is exactly why Spinoza files imagination under the "contingent" view of things (E2P44C1: it is from the imagination alone that we regard things as contingent [C, verify]). Spinoza himself makes the image the site of contingency. You can keep both: necessity sub specie aeternitatis, contingency as the image's mode. That's an honest settlement, not a fudge.
- **"Anastrophised"**: lovely (and an A&D callback: AIAYN as anastrophe). Keep it, but say what turns over. An anastrophe is an inversion of order, so "molecular assembly anastrophised into the actual" is the Mohist inversion again, at the scale of matter. Use it on purpose.

**On my pragmatist lean:** fair hit. I've reached for deflationary framings (Rorty's reply, "practice") because they're the strongest objections. But the reading has moved me toward your side on the main point. Brandom's own account shows Rorty's view depends on the reason/cause split being *absolute*, and the refraction/index model above is a case where the "cause" side has real structure (determinate, differential, non-resembling) that the space of reasons doesn't exhaust and can't legislate. The Sellarsian can still say that structure isn't *knowledge* until it's taken up in reasons. Fine: but then the image is where cause is taken up into reasons, which is Whitehead's symbolic reference. **The image is the interface between the space of causes and the space of reasons.** That's a refraction too.

## Straight responses

**1. Your two tracks: now I see it, and it's stronger than I gave it credit for. Two challenges, then a gift.**

Restated so you can check it:
- **Track P (possibility):** necessary, eternal, unexhausted. Nothing that happens uses it up. ("When events occur, it doesn't retroactively negate the infinite possibility latent before the occurrence.")
- **Track A (actuality):** contingent events, which happen *partly in virtue of* P but are not entailed by it.
- **The past:** once an event has occurred, its having-occurred becomes necessary, a standing fact about every later present.

Deleuze's objection to "the possible" (D&R p. 212) targets a possible that is a *pre-formed image* of the real, which the real then copies. **Yours isn't that:** it doesn't pre-contain the shape of what happens, so it escapes the critique. Terminologically, though, readers who know D&R will hear "possible" as the thing Deleuze condemns. Two options: (a) call track P "the virtual" and add a sidenote saying your virtual is unexhausted and modally necessary; or (b) keep "possible" and add a sidenote: *not the possible Deleuze condemns, which is an image of the real; this possible is never an image of anything.* (b) is more interesting, because it lets the essay say: **the possible becomes a dogmatic image only when it is pictured.** That fits the essay's thesis exactly.

**Challenge 1: "anything may do anything" vs Spinoza's determinate capacities.** For Spinoza a thing can do only what follows from its essence and its encounters. Bodies have determinate capacities to affect and be affected, even if "nobody has yet determined what a body can do" (E3P2S [C]). That's openness, not lawlessness. And the essay's own refraction model points the same way: water bends light by exactly its index, not by anything. So "anything may do anything" is Meillassoux's hyper-chaos (laws themselves contingent), and it sits uneasily with Spinoza's *potentia* and with your index. A possible resolution: **"anything may do anything" holds at the level of P across all time (no law is eternal), while within an actualised order bodies have determinate capacities.** That is roughly Meillassoux's own split: laws are stable *de facto* and contingent *de jure*. If that's your view, say so in a sidenote. If not, Willowism needs to choose between Spinoza's determinate potentia and Meillassoux's lawless possibility. This is the real fork in your modal metaphysics, and the Willowism essay is where to fight it.

**Challenge 2: "necessary things play no part in contingency".** If P is necessary and contingent events occur partly *in virtue of* P, then necessity does play a part: it's a condition. I think you mean necessity doesn't *select*: it enables, but doesn't decide which event happens. That's a good distinction (enabling vs selecting) and worth stating that way. Spinoza wouldn't accept it (for him, God's nature selects everything), so this is the point where Willowism departs from Spinoza, toward Deleuze and Meillassoux. Name the departure; it's honest and it's interesting.

**The gift: the photograph is where contingency becomes necessary.** Your third point (the past acquires necessity: "that which was" is a fact about the present) is old and distinguished. Aristotle, *De Interpretatione* 9: "what is, necessarily is, when it is" [C]. Diodorus' Master Argument: "every past truth is necessary" [C]. Bergson's and Deleuze's pure past (D&R ch. 2): the past coexists with the present it was. And it's the exact structure of Barthes' *ça-a-été*: the event photographed was contingent ("the Photograph is pure contingency and can be nothing else", *Camera Lucida* §13 [C]), but *that it has been* can never not have been. **The photograph is a machine for converting contingency into necessity.** It cuts a contingent event out of the flow, and the cut makes its having-happened permanent and present. Barthes (pure contingency) and Meillassoux (contingency is the only necessity) aren't in conflict, and the photograph is the object that shows why. **I think this is the deepest thing the essay can say about images, and it belongs in "What Is an Image?" or Seven.** It also connects to your Spinoza clause: the contingency of events doesn't discount the necessity of their becoming *sub specie aeternitatis*. The photograph is a small, material *sub specie aeternitatis*: an event seen as having-been, forever.
