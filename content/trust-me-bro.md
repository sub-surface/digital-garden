Almost everything you know, you were told.

This is not a failure of rigour. It is the only arrangement under which knowledge can scale beyond one skull. No individual has verified the boiling point of water, the existence of Uruguay, and the mechanism of their own liver. We operate on a vast inherited ledger of claims we have accepted on the word of others, and the ledger works — the planes land, the bridges hold.[^ledger]

The interesting question, then, was never *whether* to accept testimony. It is: **what makes a source good?**

The classical account demands three things (Bro 2019, 4–7): that the speaker be in a position to know; that the speaker be sincere; and that the hearer possess some independent means of calibration. Where all three obtain, testimony transmits knowledge as cleanly as perception does. Where one fails, the chain is broken and the claim inherits no warrant at all.

## The problem of the unverifiable source

Difficulty arises in the limiting case: a source who **cannot be checked**, but whose track record is asserted — by the source — to be excellent.

This is not a marginal case. It is most of the internet, and, increasingly, most of everything.[^most]

Bro's own treatment is characteristically brisk. He argues that the demand for independent verification is itself a species of infinite regress: any verifying source must in turn be verified, and so on, until one either accepts a foundational testimony or accepts nothing at all (Bro 2021a, *passim*). Since accepting nothing at all is not a life anyone actually lives, some terminal act of trust is structurally unavoidable. The only honest question is *where* you put it.

He puts it in himself. There is a certain rigour to this.

## Against the regress

The counter-position — that trust should terminate only in institutions, credentials, or the peer-reviewed record — has been forcefully stated (Bro and Bro 2022) and just as forcefully answered (Bro 2023, ch. 4), and I will not rehearse the exchange here beyond noting that both parties conceded the central point and neither conceded it to the other.

What survives the dispute is modest but real: **the demand "source?" is a demand for a chain, not a foundation.** It asks where a claim came from, not where the universe bottoms out. A source that answers the question at all — even badly, even circularly — has done more epistemic work than one that declines.

Which is, I think, the strongest available defence of the position, and I want to be clear that it is not mine. I got it from a guy.
