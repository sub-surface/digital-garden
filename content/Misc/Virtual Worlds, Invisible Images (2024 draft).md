# The future-past of photography

By Leon Morgan, July 2024.

> We look at the present through a rear-view mirror. We march backwards into the future. (McLuhan & Fiore, The Medium is the Message, 1967, p.75)

The modernist belief that photography faithfully depicts a "trace" of the real external world is a long-fought battlefield on which many philosophical titans have run the gauntlet.

So, in light of 198 years of history, what is the state of Photographic Philosophy? Starting with the question:

## What is an image?

Answers here imply competing theses about the image's privation over sensation, the responsibility artists hold to their subjects, the status of the depicted and depictor, and we hold such theses close to our own self-image. With this, the stakes are indeed high. So, where do we begin?

> Illustration of Camera Obscura by the Optician James Ayscough, 1762 (digitised 2018)

A Virtual World is a world made entirely of images. Plato's allegory of the Cave was arguably the first virtual world in philosophy, but the idea of a projection of light through a camera obscura is observed as far back as the Chinese philosopher Mozi around 400 BCE (later described by the Mohists around the time of Plato). These ancient ideas coalesced with the invention of photography in 1826 (or earlier with the Turin Shroud), and evolved rapidly through the 20th Century.

Today, philosophy is a diverse and expansive field of thought. Plato's cave, while antiquated, remains a prescient example of the fallibility of using images to understand the world. The Matrix (1999) would be the ultimate cultural example of this, influenced directly (According to the Wachowskis) by Jean Baudrillard's Simulacra and Simulation (1991).

In 1985, Vilém Flusser eerily captured this world of images that we live in today. Flusser wrote extensively on history's race towards the image, and in his book, Into the Universe of Technical Images, he paints what might seem a bleak picture of a "telematic society" - where civilization is governed by "automatic guidance and control of complex systems to take advantage of improbable accidents and to generate information" or, Cybernetics. Flusser writes,

> There can be no doubt that the structure of the emerging society increasingly resembles that of a brain.
>
> The notion of technical images as a kind of secretion of a global nervous system, the dreaming of a superbrain, comes to mind [...] a dreaming global brain controlled cybernetically through technical images. That would be a metaphor for the telematic society (Flusser, 1985 p.125)

When Baudrillard was commissioned to cover the Gulf War, he famously chose to cover the conflict through television reports such as CNN, leading to the declaration that "The Gulf War Did Not Take Place." These thinkers captured an image of our increasingly virtual world, which suggests why Baudrillard's ideas remain relevant. Today's photographers do grapple not only with capturing reality, but with the ways in which images determine reality.

William Eggleston's "War with the obvious", Hito Steyerl's "defence of the poor image", Jonas Bendiksen's "The Book of Veles", these projects offer a different way of looking at the world. Often we "Look through" images, discarding the paper and ink, or indeed the pixels that constitute them. Ultimately, however, it remains to be seen if this virtual world will prove fatal. Trevor Paglen's Invisible Images for The New Inquiry, highlights how most images are not for human eyes:

> Visual culture has changed form. It has become detached from human eyes and has largely become invisible. Human visual culture has become a special case of vision, an exception to the rule. (Paglen, 2016)

Just like Flusser's cybernetic future, images are used by and for digital systems, communicating at light-speed across great under-sea cables encoded in giant inscrutable matrices of floating-point numbers inside the transformers that undergird the most cutting edge LLMs and GenAI models like ChatGPT and Midjourney, or the vast computational algorithms behind YouTube and Facebook that curate the images we get to see on a daily basis and of course, the elephant in the room, surveillance. A gigantic global cybernetic apparatus of self-imaging, that is the legacy of our virtual world.
