Harvard photographed the whole sky, over and over, from 1885 to 1990: about half a million glass plates, now digitised as [DASCH](https://dasch.cfa.harvard.edu/). It is the longest film of the night there is, and most of it has never been watched closely.

Most recurrent-nova eruptions go unobserved ([Pagnotta & Schaefer 2014](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014ApJ...788..164P)), so with Claude I pre-registered a search: the light curves of 1,211 known symbiotic and cataclysmic binaries, each compared with 2,101 matched field stars, a brightening that had to persist for two nights, and a threshold set by the highest score among the controls - all fixed before looking.

Ten stars cleared the bar. Nine were known outbursts, slow activity or a plate flaw. The tenth was **Hen 2-147 (V347 Nor)**, a symbiotic binary with a 373-day Mira inside a ring nebula: long called a "symbiotic nova", with no outburst ever reported.

![[daily-v347nor-1943.png]]

It erupted in February 1943. Absent on the 9th and 10th, a strong image on two plate series on the 11th, peaking at B = 8.0 on the 26th, then fading over months. More than a thousand plates before and after show nothing like it. The red ticks are the best part: plates where the *image cutouts* clearly show the star but the catalogue logged a non-detection. The machine reading the plates looked straight at it and missed it, and you only find it by looking at the pictures again.

Which is, I suppose, what this whole site is about - [[the history of images]], and what's still in them that nobody saw at the time. The plates were exposed in the middle of a war, by people who had other things to look at.

There is a short paper drafted for the [Research Notes of the AAS](https://iopscience.iop.org/journal/2515-5172). Before it goes anywhere the original glass needs checking. The research instrument it came from, Celestrium, has its public face at [astro.subsurfaces.net](https://astro.subsurfaces.net).
