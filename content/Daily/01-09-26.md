What if harmony were somewhere you could walk about in, instead of a row of keys? [Lissajous](https://lissajous.subsurfaces.net) is my attempt: a music-theory instrument for people who like their chords with a diagram.

The middle of it is a **wheel of twelve notes**. Chords are shapes on the wheel, transposing is turning it, and a draggable mirror gives you *negative harmony*: every note reflected across an axis, so a bright major cadence comes back as its shadow. Around the wheel, as of today:

- **The Tonnetz** - Euler's 1739 lattice of triangles where each triangle is a triad and neighbours share two notes. Wrapped round, it's a torus. Click two chords and it finds the shortest walk between them.
- **Dissonance curves** - the Plomp-Levelt and [Sethares](https://sethares.engr.wisc.edu/consemi.html) model of *roughness*: why an octave or a fifth sits still and a semitone grinds. The valleys in the curve are the consonant intervals, and they move if you change the timbre, which is the whole surprising point. You can sculpt a timbre and watch your scale stop working.
- **A landscape for three notes** - the same idea in 3D, where triads sit in basins.
- **Equal temperament against just intonation**, A/B, so you can hear the beating a piano has taught you to ignore.

There's also a generator for cycles and progressions (Coltrane changes, hexatonic loops, Messiaen's modes), a corner for the odd chords called Z-related twins that share every interval yet aren't the same shape, and export to MIDI and notation so anything you find can go into a track.

It is, I admit, a lot of buttons. A second pass later in the month reworked the whole thing, and it now has two modes: one for writing songs and one for the full algebra.
