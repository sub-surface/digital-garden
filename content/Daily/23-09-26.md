In 2023 the [hat](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10798) settled the *einstein* problem in the plane: one shape (*ein Stein*, one stone) that tiles forever and never repeats. This month Ioannis Tsiokos posted [*A strongly aperiodic monotile in three dimensions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19214), with a machine-checked Lean 4 proof, claiming the same for space. The tile is called **Chair44**, and I spent the day building a little lab to look at it.

![[daily-chair44-supertile.png]]
*Eight chairs make a chair twice the size. Bumps and dents drawn 80 times too tall.*

The shape is almost insultingly simple: seven unit cubes, a 2×2×2 block with one corner bitten out. That "chair" on its own tiles space in boring periodic ways. The trick is on the surface. Each of its 24 exposed square panels carries eight tiny square pyramids, 192 in all, some pointing out and some in, with twelve different heights. A bump only fits a dent of exactly its own height, so the panels act like a twelve-channel handshake. Of 2,388 ways two chairs could touch, 44 survive. Hence the name.

![[daily-chair44-bumps.png]]
*The handshake, close up. At true scale these are a ten-thousandth of a cube tall and you'd never see them.*

Why it can't repeat is the lovely part. The 44 contacts force eight chairs to gather into a chair of twice the size, eight of those into one four times the size, and so on up, forever. Any sliding symmetry of the whole tiling would have to respect every level of that hierarchy, so it would have to be divisible by two again and again without ever leaving the grid. Only zero manages that. (It's the 2-adic integers doing the work - a different flavour from Penrose and the hat, where the golden ratio is in charge.) The tile has no symmetries of its own, and although mirror images are allowed, every tiling ends up entirely one-handed.

![[daily-chair44-inside.png]]
*Inside a patch of 4,096.*

The lab has a 3D viewer (one tile, then 8, 64, 512 and 4,096, with an exploded view and a slider between true scale and "schematic"), a period tester that shows where any proposed translation breaks, a terminal playground for the substitution and the halving tower, and scripts that re-check the paper's arithmetic exactly. It lives in my maths repo for now; the viewer wants a home on this site.

A caution I'd apply to myself: the paper is new, and "machine-checked" covers what was formalised, not what one hopes was. The lab re-runs the arithmetic; it does not re-read the Lean.
