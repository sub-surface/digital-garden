On 2 November a group at MIT CSAIL (Takehi, Teoh, Zhang and Khattab) published *Sparks of Weight-Space Programming*: a frontier model, GPT-6 Astra, **read a small model's weights and edited them directly**, no training, and Qwen3.5-0.8B went from 7.9% to 48.1% on the MATH-500 benchmark. Write-ups: [DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/weight-space-programming), [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/a/5307105). They released the logs, so we could take the edit apart.

What it actually was:

- The edit is exactly rank one: $W - 6\,u(u^\top W_S)$ on 64 columns in two layers, about 0.016% of the weights. Those two layers are precisely the model's full-attention layers.
- The little model's bug was a re-checking habit, a loop: it kept second-guessing itself and ran out of room before finishing answers it already knew. Through a Jacobian lens the direction Astra removed reads as a **doubt ↔ conclude** axis. One end is `?`, `??`, ` Actually`, ` Wait`, `不是`; the other end is "results", "outcomes", `得出结论` ("reach a conclusion").

Then we grew a tiny "organism", a one-million-parameter model trained to have the same re-checking habit, to test methods quickly. The obvious fix (subtract the average "loop" direction) made it *worse*. One honest gradient step, compiled into an edit of the same size, fixed it completely (0.23 → 1.00) and held when replicated. The bitter lesson, again.

The side quest was prettier. **Do weights remember where the data went?** For plain gradient descent there's an exact theorem: every weight change lies in the span of the inputs that layer saw (checked to $10^{-9}$). And in a small trained transformer you can read its attention off the weights *without running it*:

The same weight-reading identified four game-playing agents, four for four, from their first-layer weights: gradients only move weights whose inputs were active, so a policy's history is written in its body.

All of it sits under a wider question I care about, which is my jev research thread: when is one calibrated, fast judgement good enough, and when is the slow, expensive deliberation worth running? Applied here: which of a thousand possible weight edits deserve the expensive test.
