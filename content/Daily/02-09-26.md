[JANKEN](https://rps.subsurfaces.net) is my rock-paper-scissors chess: the pieces move like kings and may only take what they beat, so a piece you can't take is a wall. Rock stops scissors dead; paper walks straight through rock. It has online play, ratings, an analysis board and a pile of variants.

Today the smallest interesting version got **solved**. On a seven-cell hexagonal board - one centre, six around it - with one rock, one paper and one scissors each, there are 37,633 ways to place the pieces and 75,266 positions once you say whose turn it is. A retrograde solver works backwards from every finished game and labels each position won, lost or drawn with perfect play. The table is small enough to ship, so the site's "Hex Pocket" preset now has a bot that never makes a mistake, and tells you the verdict as you play. It is a humbling opponent for a game with six pieces.

The same day the theory pages grew up a bit:

- **How big is this game?** A slider in the manner of *Powers of Ten*: noughts and crosses at about 10^1 positions, 3×3 JANKEN at 10^5, 5×5 at 10^14, the atoms in the universe at 10^80, and the 13×13 board sailing past at 10^170. Counting them used to take ten seconds; a generating-function trick got it to 2.5 milliseconds.
- **What if the cycle were different?** A workbench for other "who beats whom" diagrams - five-way pentagrams, lopsided tournaments - with the deadlocks each one produces.
- **Hex boards** in general, with twelve-neighbour kings.

The reason to bother: rock-paper-scissors is the simplest game with no best move, and chess the classic game with one. Gluing them together gives positions where identity matters more than material, and solving the tiny cases is how you find out what the big ones are about.
