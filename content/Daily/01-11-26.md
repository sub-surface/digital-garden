[FRACTRAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FRACTRAN) is [John Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Horton_Conway)'s joke programming language. A program is just a list of fractions. Start with a number; multiply it by the first fraction that gives you a whole number; repeat. That's all, and it's Turing-complete. Conway's PRIMEGAME is fourteen fractions that, started at 2, spit out every prime as a power of 2. (Our interpreter checks it: the first prime power, $2^2$, appears at step 19.)

The [Busy Beaver](https://bbchallenge.org/) people hunt for the smallest programs whose behaviour nobody can settle, and they call the unsettled ones **cryptids**. Many of the FRACTRAN cryptids turn out to be the same creature underneath, which we've been calling a *Hydra*: a walker with a position $x$ and an altitude $y$. Each step multiplies $x$ by about $3/2$ and moves $y$ up or down according to $x$'s remainder, and the program halts if the walker ever reaches the valley ($y < 0$). The digits of the starting position, in base 2, secretly *are* the list of directions it will take.

![[daily-fractran-mountain.png]]
*"The Mountain": each column is a starting position, coloured if it ever reaches the valley; dark means it climbs away forever. The star is the cryptid.*

What came out of the first session (me bringing intuition for games, Claude the literature and computation):

- **The fog is exact.** For the whole zoo, the first $K$ directions are *exactly* a fair coin-flip sequence for a uniform start. So the share of starts that get down matches a random walk to five decimals.
- **The odds are Pisot numbers.** The chance of ever dropping a level is a root of a little polynomial. For the commonest step pattern it's $1/\varphi$, one over the golden ratio. The plot on the right is the population check: 0.619, 0.383, 0.237, 0.146... against $(1/\varphi)^{y_0+1}$ = 0.618, 0.382, 0.236, 0.146. Other cryptids get the silver ratio and the tribonacci constant.
- **Some cryptids are Mahler's problem in disguise.** For six of them the whole orbit is one real number, $\xi(3/2)^k$, rounded at each step, which makes "does it halt?" a question about the fractional parts of powers of 3/2 - [an open problem of Mahler's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahler%27s_3/2_problem) since 1968. Nobody knows how to do those, which is why they're still cryptids.

So the cryptids aren't really hard because they're clever. They're hard because they're *fair*: they look exactly like coin flips, and the coins are the digits of a number nobody understands.
