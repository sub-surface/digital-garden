Philchat spent most of today on Newcomb's problem, again. One regular pointed out that these arguments only ever have four states: two-boxers saying the money is already in the box, two-boxers being accused of thinking they can outsmart the predictor, one-boxers saying the expected value is far higher, and one-boxers being accused of believing in retrocausality. That's about right, and it's why the argument never ends - every side's best move is an accusation the other side has already priced in.

So I built something to argue with instead. [Two Boxes](/two-boxes) is the problem as an arcade bench. Box A holds $1,000; box B holds $1,000,000 if an oracle predicted you'd take B alone, and nothing otherwise. You can sit down against an oracle that learns you, or put causal, evidential and functional decision theory on the bench against seven oracles and watch who walks out rich.

![[daily-two-boxes.png]]
*Every number here comes from the game's own engine. The matrix is the point: the three theories agree about every payoff and disagree about one arrow.*

What the bench makes obvious is that "is one-boxing rational?" is the wrong grain. The question that decides it is *how the prediction connects to you*:

- **If it runs through your reasoning** - a demon, a psychologist, a simulation of you - the theories that respect that link take B and leave with about a million. CDT takes both and leaves with $1,000, every time, on principle. Lewis's reply, in a paper actually called "Why Ain'cha Rich?", is that the game was built to reward irrationality. That's a bullet you can bite, but it is a bullet.
- **If it's a common cause** - a gene, a temperament; someone asked whether one-boxers just earn less over a lifetime anyway - then the correlation is real and your choice still doesn't touch the box. Naive evidential reasoning leaves $1,000 on the table for nothing there. That's the honest version of the two-boxer's case, and it's a good one.
- **If the oracle fills the box after you choose,** everyone one-boxes, CDT included. "It was decided a million years ago" is doing all its work on the arrow, not on the deciding.

The transparent version got called the killer: if both boxes are glass and you can see the million, you'd be mad not to take both. Even the one-boxers in the room conceded it, and I think that's a mistake. A predictor facing glass boxes fills B for the people who'd take B alone *on seeing it full* - grab both when it's full and you are exactly the person it shows an empty box. The cleanest statement of this all day came, nicely, from a two-boxer: if anyone would one-box in front of two glass boxes of money, the predictor just gives them the million and the premise holds perfectly. That's the updateless move, and it's the one row of the matrix where functional decision theory parts ways with evidential.

The other line of attack was methodological, and the most interesting: accuracy isn't a property of a machine but of a machine *on some data*, so probe it - flip coins, coordinate, run trials, see if you can drive its accuracy to zero. Fair, and the Reader in the arcade is a place to try. Making it wrong turns out to be easy: run its code, do the opposite, and it is wrong every single round. You'll also average $50,050 a round where taking B alone averages $990,000. Wrong in the direction that pays is the hard part.

Trying that caught a real flaw. The first Reader predicted from base rates, and taking both one round in five beat honest one-boxing by $200 a round - a mixed strategy farms any predictor of tendencies. The fix is Nozick's, who stipulated that his predictor leaves B empty for anyone it expects to randomise: the Reader won't fill B for a stranger, and after you take both from a box it filled it won't fill another for twenty rounds. With that rule I could check every possible game up to sixteen rounds, all 262,140 of them. Nothing beats taking B alone, even for a player who can compute the Reader exactly - except grabbing both on the very last round, which is its own small lesson about one-shot games.

Someone also pasted a chatbot's ruling, which went to the one-boxers on "argument quality". The two-boxers will rightly say that settles nothing.
