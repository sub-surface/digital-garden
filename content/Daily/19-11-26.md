Philchat spent most of today on Newcomb's problem, again. One regular pointed out that these arguments only ever have four states: two-boxers saying the money is already in the box, two-boxers being accused of thinking they can outsmart the predictor, one-boxers saying the expected value is far higher, and one-boxers being accused of believing in retrocausality. That's about right, and it's why the argument never ends - every side's best move is an accusation the other side has already priced in.

So I built something to argue with instead. [Two Boxes](/two-boxes) is the problem as an arcade bench. Box A holds $1,000; box B holds $1,000,000 if an oracle predicted you'd take B alone, and nothing otherwise. You can sit down against an oracle that learns you, or put causal, evidential and functional decision theory on the bench against seven oracles and watch who walks out rich.

![[daily-two-boxes.png]]
*Every number here comes from the game's own engine. The three theories agree about every payoff and disagree about which node a choice gets to move - the rest is consequences.*

What the bench makes obvious is that "is one-boxing rational?" is the wrong grain. The question that decides it is *how the prediction connects to you*:

- **If it runs through your reasoning** - a demon, a psychologist, a simulation of you - the theories that respect that link take B and leave with about a million. CDT takes both and leaves with $1,000, every time, on principle. Lewis's reply, in a paper actually called "Why Ain'cha Rich?", is that the game was built to reward irrationality. That's a bullet you can bite, but it is a bullet.
- **If it's a common cause** - a gene, a temperament; someone asked whether one-boxers just earn less over a lifetime anyway - then the correlation is real and your choice still doesn't touch the box. Naive evidential reasoning leaves $1,000 on the table for nothing there. That's the honest version of the two-boxer's case, and it's a good one.
- **If the oracle fills the box after you choose,** everyone one-boxes, CDT included. "It was decided a million years ago" is doing all its work on the arrow, not on the deciding.

The transparent version got called the killer: if both boxes are glass and you can see the million, you'd be mad not to take both. Even the one-boxers in the room conceded it, and I think that's a mistake. A predictor facing glass boxes fills B for the people who'd take B alone *on seeing it full* - grab both when it's full and you are exactly the person it shows an empty box. The cleanest statement of this all day came, nicely, from a two-boxer: if anyone would one-box in front of two glass boxes of money, the predictor just gives them the million and the premise holds perfectly. That's the updateless move, and it's the one row of the matrix where functional decision theory parts ways with evidential.

The other line of attack was methodological, and the most interesting: accuracy isn't a property of a machine but of a machine *on some data*, so probe it - flip coins, coordinate, run trials, see if you can drive its accuracy to zero. Fair, and the Reader in the arcade is a place to try. Making it wrong turns out to be easy: run its code, do the opposite, and it is wrong every single round. You'll also average $50,050 a round where taking B alone averages $990,000. Wrong in the direction that pays is the hard part.

Trying that caught a real flaw. The first Reader predicted from base rates, and taking both one round in five beat honest one-boxing by $200 a round - a mixed strategy farms any predictor of tendencies. The fix is Nozick's, who stipulated that his predictor leaves B empty for anyone it expects to randomise: the Reader won't fill B for a stranger, and after you take both from a box it filled it won't fill another for twenty rounds. With that rule I could check every possible game up to sixteen rounds, all 262,140 of them. Nothing beats taking B alone, even for a player who can compute the Reader exactly - except grabbing both on the very last round, which is its own small lesson about one-shot games.

Someone also pasted a chatbot's ruling, which went to the one-boxers on "argument quality". The two-boxers will rightly say that settles nothing.

For tomorrow, when this is still going: Oesterheld and Conitzer, ["Extracting Money from Causal Decision Theorists"](https://academic.oup.com/pq/article/71/4/pqaa086/6118001); Levinstein and Soares, ["Cheating Death in Damascus"](https://intelligence.org/2017/03/18/new-paper-cheating-death-in-damascus/); and the [SEP on causal decision theory](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/decision-causal/). ## Later: cheating Death

In the evening I read Levinstein and Soares properly, and it changed what I think the interesting question is. Their move is to stop arguing about Newcomb at all - intuitions there are split down the middle and will stay split - and go after causal decision theory with *unstable* problems, where the right answer is much less contested.

Death in Damascus is the cleanest. Death has already written down where you'll be tomorrow, he's never wrong, and fleeing to Aleppo costs a dollar. CDT can't settle: if it expects to stay, Death is in Damascus and fleeing looks better; if it expects to flee, the reverse. Joyce's fix is to let your credence drift until the two options balance, and in the asymmetric version that happens at 50.05% stay - the same 0.5005 as Newcomb's break-even, which is just the same $1,000-against-$1 arithmetic turning up again. Then you act on that weighted coin, and Death is there, because he predicted the coin too. CDT expects $499.50 and gets minus fifty cents. Every time it's true that *had I gone the other way I'd have lived*, and it never helps once.

Death on Olympus is worse for it. Add a third option - an expensive climb up a mountain where dying gets you a conversation with the gods - which is worse than both cities in every world CDT can imagine, and also the only option where Death's being there is good news. Causal dominance rules it out. Every causal decision theorist dies in a city with nothing; the functional one climbs.

So [Two Boxes](/two-boxes#damascus) now has a third room, **Case files**: ten problems on the same engine, each with its causal graph, the three verdicts, what each theory *expects* against what agents who reason that way *walk away with*, and for the unstable ones an animation of CDT deliberating its way to the coin. The gap between those two numbers is the whole argument, laid out case by case.

It isn't only a case for FDT. The paper is honest that FDT needs a theory of when two algorithms are "the same" closely enough to move together, and nobody has one. The [twin prisoner's dilemma](/two-boxes#twin) has that as a dial: with a true copy, cooperating pays above 75% likeness, and if you make the likeness a shared upbringing instead, EDT keeps cooperating on the correlation while FDT defects. That's where the real philosophy is hiding, and it's the same question as in Newcomb - is the predictor running *you*, or something that merely travels with you?

And the critics' cases are in there with the same treatment. [Counterfactual mugging](/two-boxes#mugging): the coin came up tails, Omega asks for $100 on the strength of what it would have paid you on heads, and FDT pays into a world where it buys nothing. [The Bomb](/two-boxes#bomb), which is MacAskill's: the predictor is wrong one time in a trillion trillion, you can see the live bomb in Left, and FDT takes Left anyway because that's the policy that keeps Left empty for nearly everyone. On the scorecard FDT averages best there too. That's exactly the problem - the number is about everyone, and the person in the room is the one standing in front of the bomb.

My take, for what it's worth after a day of this: the dilemma cases are FDT's strongest ground and Newcomb its weakest, which is backwards from how the argument usually goes. Against CDT, Damascus and Olympus are close to decisive - a theory that predictably dies on principle while calling the escape route dominated has lost track of what deciding is for. Against FDT, the Bomb lands, but it lands as a bill rather than a refutation: being the kind of agent predictors can trust costs you in the rare world where the trust was misplaced, the same way keeping a promise does after it stops paying. Parfit's hitchhiker, stranded in the desert in front of a driver who can read faces, is the case that makes the price look worth it, and it's in the files too. I'd pay the driver. I'm less sure I'd take Left, and I think that uncertainty is the honest place to stand.
