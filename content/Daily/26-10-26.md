October's internet fad was single-neuron steering: people found that pushing *one* neuron in [Qwen3.5-4B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen) gives the model a one-track mind. One makes it talk about cookies, one makes it SHOUT, one pair makes it evil. Then [Goodfire](https://already-clean.goodfire.com/) published *Neural Networks Are Already Clean* (Cammarata, 15 October), arguing that most neurons are legible if you read them properly - about 90% of neuron "poles" in Qwen3-32B with one main job.

I wanted to see for myself, cheaply. So now there's a microscope, and it's public: **[neurons.subsurfaces.net](https://neurons.subsurfaces.net)**.

![[daily-neuron-atlas.png]]

Every one of the model's 294,912 neurons (32 layers × 9,216) gets a card: what it writes, where it fires hardest, at both poles, and whether it behaves like a dial or a detector. The whole atlas came from one pass over about a million tokens - 870 seconds on one rented GPU, about 20 cents. The site is static: nothing runs a GPU when you visit.

Then an evening of small pre-registered studies (hypotheses and refutation thresholds written down *before* each run - otherwise you find whatever you were hoping for):

- **The SHOUT neuron isn't a shouting detector.** Its strongest pole fires on CamelCase, capitals in the middle of code identifiers. Pushing it makes the model shout, but it doesn't read shouting. Readers are not controllers.
- **"Clean" held at 58%, not 90%** - on a random sample of 150 neurons rated blind, then checked by a second, machine rater (κ = 0.79; my own blind re-rating is still owed). Most of the mess is in weak poles, and the middle layers are murkiest.
- **Read ≠ write.** Picking neurons by what they *read* steers no better than random; picking by what they *write* does.
- **Neuron order is a fingerprint.** The low-numbered neurons in every layer matter far more: knock out the first 512 per layer and the model loses 1.98 nats; a random 512, 0.13. Something about how the model was made is written in its index numbers.
- **It has a sense of the history of philosophy.** Run 8,015 paragraphs from 54 philosophers through it, and authors from the same school are nearest neighbours 74% of the time (chance is 13.5%); profile distance tracks birth-year gap. The top neuron for Georg Trakl fires on *"glistened in the sunlight"*.

Total compute for the day, about three dollars.

The atlas is half of a program run with Claude (it designed and ran most of the studies; I picked the questions). The [Thinkers](https://neurons.subsurfaces.net/#/thinkers) page is my favourite: a map of philosophers as a 4-billion-parameter model sees them.
