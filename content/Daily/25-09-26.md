You can't do astronomy from a desk in Wales by collecting more light. But the big surveys publish nearly everything, and what's scarce now is ideas and careful analysis. **Celestrium** is my instrument for that side of it: read the literature, turn a theory into "here is what you'd see in the data", pull a *small* slice from a public archive (Gaia, Euclid, WISE, DESI and friends), and test the claim. Every query is logged, so a result can be traced back to exactly what was asked.

Today it got a public face at [astro.subsurfaces.net](https://astro.subsurfaces.net), and the day's experiment was a triage of **fast radio bursts** - millisecond flashes from other galaxies, catalogued in their hundreds by the Canadian [CHIME](https://www.chime-frb.ca/) telescope - asking which ones are odd enough to deserve a second look.

The more useful event was a pull request. A friend, mrcal17, went through the code and came back with a list: a sign error in a dipole fit, an "oracle leak" where a model could see the answer it was meant to predict, GPS time handled wrongly, tests that leaned on things outside themselves. All fixed today, all the kind of thing that produces a beautiful, wrong figure.

So the site says, near the top, that these are internal work products and not peer-reviewed findings. Three weeks later I went further: reorganised everything into four programmes, audited every experiment, and stripped the website from a grand interactive platform down to one plain page of results with their figures. It's a better site for it.

One of those audited experiments turned up something that might be real: [[14-10-26|a nova nobody saw]].
