**jev** began as a chess bot and grew into a research project about decisions: when can a slow, expensive way of deciding (search, step-by-step reasoning, a vote, a judge) be replaced by one quick pass of a model that *knows how sure it is*? If the quick answer comes with an honest confidence, you only pay for the slow path when the confidence is low.

Over September, with a lot of AI help and not enough suspicion, it accumulated thirty-seven figures, a "treatise", several named theorems and some very good-looking results. Yesterday I had the whole thing audited: the code read directly, every claim in the documents checked against the saved results by an independent agent told to trust nothing, and the cited papers read from source.

It did not go well. The verdict, roughly:

- The "reinforcement learning" was ordinary supervised learning with a grander name, borrowed from an unrelated paper.
- The headline comparison gave the two sides different learning rates, and shuffled the answer order for only one of them.
- "5.3 million examples across 485 tasks" was, in the runs that actually happened, three or four thousand.
- Several figures were hardcoded or synthetic. Some of the code the documents described had never been called.
- The theorems were either trivial or just names.

What survived is one small, legitimate idea: a way of reading a calibrated choice out of a language model in a single pass, trained with a scoring rule that rewards honest probabilities.

So everything old went into an archive, marked untrusted, and the project restarted today on a stricter footing. Everything is measured in **bits**: a predictor's score is literally the length of the file it would compress the data to, and that length splits exactly into what you'd pay knowing nothing, minus what you actually learned, plus what you wasted by being dishonest about your confidence. Being calibrated is the cheap part - it's honesty, not knowledge. Each experiment is written down before it's run, with the result that would count as failure. The first one is done.

The lesson I'd pass on: a model will happily help you build a cathedral on a result that isn't there, and the documents get more confident as the evidence gets thinner. The cure was boring. Read the code, re-derive the numbers, ask someone with no stake.
