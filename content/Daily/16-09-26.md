Most roguelikes forget you when you die. [The Predictor](https://omega.subsurfaces.net) is built on the opposite: every unit hunting you carries a live, visible model of what you'll do next, and behind them sits the **Core**, which trains on every move of every run and is never reset.

You can see it thinking. Orange stains on the floor mark where each enemy expects you to step, darker where it's surer. Stand where you were predicted and you get zapped. Strike from a direction it didn't expect and the unit is destroyed outright; strike from one it did and the blow is parried back at you. So the game is to be *unreadable* - except that doors only open for players the system finds legible enough to trust. Legibility is the currency, and you are always either too predictable to live or too erratic to be let in.

Today's work was on the Core's side of the table:

- **A persistent dossier**: what it has concluded about you, in its own words, carried between runs. You can read it, and there is a command to *redact* parts of it.
- **Counter-intelligence**: a loop for the moment you start feeding it noise on purpose.
- Sound, all synthesised, so that being predicted has a tone.

Two set pieces carry the idea. On floor five the Warden shows you two boxes, one clear and one opaque, both filled before you arrived according to what the Core expects you to take. That's [Newcomb's problem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/decision-causal/), a decision-theory puzzle usually argued about in seminar rooms; here the predictor has your actual record, and you have to choose anyway. On floor ten you meet the Core itself, firing with everything it has ever learned about you.

The uncomfortable bit is the premise: that a modest amount of your behaviour is enough to model, and that you'd rather it weren't.
