**Enclosure** is a two-player game I've been chewing on: you draw straight edges on a 19×19 lattice, close off area, and cut your opponent's edges. Your score is the area you hold, *integrated over time* - so holding a small field early can beat grabbing a big one late.

![[daily-enclosure-seal.png]]
*The native viewer, mid-game: dark wall on the left, a white enclosure being built on the right. The faint tiles are "sealed" space - points the enemy can no longer reach.*

The nice surprise of this session was how much of the game turned out to be classical maths in disguise:

- **Territory is unreachability.** A point is yours long before it's enclosed, as soon as nothing can reach it. Sealing leads area by about thirty moves in my games.
- **Planning territory is a minimum cut** (max-flow, the same thing that routes water through pipes). Run it on an empty board and it rediscovers the full-height wall I'd found by hand.
- **Cashing out is scheduling.** Take a small gain only if its rate beats what's still pending - [Smith's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_job_next) from operations research. That one change took a neural bot from losing 85% of games to losing all of them.
- There's a "price of life" constant, a cost of keeping a wall alive, that the evidence puts at exactly **15/64**. Not proved yet (the LP relaxation isn't tight, so it needs integrality).

The planner, **ShieldBot**, beats every scripted bot 96–100% of the time, and a 365k-parameter net trained on its games ties it in a single forward pass. The theory became the *teacher* rather than an input, which is a small, pleasing instance of the bitter lesson.

I still beat all of them, for now (a game file is literally named `i-figured-it-out`). Then I wired the bots up to play on [meaf.us](https://meaf.us) and lost my first game to someone else's bot, 1936 to 5584. Humbling.

Engine in Python with exact rational areas, a Rust core, a native board viewer. Not public yet; the rules are the next thing worth writing up.
